## Notation introduced

- D(a, r), D̄(a, r): open and closed discs; C(a, r): the circle |z − a| = r traversed once counterclockwise, ζ = a + re^{iθ}, θ ∈ [0, 2π]. ∮ is used only for closed curves.
- T: a closed (solid) triangle, non-degenerate or degenerate; ∂T its boundary traversed counterclockwise as the three segments; [z, w] the segment from z to w as a curve.
- "Locally uniformly on Ω" = uniformly on every compact subset of Ω (`def-locally-uniform`).

## Chapter hook and where it is paid

**Opening question (index.md):** *Find the radius of convergence of the Taylor series of x/(eˣ − 1) at 0 without computing a single coefficient.* On ℝ the function is smooth (with value 1 at 0) and positive everywhere, nothing visible goes wrong at any real x, and real analysis cannot answer: e^{−1/x²} is C^∞ with Taylor series 0, and 1/(1 + x²) (the CA.1 hook, paid there) has a series that dies at |x| = 1 for reasons invisible on ℝ. The chapter's thesis: a function that is complex-differentiable once is analytic, and its Taylor series converges on the largest disc the function lives on. **Paid** in §3 by `cor-taylor-largest-disc` and `exm-bernoulli-radius` (answer: 2π, the distance to the nearest non-real zero 2πi of e^z − 1).

## Named moves (index.md)

1. **Subdivide and nest** (§1, new; Scratch Work): split a triangle into four, keep the worst piece, intersect the nested compacta, and beat the quantity with a local estimate at the limit point.
2. **Primitive by integrating from a base point** (§2, new in this form; Scratch Work): define F(z) = ∫_{[c, z]} f and differentiate using a vanishing triangle integral.
3. **Subtract the value, divide by the distance** (§3): g(ζ) = (f(ζ) − f(z))/(ζ − z) turns a statement about f(z) into a Cauchy theorem for g.
4. **Expand the kernel** (§3, new; Scratch Work; the chapter's faded triple): 1/(ζ − z) = Σ (z − a)ⁿ/(ζ − a)^{n+1}, uniformly for ζ on the circle; integrate termwise.
5. **Estimate on a circle, then move the radius** (§4, new; Scratch Work): a bound that holds for every r is sent to r → ∞ (or optimized over r).
6. **Open, closed, non-empty** (§6; first appearance in this volume; Scratch Work): the set where something happens is shown clopen in a region.

## Sections

### 01-goursat.md — Goursat's theorem (budget 3,500 words)

**Opening frame.** In CA.1, a closed-curve integral of f vanished when f had a primitive (`thm-primitive-closed-curve`), and ∮_{C(0,1)} dz/z = 2πi showed that not every holomorphic function has one. Which closed curves and which domains force ∮ f = 0? Green's theorem gives a two-line answer if f is C¹: ∮_{∂T} f dz = 2i ∬_T ∂f/∂z̄ dA = 0 by Cauchy–Riemann. **Failure that motivates the section:** the definition of holomorphic (CA.1) asks only that f′ exist; continuity of f′ is a conclusion of this chapter (§3), so assuming it here would be circular. The section proves the triangle case with no hypothesis on f′. Remark (one paragraph, no proof, optional): for readers who know Green's theorem, the Pólya vector field of f reads Re ∮ f dz as work and Im ∮ f dz as flux (Needham §11.2), and Cauchy–Riemann says the field is curl-free and divergence-free.

**Definitions.**

- `def-triangle-boundary`: closed triangle T = conv{z₁, z₂, z₃}; ∂T = [z₁, z₂] + [z₂, z₃] + [z₃, z₁] with the vertices ordered counterclockwise (degenerate T allowed: the integral is then 0 by cancellation, checked in a Quick check). diam T, perimeter p(T).
- `lem-midpoint-subdivision`: joining the midpoints splits T into four triangles T⁽¹⁾…T⁽⁴⁾, each with diam = ½ diam T and p = ½ p(T), and ∮_{∂T} f = Σ_j ∮_{∂T⁽ʲ⁾} f for continuous f (interior edges cancel). Proof sketch: similar triangles with ratio ½; the three interior segments are traversed once in each direction.

**Main statements.**

- `thm-goursat` [Goursat's Theorem]. Let Ω ⊆ ℂ be open, p ∈ Ω, f: Ω → ℂ continuous on Ω and holomorphic on Ω ∖ {p}. Then ∮_{∂T} f(z) dz = 0 for every closed triangle T ⊆ Ω.
  - *Scratch Work* (separate block, before the proof; first appearance of "subdivide and nest"): first the case p ∉ T. Write η = |∮_{∂T} f|. Subdividing, one of the four pieces carries at least η/4; iterating gives Tₙ with |∮_{∂Tₙ} f| ≥ η/4ⁿ, diam Tₙ = 2^{−n} d, p(Tₙ) = 2^{−n} p. The Tₙ are nested non-empty compacta, so they meet at some z₀ ∈ T (AN2.3). Near z₀, f(z) = f(z₀) + f′(z₀)(z − z₀) + ψ(z)(z − z₀) with ψ(z) → 0. The linear part has a primitive, so its integral over ∂Tₙ is 0 (`thm-primitive-closed-curve`). The remainder is at most sup_{Tₙ}|ψ| · diam Tₙ · p(Tₙ) = εₙ 4^{−n} d p by the ML-inequality. So η ≤ εₙ d p → 0. The scratch work ends by noticing that the 4ⁿ from subdividing is exactly cancelled by diameter × perimeter: that is why triangles, not arbitrary curves.
  - Proof: Step 1 (p ∉ T) as above, written forward with the choice of the piece made explicit ("choose j with |∮_{∂T⁽ʲ⁾} f| maximal"). Step 2 (p a vertex of T): cut T into a small triangle at p of diameter < δ and two or three triangles not containing p; Step 1 kills the others; the small one is ≤ max_T |f| · p(small) → 0 using continuity (boundedness) of f on the compact T. Step 3 (p ∈ T otherwise): split T at p into triangles having p as a vertex. (Degenerate pieces contribute 0.)
  - Self-explanation prompt: *Before reading on: where exactly is complex differentiability at z₀ used, and why would differentiability in the real sense not suffice?* (Answer, in the next paragraph: the linear part must be α + βz, which has a primitive; a real-linear map α + βz + γz̄ does not.)
  - Aftermath: every triangle integral of a holomorphic function vanishes; §2 turns this into primitives. The exceptional point is what §3 needs.

**Weird example / counterexamples.**

- `exm-zbar-triangle`: ∮_{∂T} z̄ dz = 2i · Area(T) for every triangle (computed directly on one triangle with vertices 0, 1, i: value i; then in general by the Green computation in the remark, or by an affine change of variables in an exercise). z̄ is real-differentiable everywhere: the theorem needs complex differentiability. Enters the weird-examples table.
- Warning: the exceptional point needs continuity there. f(z) = 1/z (with any value at 0) is holomorphic on ℂ ∖ {0} but not continuous at 0, and its integral over the boundary of a square around 0 is 2πi (exercise C2(b), computed directly). The triangle version of that computation waits for CA.4.

**Quick checks.** (1) Why is ∮_{∂T} f = 0 for a degenerate (collinear) T and any continuous f? (2) In Step 1, why does some piece carry at least a quarter? (3) Which of the four properties diam, perimeter, area, number of pieces is not used in the estimate? (area).

**Exercises.**

- A1 (a) State Goursat's theorem with all hypotheses. (b) True/false: if f is real-differentiable on ℂ, ∮_{∂T} f = 0 for every T. (c) [proof comprehension, key proof 1] In Step 1 with T having vertices 0, 2, 2i, give diam T₃ and p(T₃). Answer: 2√2/8, (4 + 2√2)/8. (d) [comprehension] Which step fails if f is only continuous at p but p is an interior point and we skip Step 3? (e) Why does the nested intersection contain exactly one point?
- B1: compute ∮_{∂T} z̄ dz for T with vertices 0, 2, 1 + i directly from the three segments and compare with 2i · Area. Answer: 2i.
- B2: Let f(z) = |z|². Show ∮_{∂T} f ≠ 0 for T with vertices 0, 1, i, so f is not holomorphic on any open set containing T. Answer: ∮ = (i − 1)/3 (compute; confirm at S2a with sidecar).
- C1: (a) Prove Goursat for a closed rectangle R (Ahlfors' setting) directly by subdivision into four rectangles. (b) Deduce it from `thm-goursat` by cutting R along a diagonal.
- C2 (exceptional points): (a) Prove the conclusion of `thm-goursat` when f is continuous on Ω and holomorphic on Ω ∖ F for a finite set F. (b) (dropped hypothesis) Let f(z) = 1/z for z ≠ 0 and f(0) = 0. Compute ∮_{∂R} f for the square R with vertices ±1 ± i (answer 2πi: each side contributes iπ/2, e.g. ∫_{−1}^{1} i dt/(1 + it) = ∫_{−1}^{1} (t + i)/(1 + t²) dt = iπ/2) and say which hypothesis of C1(a) fails. *Hint ladder:* rung 1 "split T so that each piece has at most one point of F, at a vertex"; rung 2 "cut T at the points of F into triangles having them as vertices, as in Step 3".

### 02-cauchy-in-a-disc.md — Primitives and Cauchy's theorem in a disc (budget 3,500 words)

**Opening frame.** CA.1 showed: primitive ⇒ closed integrals vanish. The converse direction, built from triangles, is the content here.

- `thm-disc-primitive` [Primitives in a Disc]. Let D be an open disc (more generally an open convex set) with centre c, and f: D → ℂ continuous with ∮_{∂T} f = 0 for every closed triangle T ⊆ D. Then F(z) = ∫_{[c, z]} f(ζ) dζ is holomorphic on D with F′ = f.
  - *Scratch Work* (first appearance of "primitive by integrating from a base point"): F(z + h) − F(z) = ∫_{[z, z+h]} f, because the triangle c, z, z + h lies in D (convexity) and its integral vanishes; then ∫_{[z,z+h]} f − f(z)h = ∫_{[z,z+h]} (f(ζ) − f(z)) dζ is at most |h| · sup_{[z,z+h]} |f − f(z)| = o(|h|) by continuity.
  - Proof forward from the scratch work. Remark: convexity is used only through "the segment [c, z] and the triangle (c, z, z + h) lie in D"; star-shaped about c suffices (exercise C1).
- `thm-cauchy-disc` [Cauchy's Theorem in a Disc]. Let D be an open disc, p ∈ D, f: D → ℂ continuous and holomorphic on D ∖ {p}. Then f has a primitive on D, and ∮_γ f = 0 for every closed piecewise C¹ curve γ in D. Proof: `thm-goursat` gives the triangle hypothesis; `thm-disc-primitive` gives F; `thm-primitive-closed-curve` (CA.1) finishes. Corollary `cor-cauchy-convex`: the same on any open convex set (same proof).
- **Not here: the two-circles (annulus) lemma.** Apostol 16.4 proves ∮_{C(a,r)} f independent of r by differentiating in r under the integral sign, which needs f′ continuous, a conclusion of §3; so it would be circular here. §3 avoids it (`lem-circle-kernel` expands the kernel instead), and the annulus statement becomes CA.3's opening lemma. (S2a item 3.)

**Worked examples.**

- `exm-gaussian-shift` [Shifting a Gaussian Contour]. For ξ ∈ ℝ, ∫_ℝ e^{−x²/2} e^{−iξx} dx = √(2π) e^{−ξ²/2}. Integrate e^{−z²/2} over the rectangle with vertices ±R, ±R + iξ (`cor-cauchy-convex`, entire integrand); the vertical sides are ≤ |ξ| e^{−(R² − ξ²)/2} → 0; on the top side e^{−(x + iξ)²/2} = e^{ξ²/2} e^{−x²/2} e^{−iξx}; ∫_ℝ e^{−x²/2} = √(2π) is quoted, with a `see` pointer to wherever the series proves it (S2a item 10). Aftermath: the Gaussian is its own Fourier transform (pointer to HA, see only).
- `exm-power-integrals-circle`: ∮_{C(a,r)} (z − a)ⁿ dz = 0 for n ≠ −1, 2πi for n = −1 (n ≥ 0 by Cauchy; n ≤ −2 by a primitive on ℂ ∖ {a}; n = −1 direct, recalled from CA.1).

**Warning** `wrn-annulus`: 1/z is holomorphic on the annulus 1/2 < |z| < 2 and ∮_{C(0,1)} dz/z = 2πi ≠ 0. The theorem needs a disc (or convex set), not merely "f holomorphic near the curve". Which shapes are allowed is CA.4's question (hook planted).

**Quick checks.** (1) Where does the proof of `thm-disc-primitive` use that D is convex? (2) Is F in `thm-disc-primitive` unique? (up to a constant, by `thm-derivative-zero-constant`). (3) Does Cauchy in a disc apply to f(z) = 1/(z − 3) on D(0, 2)? On D(0, 4)?

**Exercises.**

### 03-cauchy-integral-formula.md — The Cauchy integral formula; holomorphic functions are analytic (budget 4,500 words)

**Opening frame.** The hook question is restated. Two steps: a formula recovering f inside a disc from its values on the boundary circle, then a power series read off from the formula.

- `lem-circle-kernel`. For |z − a| < r: ∮_{C(a,r)} dζ/(ζ − z) = 2πi. Proof: for ζ on C(a, r), 1/(ζ − z) = Σ_{n≥0} (z − a)ⁿ/(ζ − a)^{n+1}, and the terms are bounded by r^{−1}(|z − a|/r)ⁿ, so the M-test (AN1.9) gives uniform convergence on the circle; integrate termwise (AN1.8/AN1.9); `exm-power-integrals-circle` leaves only n = 0. This is the first use of **expand the kernel**: *Scratch Work* block shows where the geometric series comes from (write ζ − z = (ζ − a)(1 − (z − a)/(ζ − a)) and note |(z − a)/(ζ − a)| = |z − a|/r < 1 uniformly in ζ). No winding numbers.
- `thm-cauchy-formula-disc` [Cauchy's Integral Formula] (key proof 2). Let Ω be open, f holomorphic on Ω, and D̄(a, r) ⊆ Ω. Then for every z with |z − a| < r,
  f(z) = (1/2πi) ∮_{C(a,r)} f(ζ)/(ζ − z) dζ.
  - Idea: subtract the value, divide by the distance; the quotient is a function to which Cauchy's theorem applies on a slightly larger disc.
  - Proof: D̄(a, r) is compact and ℂ ∖ Ω closed and disjoint from it, so there is r′ > r with D(a, r′) ⊆ Ω (positive distance, AN2.3). Fix z; g(ζ) = (f(ζ) − f(z))/(ζ − z) for ζ ≠ z, g(z) = f′(z), is continuous on D(a, r′) and holomorphic off z. `thm-cauchy-disc` gives ∮_{C(a,r)} g = 0; expand and use `lem-circle-kernel`.
  - Self-explanation prompt: *Where is the closedness of the disc D̄(a, r) ⊆ Ω used, and what goes wrong for f(z) = 1/(1 − z) with a = 0, r = 1?*
  - Remark: the formula expresses an interior value as an average of boundary values against a kernel; CA.6 rewrites it as the Poisson integral (hook).
- `thm-holomorphic-analytic` [Holomorphic Functions Are Analytic]. f holomorphic on Ω, D(a, R) ⊆ Ω. Then f(z) = Σ cₙ (z − a)ⁿ for all z ∈ D(a, R), with cₙ = (1/2πi) ∮_{C(a,r)} f(ζ)/(ζ − a)^{n+1} dζ for any 0 < r < R (independent of r).
  - Proof (worked instance 1 of the faded triple, in full): fix z, pick r with |z − a| < r < R; Cauchy's formula on D̄(a, r); expand the kernel; |f| ≤ M_r on the circle gives the M-test bound M_r r^{−1}(|z − a|/r)ⁿ; termwise integration. Independence of r: by uniqueness of power series coefficients (CA.1 / AN1.10) the cₙ equal f⁽ⁿ⁾(a)/n!, which does not involve r.
  - Corollaries: `cor-holomorphic-infinitely-differentiable` (f′ is holomorphic; f ∈ C^∞ in the real sense; in particular f′ is continuous, the hypothesis Green's theorem wanted in §1, now a theorem); `cor-cauchy-formula-derivatives`: f⁽ⁿ⁾(z) = (n!/2πi) ∮_{C(a,r)} f(ζ)/(ζ − z)^{n+1} dζ for |z − a| < r. Route: differentiate the Cauchy integral in z through difference quotients; for ζ on the circle and z, z + h in a smaller disc, [1/(ζ − z − h) − 1/(ζ − z)]/h → 1/(ζ − z)² uniformly in ζ, so the limit passes under ∫ (AN1.9); no differentiation-under-the-integral theorem (AN3.4) is used. n = 1 in the text; induction is exercise C1.
  - `cor-taylor-largest-disc`: the Taylor series of f at a converges to f on the largest open disc centred at a contained in Ω; its radius of convergence is at least dist(a, ℂ ∖ Ω).
- `exm-bernoulli-radius` [Paying the Hook]. h(z) = (e^z − 1)/z = Σ zⁿ/(n + 1)! is entire with h(0) = 1 and zeros exactly at 2πik, k ≠ 0 (CA.1: e^z = 1 ⇔ z ∈ 2πiℤ). So z/(e^z − 1) := 1/h(z) is holomorphic on D(0, 2π) and its Taylor series at 0 has radius ≥ 2π. Radius ≤ 2π: if it were larger, the series would define a function continuous at 2πi agreeing with 1/h on D(0, 2π), but |1/h(z)| → ∞ as z → 2πi inside the disc. Answer 2π. Aftermath: the coefficients are B_n/n! (Bernoulli numbers; named, not developed), so B_n/n! decays like (2π)^{−n}: a statement about real numbers proved in ℂ.

**Weird examples (table entries).** e^{−1/x²} (C^∞ on ℝ, Taylor series 0, not analytic) versus `cor-holomorphic-infinitely-differentiable`; f(x) = x|x| (once differentiable on ℝ, not twice) versus "once complex differentiable ⇒ infinitely".

### 04-cauchy-estimates-liouville.md — Cauchy estimates, Liouville, and the fundamental theorem of algebra (budget 3,500 words)

**Opening frame.** The coefficient formula of §3, estimated crudely, bounds every derivative by the maximum on one circle.

- `thm-cauchy-estimates` [Cauchy's Inequalities]. f holomorphic on an open set containing D̄(a, r), M_r = max_{|ζ − a| = r} |f(ζ)|. Then |f⁽ⁿ⁾(a)| ≤ n! M_r / rⁿ for all n ≥ 0. Proof: ML-inequality on `cor-cauchy-formula-derivatives` at z = a.
  - *Scratch Work* (first appearance of "estimate on a circle, then move the radius"): with n = 1 and f bounded by M on all of ℂ, the bound |f′(a)| ≤ M/r holds for **every** r > 0; nothing depends on r but the right side; send r → ∞.
- `thm-liouville` [Liouville's Theorem]. A bounded entire function is constant.
  - *Try it first (ten minutes): use the Cauchy inequality with n = 1 to show f′(a) = 0 for every a.*
  - Idea starts from the obstacle a first attempt meets: the bound M/r looks like a fixed positive number until one notices r is free.
  - Proof: `thm-cauchy-estimates` with n = 1, r → ∞; then `thm-derivative-zero-constant` on the connected set ℂ.
  - Remark: the proof uses only the Cauchy formula and the ML-inequality, so it works verbatim for functions with values in a Banach space once integration of such functions is available (FA.8 uses this for Gelfand–Mazur; pointer only).
- `cor-polynomial-growth`: entire f with |f(z)| ≤ A + B|z|ⁿ for all z is a polynomial of degree ≤ n (Cauchy estimates for f⁽ⁿ⁺¹⁾ at every point a, using a circle C(a, r), r → ∞). Exit check C1 asks for the proof in a slightly different form (bound only for |z| ≥ R₀).
- `thm-fta` [Fundamental Theorem of Algebra] (revisits F5.5). Every non-constant polynomial p with complex coefficients has a zero in ℂ. Proof: if not, 1/p is entire; |p(z)| ≥ |a_n||z|ⁿ/2 for |z| ≥ R₀ gives boundedness for |z| ≥ R₀, continuity on the compact D̄(0, R₀) gives it inside; Liouville makes 1/p constant. Remark (revisits F5.5): F5.5 proved this from a minimum of |p| on a disc and a local d'Alembert step; the Liouville proof hides the same compactness in the boundedness of 1/p and replaces the local step by Cauchy's formula. Remark: factorization into linear factors follows by polynomial division and induction (see F5.5).

**Worked examples.**

- `exm-re-bounded-entire`: entire f with Re f ≤ C is constant: e^{f} is entire with |e^{f}| = e^{Re f} ≤ e^{C}; Liouville gives e^{f} constant, so f′e^{f} = 0, so f′ = 0.
- `exm-sin-unbounded`: sin is bounded on ℝ and entire, so it is unbounded on ℂ: |sin(iy)| = |sinh y|. Real analogue that Liouville forbids in ℂ: x ↦ 1/(1 + x²) is real-analytic and bounded on ℝ; its "entire extension" would have to be bounded too, and indeed it has poles at ±i. Both enter the weird-examples table.

### 05-morera-and-limits.md — Morera's theorem, locally uniform limits, and functions defined by integrals (budget 4,500 words)

**Opening frame.** §2 went from "holomorphic" to "triangle integrals vanish"; §3 showed that anything with a primitive is holomorphic twice over. Combining gives a converse that needs no derivative at all.

- `thm-morera` [Morera's Theorem]. Let Ω be open and f: Ω → ℂ continuous with ∮_{∂T} f = 0 for every closed triangle T ⊆ Ω. Then f is holomorphic on Ω. Proof: on each disc D ⊆ Ω, `thm-disc-primitive` gives F holomorphic with F′ = f; by `cor-holomorphic-infinitely-differentiable` F′ = f is holomorphic on D. (The named move "localize, then use a disc" is pointed out in prose, no Scratch Work: it is short.)
- **Failure-motivated definition.** The geometric series Σ zⁿ converges to 1/(1 − z) on D(0, 1), and the sum is holomorphic, but the convergence is not uniform on D(0, 1) (sup |s_N(z) − 1/(1 − z)| = ∞ for every N). Uniform convergence on Ω is too strong to be the right hypothesis; uniform convergence on each smaller closed disc holds. Hence:
- `def-locally-uniform`: fₙ → f locally uniformly on Ω if fₙ → f uniformly on every compact K ⊆ Ω. With quantifiers in symbols (STYLE §10): ∀K ⊆ Ω compact ∀ε > 0 ∃N ∀n ≥ N ∀z ∈ K |fₙ(z) − f(z)| < ε. Quick check: swap "∀K" and "∃N" — what does the swapped statement say for Σ zⁿ on D(0, 1)? `lem-locally-uniform-discs`: equivalently, uniform on every closed disc contained in Ω (a compact K is covered by finitely many such discs, AN2.3).
- `thm-weierstrass-limits` [Weierstrass's Theorem on Holomorphic Limits]. Let fₙ be holomorphic on Ω and fₙ → f locally uniformly on Ω. Then f is holomorphic on Ω and fₙ⁽ᵏ⁾ → f⁽ᵏ⁾ locally uniformly on Ω for every k ≥ 1.
  - Proof: f is continuous (uniform limits, AN1.9); for T ⊆ Ω, ∮_{∂T} fₙ → ∮_{∂T} f by the ML-inequality (∂T compact), and each ∮_{∂T} fₙ = 0 (`thm-goursat`); Morera. Derivatives: take D̄(a, 2r) ⊆ Ω; Cauchy estimates applied to fₙ − f on circles of radius r centred at points of D̄(a, r) give sup_{D̄(a,r)} |fₙ⁽ᵏ⁾ − f⁽ᵏ⁾| ≤ k! r^{−k} sup_{D̄(a,2r)} |fₙ − f|; finish with `lem-locally-uniform-discs`.
  - Warning `wrn-real-limits` (weird examples, two entries): on ℝ the analogue fails twice. Polynomials converge uniformly on [−1, 1] to |x| (Weierstrass approximation, AN2.6, see), so uniform limits of real-analytic functions need not be differentiable; and sin(n²x)/n → 0 uniformly on ℝ while the derivatives n cos(n²x) equal n at x = 0 and diverge there.
  - Remark: the inequality in the proof (derivatives controlled by sup norms on a larger set) is the seed of Montel's theorem (CA.5; hook).
- `cor-series-holomorphic`: if Σ fₙ converges locally uniformly on Ω (e.g. by the M-test on each closed disc) with fₙ holomorphic, the sum is holomorphic and may be differentiated termwise.
- `thm-holomorphic-param-integral` [Holomorphic Dependence on a Parameter]. Let Ω be open, φ: Ω × [α, β] → ℂ continuous, and φ(·, t) holomorphic on Ω for each t. Then F(z) = ∫_α^β φ(z, t) dt is holomorphic on Ω. If moreover ∂_z φ is continuous on Ω × [α, β], then F′(z) = ∫_α^β ∂_z φ(z, t) dt. Proof (Riemann-sum route, avoiding Fubini and differentiation under the integral sign): the Riemann sums Fₙ(z) = Σ_j φ(z, t_j) Δt_j for the uniform partition are holomorphic; on a closed disc K ⊆ Ω, φ is uniformly continuous on the compact K × [α, β] (AN2.3), so Fₙ → F uniformly on K; `thm-weierstrass-limits` gives F holomorphic and Fₙ′ → F′; Fₙ′ are the Riemann sums of ∂_z φ, which converge to ∫ ∂_z φ by the same uniform-continuity argument. Remark: the real-variable theorem on differentiation under the integral sign (AN3.4) is not needed; the measure-theoretic version with a dominating function is in MT.6 (see).

**Worked examples.**

- `exm-zeta-holomorphic`: ζ(z) = Σ_{n≥1} n^{−z} (n^{−z} := e^{−z log n}) is holomorphic on Re z > 1, with ζ′(z) = −Σ (log n) n^{−z}: M-test on the closed half-plane Re z ≥ σ > 1, |n^{−z}| = n^{−Re z} ≤ n^{−σ}, every compact subset of {Re z > 1} lies in such a half-plane. Hook → CA.13.
- `exm-gamma-holomorphic`: Γ(z) = ∫₀^∞ t^{z−1} e^{−t} dt is holomorphic on Re z > 0: G_k(z) = ∫_{1/k}^{k} t^{z−1} e^{−t} dt are holomorphic by `thm-holomorphic-param-integral`, and on a ≤ Re z ≤ b (0 < a) the tails are bounded by ∫₀^{1/k} t^{a−1} dt + ∫_k^∞ t^{b−1} e^{−t} dt → 0 uniformly. Spiral: AN2.11's real Γ is the restriction (see AN2.11). Hook → CA.13 and §6.

### 06-zeros-identity-maximum.md — Zeros, the identity theorem, and the maximum modulus principle (budget 4,500 words)

**Opening frame.** A power series vanishing on a sequence tending to its centre vanishes identically (CA.1/AN1.10). With §3 every holomorphic function is locally a power series; connectedness spreads "locally" to "everywhere".

- `def-region` (if not already in CA.1): a non-empty connected open subset of ℂ.
- `thm-zeros-isolated` [Zeros Have Finite Order]. Let f be holomorphic on a region Ω, not identically zero, and f(a) = 0. Then there are a unique m ≥ 1 and a holomorphic g on Ω with g(a) ≠ 0 and f(z) = (z − a)^m g(z) on Ω; in particular a has a punctured neighbourhood free of zeros of f. (`def-zero-order`: ord_a f := m.) Proof: by `cor-identity-power-series` not all f⁽ⁿ⁾(a) vanish; let m be the least n with f⁽ⁿ⁾(a) ≠ 0; Taylor series on a disc; define g by the quotient off a and by the series near a; g(a) = f⁽ᵐ⁾(a)/m! ≠ 0 and continuity.
  - Order in the section: `thm-identity` comes **before** `thm-zeros-isolated`, because the clopen argument is about the set where all derivatives vanish, and finite order needs it.
- `thm-identity` [Identity Theorem] (key proof 3). Let f, g be holomorphic on a region Ω. If {z ∈ Ω : f(z) = g(z)} has a limit point in Ω, then f = g on Ω.
  - *Scratch Work* (first appearance in this volume of "open, closed, non-empty"): with h = f − g, the natural set {h = 0} is closed but not open. The set that is both is A = {z ∈ Ω : h⁽ⁿ⁾(z) = 0 for all n ≥ 0}: closed as an intersection of zero sets of continuous functions (`cor-holomorphic-infinitely-differentiable`), open because h is its Taylor series on a disc around each point of A (`thm-holomorphic-analytic`). The limit point lands in A by the power-series fact.
  - Proof: Step 1, A is closed in Ω; Step 2, A is open; Step 3, the limit point z₀ ∈ A: on a disc D(z₀, r) ⊆ Ω, h = Σ cₙ(z − z₀)ⁿ; if not all cₙ = 0, the least m with c_m ≠ 0 gives h(z) = (z − z₀)^m k(z) with k(z₀) ≠ 0, so h has no zeros in a punctured disc, contradicting the limit point; Step 4, Ω connected and A ≠ ∅ give A = Ω (AN2.3).
  - Self-explanation prompt: *Self-explain why the set {h = 0} itself would not do in Step 2.*
- `cor-identity-power-series`: f holomorphic on a region and f⁽ⁿ⁾(a) = 0 for all n at one point ⇒ f ≡ 0.

**Worked examples.**

- `exm-permanence`: sin² z + cos² z = 1 on ℂ, because both sides are entire and agree on ℝ. And Γ(z + 1) = zΓ(z) on Re z > 0, because both sides are holomorphic there (`exm-gamma-holomorphic`) and agree on (0, ∞) (AN2.11, see).
- `exm-sin-one-over-z` (weird-examples entry): sin(1/z) is holomorphic on the region ℂ ∖ {0}, vanishes at 1/(kπ) → 0, and is not identically zero; no contradiction, because the limit point 0 is not in the region. And on ℝ, a C^∞ bump function vanishes on an interval without vanishing identically: real smoothness has no identity theorem.
- Warning: connectedness matters: on Ω = D(0, 1) ∪ D(3, 1), f = 0 on one disc and 1 on the other.

**Mean value and maximum modulus.**

- `thm-mean-value-property` [Mean Value Property]. f holomorphic on an open set containing D̄(a, r) ⇒ f(a) = (1/2π) ∫₀^{2π} f(a + re^{iθ}) dθ. Proof: Cauchy's formula at z = a, parametrized. Hook → CA.6.
- `thm-max-modulus` [Maximum Modulus Principle]. Let f be holomorphic on a region Ω. If |f| has a local maximum at a ∈ Ω, then f is constant on Ω.
  - *Try it first (ten minutes): average |f| over a small circle around a using the mean value property; what does |f(a)| ≥ |f| on the circle force?*
  - Idea: the obstacle is that the mean value property averages f, not |f|; the triangle inequality bridges it, and equality in the triangle inequality for integrals of a continuous non-negative function forces |f| = |f(a)| on each small circle.
  - Proof: multiply f by a unimodular constant so that f(a) ≥ 0. For 0 < r < ρ (with |f| ≤ f(a) on D(a, ρ)), the mean value property gives f(a) = (1/2π) ∫ Re f(a + re^{iθ}) dθ ≤ (1/2π) ∫ |f(a + re^{iθ})| dθ ≤ f(a). Both integrands are continuous and the inequalities Re f ≤ |f| ≤ f(a) hold pointwise, so equality holds pointwise: Re f = |f| = f(a) on each circle, hence Im f = 0 and f = f(a) on D(a, ρ). The identity theorem extends this to Ω. (No appeal to Cauchy–Riemann or to a constant-modulus exercise.)
- `cor-max-boundary`: Ω a **bounded** region, f continuous on Ω̄ and holomorphic on Ω ⇒ max_{Ω̄} |f| is attained on ∂Ω (compactness of Ω̄; if attained inside, f constant and the max is also on ∂Ω, which is non-empty since Ω ≠ ℂ is bounded).
- `cor-min-modulus`: same hypotheses and f has no zeros in Ω ⇒ min_{Ω̄} |f| is attained on ∂Ω (apply to 1/f).
- Weird example `exm-half-plane-exp`: on the unbounded region Re z > 0, |e^z| = 1 on the boundary iℝ but e^z is unbounded inside. Boundedness of Ω in `cor-max-boundary` cannot be dropped. Hook → Phragmén–Lindelöf (exercise in CA.5).
- Remark: the open mapping theorem (Apostol 16.18, Ahlfors 4.3.3) gives the maximum principle at once; it waits for CA.4, where counting zeros proves it.

**Quick checks.** (1) Does the identity theorem apply to f(z) = 0 and g(z) = z sin(1/z) on ℂ ∖ {0}? (No: the common zeros accumulate only at 0 ∉ Ω.) (2) Find max_{|z| ≤ 1} |z² − 2| and where it is attained. (3, at ±i.) (3) Is |cos z| ≤ 1 on ℂ? (No: cos(iy) = cosh y.)

**Exercises.**

- B1: Is there a function f holomorphic on D(0, 1) with f(1/n) = 1/(n + 1) for all n ≥ 2? Determine all such f. Answer: f(z) = z/(1 + z) is the only one.

Marking scheme (10): 3 max modulus gives |f| ≤ 1 inside; 3 min modulus (no zeros) gives |f| ≥ 1 inside; 2 hence |f| ≡ 1, a local maximum at an interior point; 2 constant by `thm-max-modulus` (or constant modulus ⇒ constant).
