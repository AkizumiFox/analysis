# Blueprint: CA.3 — Laurent Series, Singularities, and Residues

**Directory:** `ca/src/ch04-laurent-singularities-residues/` (CA chapter 4 in the spec/08 §D1 order; was `src/ch57-singularities-residues/`, old blueprint `ca/authoring/blueprints/ch03.md`). State: S1 done, awaiting S2.

**Volume.** CA *Complex Analysis*, the last undergraduate volume (T1, main line). Apostol, *Mathematical Analysis* 2e, Chapter 16 is the entry. The volume then extends toward Ahlfors, Conway and Stein–Shakarchi, with Needham for pictures. Order: CA.1, CA.10, CA.2, **CA.3**, CA.4, CA.6, CA.5.

**Purpose.**
- CA.2 showed that a function holomorphic on a disc is controlled by one integral formula. This chapter asks what happens at a point where the function is **not** holomorphic, but is holomorphic on every punctured disc around it.
- The answer has three parts:
  - the Laurent expansion, a power series that is allowed negative powers;
  - a classification of isolated singularities into three kinds: removable, pole and essential;
  - the residue, the single Laurent coefficient that survives a contour integral.
- The payoff is the residue theorem. It turns real integrals and sums that real-variable methods cannot do in closed form into finite algebra.
- **Scope cut.** This chapter proves the residue theorem for circles and for toy contours only. A toy contour is a closed curve cut into pieces that each lie in an open convex set; it is defined in §1, since CA.2 proves Cauchy's theorem only on discs and convex sets. The winding-number (homology) version is CA.4 (`thm-residue-general`). The argument principle, Rouché and the counting of zeros are CA.4 as well; they are Apostol 16.23.

## 01-laurent-series.md — Laurent series on an annulus

- **Hook: a Taylor series that cannot exist.**
  - f(z) = 1/(z(1 − z)) is holomorphic on 0 < |z| < 1, but on no disc around 0. CA.2's Taylor expansion has nothing to say.
  - Partial fractions give f = 1/z + Σ_{n≥0} zⁿ, so allowing one negative power repairs it.
  - Question: does every function holomorphic on a punctured disc, or more generally on an annulus, have such an expansion?
- **Failure-motivated definition** (`def-laurent-series`, `def-laurent-convergence`).
  - A Laurent series about a is a two-sided series Σ_{n∈ℤ} cₙ(z − a)ⁿ. It *converges* at z when both Σ_{n≥0} cₙ(z − a)ⁿ and Σ_{n≥1} c₋ₙ(z − a)^{−n} converge. The sum is the sum of the two.
  - Warning: convergence of the symmetric partial sums Σ_{|n|≤N} is not the definition. Under the symmetric sums, Σ_{n∈ℤ} n is "0".
- `def-annulus`: A(a; r, R), with r = 0 (punctured disc) and R = ∞ allowed.
- **`lem-laurent-series-converges`.**
  - Let R₂ = 1/limsup |cₙ|^{1/n} (n → +∞) and r₁ = limsup |c₋ₙ|^{1/n}.
  - If r₁ < R₂, the series converges absolutely on A(a; r₁, R₂), uniformly on each closed sub-annulus r₁ < ρ₁ ≤ |z − a| ≤ ρ₂ < R₂. Its sum is holomorphic there.
  - It diverges when |z − a| < r₁ or |z − a| > R₂.
  - *Proof:* the regular part is a power series in z − a (AN1.10, `thm-power-series-holomorphic`). The singular part is a power series in u = 1/(z − a) with radius 1/r₁, composed with the holomorphic map z ↦ 1/(z − a).
- **`lem-annulus-circles`.**
  - Statement: if g is holomorphic on A(a; r, R) and r < ρ₁ < ρ₂ < R, then ∮_{C(a;ρ₁)} g = ∮_{C(a;ρ₂)} g.
  - *Proof route:* this is the first toy contour (below).
    - Cut the closed region between the circles into m annular sectors of angle 2π/m, with m chosen so large that ρ₁ cos(π/m) > r.
    - The convex hull of each closed sector then lies in the annulus. It is compact, so some open convex neighbourhood of it still lies in the annulus, and `cor-cauchy-convex` gives ∮_{∂(sector)} g = 0.
    - Sum over the sectors. Each radial segment is traversed once in each direction, so these terms cancel. What remains is the outer circle minus the inner circle.
    - Draw the figure, with m = 8.
- **`def-toy-contour`** (stated right after the lemma, which is its model). A closed piecewise C¹ curve γ is a *toy contour in Ω* if there are closed piecewise C¹ curves β₁, …, β_m, each lying in an open convex set Cⱼ ⊆ Ω, such that ∫_γ h = Σⱼ ∫_{βⱼ} h for every h continuous on the union of the traces.
  - The β's are the boundaries of the pieces, and the identity records that the cuts cancel.
  - The figures to draw:
    - the region between two circles (m sectors);
    - the keyhole (sectors of an annulus, with the two edges of the slit kept apart);
    - the indented half-disc;
    - the rectangle and the sector of angle < π, which are convex themselves (m = 1).
- **`thm-cauchy-toy`.** If γ is a toy contour in Ω and f is holomorphic on Ω, then ∮_γ f = 0. *Proof:* one line from `cor-cauchy-convex`.
- **`lem-convex-index`.** If K is a compact convex set, a is an interior point and γ = ∂K is positively oriented, then ∮_γ dz/(z − a) = 2πi.
  - *Proof:* the chain γ − C(a; ε) is a toy contour in ℂ ∖ {a}. Cut by rays from a into thin pieces; each piece lies in a convex set avoiding a. Then apply `lem-circle-kernel`.
  - This covers the half-disc, the rectangle, the parallelogram and the sector of angle < π used in §5–§6.
  - Remark: CA.4 replaces all of this by the winding number. Here it is enough to have one lemma per shape actually used.
  - A one-line remark: the conclusion fails if g is merely holomorphic off a point inside the inner circle and that point is not removed. That situation is not covered, because the lemma assumes holomorphy on the whole annulus. Show g = 1/(z − a) as the reason the inner circle cannot be shrunk to a point for free.
- **`thm-cauchy-formula-annulus`.**
  - Statement: if f is holomorphic on A(a; r, R), r < ρ₁ < |z − a| < ρ₂ < R, then
    f(z) = (1/2πi) ∮_{C(a;ρ₂)} f(w)/(w − z) dw − (1/2πi) ∮_{C(a;ρ₁)} f(w)/(w − z) dw.
  - *Proof route (must avoid Riemann's theorem, which comes in §2):*
    - Put g(w) = (f(w) − f(z))/(w − z) for w ≠ z, and g(z) = f′(z).
    - Near z, f(w) = Σ bₖ(w − z)ᵏ (`thm-holomorphic-analytic`), so g(w) = Σ_{k≥1} bₖ(w − z)^{k−1}. Hence g is holomorphic on the whole annulus. No removable-singularity theorem is used.
    - Apply `lem-annulus-circles` to g.
    - Then ∮_{C(a;ρ₂)} dw/(w − z) = 2πi, by `lem-circle-kernel`.
    - And ∮_{C(a;ρ₁)} dw/(w − z) = 0, by `thm-cauchy-disc` on the disc D(a; |z − a|), where w ↦ 1/(w − z) is holomorphic.
    - Rearrange.
  - S2a item 1: confirm there is no circularity.
- **Scratch Work (kernel split; first appearance).** Show the two geometric series side by side.
  - Outer circle (|z − a| < |w − a|): 1/(w − z) = Σ_{n≥0} (z − a)ⁿ/(w − a)^{n+1}, with ratio |z − a|/ρ₂ < 1.
  - Inner circle (|w − a| < |z − a|): −1/(w − z) = Σ_{n≥0} (w − a)ⁿ/(z − a)^{n+1}, with ratio ρ₁/|z − a| < 1.
  - Ask the reader which ratio is small on which circle *before* writing the series; that is the whole move. Then the M-test (AN1.9) licenses termwise integration because the ratio is a constant < 1 on each circle.
- **`thm-laurent-expansion`.** Every f holomorphic on A(a; r, R) equals a Laurent series there, with
  cₙ = (1/2πi) ∮_{C(a;ρ)} f(w)(w − a)^{−n−1} dw for every n ∈ ℤ and every ρ ∈ (r, R).
  - The independence of ρ is `lem-annulus-circles` applied to f(w)(w − a)^{−n−1}.
  - The proof is the Scratch Work made formal, with the M-test bound written out: |f(w)| ≤ M_ρ on each circle, and a geometric majorant.
- **`thm-laurent-unique`.**
  - If Σ dₙ(z − a)ⁿ converges to f on A(a; r, R), then dₙ = cₙ.
  - *Proof:* by `lem-laurent-series-converges`, the convergence is uniform on C(a; ρ). Multiply by (z − a)^{−k−1} and integrate termwise. Only n = k survives, because ∮(z − a)^m dz = 0 for m ≠ −1 (`thm-primitive-closed-curve`) and = 2πi for m = −1 (`exm-circle-one-over-z`).
  - Slogan: *any honest way of producing a Laurent series on the right annulus gives the Laurent series.* This licenses all the algebraic tricks below.
- **Worked examples:**
  1. `exm-laurent-depends-on-annulus`: 1/(z(z − 1)).
     - On 0 < |z| < 1: −1/z − Σ_{n≥0} zⁿ.
     - On |z| > 1: Σ_{n≥2} z^{−n}.
     - Same function, same centre, different annuli, different series. Uniqueness is per annulus.
  2. 1/((z − 1)(z − 2)) on 1 < |z| < 2: −Σ_{n≥0} zⁿ/2^{n+1} − Σ_{n≥1} z^{−n}. Here the Laurent series has infinitely many terms of both signs.
  3. e^{1/z} on |z| > 0: Σ_{n≥0} z^{−n}/n!, by substituting into the exponential series. Uniqueness makes the substitution legitimate.
  4. sin z / z³ on |z| > 0: z^{−2} − 1/6 + z²/120 − ⋯.
- **Coefficient estimate** (`cor-laurent-coefficient-estimate`, stated as part of `thm-laurent-expansion`): |cₙ| ≤ M(ρ)/ρⁿ, where M(ρ) = max_{|w−a|=ρ} |f(w)|. This is the Cauchy estimate on an annulus. It is the tool of §2.
- **Quick check.** On which annulus about 0 does the Laurent series of 1/(z² + 4) have only negative powers? *Answer:* |z| > 2. The series there is Σ_{k≥0} (−4)ᵏ z^{−2k−2}.
- **Warning.** "The Laurent series of f at a" is meaningless until the annulus is named. In §2 the annulus is always the punctured disc 0 < |z − a| < R, and only then does the phrase become safe.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1:** Find the Laurent series of 1/(z − 3) on |z| < 3 and on |z| > 3.
    - *Answer:* −Σ zⁿ/3^{n+1}; Σ_{n≥1} 3^{n−1} z^{−n}.
  - **A2:** True or false: a Laurent series with c₋ₙ = 0 for all n ≥ 1 is a power series.
    - *Answer:* true, and then the annulus of convergence extends to a disc.
  - **B1:** Laurent series of 1/(z²(1 − z)) on 0 < |z| < 1, and of z²e^{1/z} on |z| > 0.
    - *Answers:* z^{−2} + z^{−1} + Σ_{n≥0} zⁿ; Σ_{n≥0} z^{2−n}/n!.
  - **B2:** Show, without computing the series, that c₋₁ = 0 for 1/((z − 1)(z − 2)) on |z| > 2.
    - *Answer:* by the coefficient estimate, |c₋₁| ≤ ρ · max_{|z|=ρ} |f| ≤ ρ/((ρ − 1)(ρ − 2)) → 0 as ρ → ∞.
    - Check: the series is Σ_{n≥2}(2^{n−1} − 1)z^{−n}.
  - **B3:** Prove that a Laurent series converging on A(a; r, R) can be differentiated termwise there.
    - *Answer:* apply `thm-weierstrass-limits` to the partial sums, using the uniform convergence of `lem-laurent-series-converges`.
  - **C1:** (Laurent series from the coefficient formula.) Let f be holomorphic on A(0; r, R), and let ρ ∈ (r, R). Prove that the Laurent coefficients satisfy Σ_{n∈ℤ} |cₙ|² ρ^{2n} = (1/2π)∫₀^{2π} |f(ρe^{it})|² dt.
    - *Answer:* multiply the uniformly convergent series by its conjugate and integrate termwise. The functions e^{int} are orthogonal on [0, 2π], a direct computation; no Fourier theory is needed.
  - **C2:** Use C1 to show that if |f(z)| ≤ 1 on 0 < |z| < 1, then c₋ₙ = 0 for all n ≥ 1.
    - *Answer:* for each n ≥ 1, |c₋ₙ|²ρ^{−2n} ≤ 1 for every ρ < 1, so |c₋ₙ| ≤ ρⁿ → 0. This previews §2's theorem.

## 02-isolated-singularities.md — Removable singularities, poles, and essential singularities

- **Hook.** Three functions on 0 < |z| < 1: sin z / z, 1/z³, e^{1/z}.
  - Plot |f| near 0. The first is flat, the second has a funnel, and the third is chaotic: huge on one side, near 0 on the other.
  - The Laurent series tell them apart by how many negative powers they have: none, finitely many, infinitely many.
- **Failure-motivated definition** (`def-isolated-singularity`).
  - The naive "a point where f is not defined" lumps together 1/sin(1/z) at 0 and 1/z at 0. The first has poles at 1/(kπ) piling up at 0, so no punctured disc around 0 is free of singularities, and no Laurent series about 0 exists.
  - So define: a is an isolated singularity of f if f is holomorphic on some D*(a; R) with R > 0.
  - `exm-non-isolated`: 1/sin(1/z), and also the principal branch Log z at 0. Log z is defined on no punctured disc at all as a holomorphic function, by `exm-circle-one-over-z`: ∮ dz/z ≠ 0, so 1/z has no primitive there.
- **`def-principal-part`.** The principal part is the singular half Σ_{n≥1} c₋ₙ(z − a)^{−n} of the Laurent series on D*(a; R).
- **`def-removable`, `def-pole`, `def-essential`.** The principal part is zero, a finite nonzero sum with highest index m (a pole of order m), or has infinitely many nonzero terms.
  - Give the definitions in symbols as well (STYLE §10). For example, "pole of order m" is ∃m ≥ 1: c₋ₘ ≠ 0 and ∀n > m, c₋ₙ = 0.
- **`thm-riemann-removable`.**
  - Statement: if f is holomorphic on D*(a; R) and bounded near a, then a is removable, i.e. f extends holomorphically to D(a; R).
  - More generally, (z − a)f(z) → 0 suffices.
  - *Proof:* by the coefficient estimate, |c₋ₙ| ≤ M ρⁿ for n ≥ 1 and all small ρ. Let ρ → 0. For the general form, |c₋ₙ| ≤ ρⁿ · max_{|z−a|=ρ}|f| = ρ^{n−1} · max |(z − a)f| → 0.
  - Three lines. Contrast with real analysis: |x|, and x sin(1/x), are bounded near 0 with no smooth extension. Rigidity again.
- **`thm-pole-characterization`.** For f holomorphic on D*(a; R) and m ≥ 1, the following are equivalent:
  - (i) a is a pole of order m;
  - (ii) f(z) = g(z)/(z − a)^m with g holomorphic on D(a; R) and g(a) ≠ 0;
  - (iii) 1/f, defined near a, has a removable singularity at a whose extension has a zero of order m (`def-zero-order`).
  - In particular, a is a pole iff |f(z)| → ∞ as z → a.
  - *Proof route:*
    - (i)⇔(ii) by multiplying the Laurent series by (z − a)^m.
    - (ii)⇒(iii) by 1/f = (z − a)^m/g.
    - (iii)⇒(ii) by `thm-zeros-isolated`.
    - For the last sentence: if |f| → ∞, then 1/f → 0 is bounded near a, so `thm-riemann-removable` applies to 1/f. Then 1/f has a zero, not identically zero, so of finite order by `thm-zeros-isolated`.
- **`cor-singularity-by-limits`** (the working classification). For an isolated singularity a:
  - lim_{z→a} f(z) exists in ℂ ⇔ removable;
  - |f(z)| → ∞ ⇔ pole;
  - neither ⇔ essential.
  - Present it as a three-row table. It is used for every classification exercise.
- **Scratch Work (invert to bound; first appearance).** Suppose f misses a whole disc D(w₀; δ) near a.
  - Then |f − w₀| ≥ δ, so 1/(f − w₀) is bounded by 1/δ.
  - Riemann applies to 1/(f − w₀). Then undo the inversion with `thm-pole-characterization`.
  - Ask: what are the two cases for the extension h of 1/(f − w₀) at a? Either h(a) ≠ 0, which makes f removable, or h(a) = 0, which makes f a pole.
- **`thm-casorati-weierstrass`.**
  - Statement: if a is an essential singularity of f, then for every r ∈ (0, R) the image f(D*(a; r)) is dense in ℂ.
  - Equivalently, ∀w ∈ ℂ ∃zₙ → a with f(zₙ) → w.
  - *Proof:* the Scratch Work as a contrapositive. Give both forms in symbols.
- **`exm-exp-one-over-z`** (pays CA.1's preview).
  - On the positive real axis e^{1/x} → ∞, on the negative real axis → 0, and on the imaginary axis |e^{1/(iy)}| = 1.
  - Every w ≠ 0 is attained infinitely often in every D*(0; r): solve 1/z = log|w| + i(arg w + 2πk), and take |k| large.
  - The single value 0 is omitted.
  - **Hook opened:** is omitting one value the most an essential singularity can do? Pointer to CA.14 (great Picard) in prose.
- `exm-sinc-removable`: sin z / z, extended by 1, with Laurent series 1 − z²/6 + ⋯.
- **Quick check.** Classify 0 for (1 − cos z)/z⁴. *Answer:* a pole of order 2, with Laurent series z^{−2}/2 − 1/24 + ⋯.
- **Warning.** A function that is unbounded near a need not have a pole there. e^{1/z} is unbounded, but its modulus does not tend to ∞. Only removability is ruled out by unboundedness.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1:** Classify the singularity at 0 of z/sin z, of sin z/z², of z e^{1/z²}, and of cos(1/z).
    - *Answers:* removable; pole of order 1; essential; essential.
  - **A2:** True or false: if f has a pole of order m at a, then f′ has a pole of order m + 1.
    - *Answer:* true; differentiate the Laurent series.
  - **B1:** Show that if f has a pole at a, then e^{f} has an essential singularity at a.
    - *Answer:* write f = g/(z − a)^m with g(a) ≠ 0. On the ray z = a + te^{iθ}, f = t^{−m}e^{−imθ}(g(a) + o(1)) as t → 0⁺.
    - Choose θ with e^{−imθ}g(a) = |g(a)|. Then Re f → +∞, so e^f is unbounded and a is not removable.
    - Choose θ with e^{−imθ}g(a) = −|g(a)|. Then |e^f| = e^{Re f} → 0, so |e^f| does not tend to ∞, and a is not a pole.
  - **B2:** Find all solutions of e^{1/z} = i in 0 < |z| < 0.01, and the one of largest modulus.
    - *Answer:* z = 1/(i(π/2 + 2πk)) with |π/2 + 2πk| > 100, i.e. k ≥ 16 or k ≤ −17.
    - The largest modulus is at k = 16: z = −i/(32.5π), |z| ≈ 0.00979. Sidecar verifies.
  - **B3:** Let f be holomorphic on D*(a; R), with |f(z)| ≤ C|z − a|^{−3/2}. Show that a is removable or a pole of order 1.
    - *Answer:* the coefficient estimate gives |c₋ₙ| ≤ Cρ^{n−3/2} → 0 for n ≥ 2.
  - **C1:** Let f be entire, and let g(z) = f(1/z) on z ≠ 0. Show that 0 is removable for g iff f is constant.
    - *Answer:* removable means g is bounded near 0, so f is bounded on a neighbourhood of ∞; then Liouville. This links to §3.
  - **C2:** Prove: if f has an essential singularity at a and P is a non-constant polynomial, then P∘f has an essential singularity at a.
    - *Answer:* if P∘f were removable or a pole, |P(f)| would tend to a finite limit or to ∞. If |P(f)| → ∞, then |f| → ∞, so f is a pole: contradiction. If P(f) → L, Casorati–Weierstrass gives zₙ with f(zₙ) → w for any w, so P(w) = L for every w: contradiction.
  - **C3 (find the error; the conclusion is true):** "e^{1/z} has an essential singularity at 0. Proof: e^{1/x} → ∞ as x → 0⁺, so e^{1/z} is unbounded near 0, so 0 is not removable, hence essential."
    - *Answer:* the conclusion is true; the argument skips the pole case. Unboundedness excludes only removability. The fix is the negative real axis, where the limit is 0, so |f| does not tend to ∞.

## 03-infinity-and-meromorphic-functions.md — The point at infinity, and meromorphic functions

- **Hook.** zⁿ and e^z both tend to "infinity" in some sense, but zⁿ does it uniformly in every direction and e^z does not: on the imaginary axis |e^{iy}| = 1.
  - We need a language for the behaviour at ∞. The substitution w = 1/z turns it into the behaviour at w = 0, which §2 classifies.
  - Prose pointer: CA.10 builds the Riemann sphere, on which this is literally a chart change. This chapter needs only the substitution.
  - **Hook paid:** this section pays CA.10's hook "which maps extend to continuous maps of Ĉ? Rational ones do; are they the only meromorphic ones?" It does so by `thm-meromorphic-extended-rational`.
    - The pointer to CA.10's `def-extended-plane` and its §02 C1 is in prose, unless the coordinator grants a CA.3 → CA.10 edge.
    - In that case, "meromorphic on ℂ ∪ {∞}" is read on Ĉ with CA.10's chart at ∞, and nothing else changes.
- **`def-singularity-at-infinity`.**
  - f has an isolated singularity at ∞ if f is holomorphic on |z| > R. Its type (removable, pole of order m, essential) is that of g(w) = f(1/w) at w = 0.
  - Translate the Laurent series: the "principal part at ∞" is the part with *positive* powers of z. Show this as a table: at a finite point the singular part has negative powers; at ∞ it has positive powers.
- **Failure-motivated example:** z/(e^z − 1) has poles at 2πik, k ≠ 0, which escape to ∞. So ∞ is not an isolated singularity of it, and the definition rightly refuses to classify it. This is a weird-examples row.
- **`thm-entire-pole-at-infinity`.**
  - Statement: an entire function f has
    - a removable singularity at ∞ iff it is constant;
    - a pole of order m at ∞ iff it is a polynomial of degree m;
    - otherwise an essential singularity at ∞.
  - *Proof:* the Laurent series of f on |z| > 0 is its Taylor series (`thm-laurent-unique`). Translate.
- **`cor-transcendental-essential`.** If f is entire and not a polynomial, then for every R the set f({|z| > R}) is dense in ℂ. This is Casorati–Weierstrass at ∞.
  - Example: cos z takes values arbitrarily close to any w on |z| > 10⁶.
- **`def-meromorphic`.** f is meromorphic on an open set Ω if it is holomorphic on Ω except at a set of points, each of which is a pole.
  - That set is discrete in Ω: closed in Ω and without accumulation points in Ω. Note why: a pole is isolated by definition, and an accumulation point of poles inside Ω would be a non-isolated singularity, which `def-meromorphic` excludes.
  - It is meromorphic on ℂ ∪ {∞} if, in addition, ∞ is removable or a pole.
- **Scratch Work (subtract the principal parts; first appearance).** Given the finitely many poles a₁, …, a_N of f and their principal parts P₁, …, P_N:
  - each Pₖ is a polynomial in 1/(z − aₖ), so it is holomorphic off aₖ and tends to 0 at ∞;
  - f − ΣPₖ has only removable singularities in ℂ (`thm-laurent-unique`: subtracting Pₖ removes the singular part at aₖ and adds nothing singular elsewhere);
  - so it extends to an entire function. Then the behaviour at ∞ decides what that entire function is.
- **`thm-partial-fractions`.**
  - Statement: if f is meromorphic on ℂ ∪ {∞}, then f has finitely many poles, and f = Q + Σ_{k=1}^N Pₖ, where Pₖ is the principal part at the finite pole aₖ and Q is a polynomial (the principal part at ∞ plus a constant).
  - *Finitely many:* poles cannot accumulate at a finite point. They cannot accumulate at ∞ either, because ∞ is isolated. Hence they lie in a compact set, and a discrete closed subset of a compact set is finite. Cite AN2.3, compactness via Bolzano–Weierstrass.
  - The proof is written in full, the first stage of the faded triple.
- **`thm-meromorphic-extended-rational`.** f is meromorphic on ℂ ∪ {∞} iff f is rational. The direction ⇐ is immediate. The direction ⇒ is `thm-partial-fractions`.
  - Remark: this is the uniqueness half of partial fractions over ℂ, used throughout §5.
  - Remark (hook to CA.12): for infinitely many poles in ℂ, "subtract the principal parts" diverges in general. Mittag-Leffler repairs it with convergence-producing corrections. §6 C3 meets the first instance.
- **Worked example.** Decompose (z⁴ + 1)/(z(z − 1)²).
  - *Answer:* z + 2 + 1/z + 2/(z − 1)² + 2/(z − 1).
  - The poles: at 0, Res 1; at 1, of order 2. Sidecar verifies, and also shows the principal part at 1 computed via the Laurent series in u = z − 1.
- **Quick check.** What is the type of ∞ for (z² + 1)/(z − 1)? For e^{1/z}? *Answers:* a pole of order 1; removable, with value 1.
- **Warning.** A rational function's pole at ∞ is not visible from its denominator. 1/(z − 1) has none, and z²/(z − 1) has one. Compare degrees.
- **[C1: Exit check: classify every singularity, including ∞.]** For z/(e^z − 1), sin(1/z) and e^{1/z}/(z² − 1).
  - *Answer:*
    - z/(e^z − 1): removable at 0 (value 1); simple poles at 2πik, k ≠ 0; ∞ not isolated.
    - sin(1/z): essential at 0; removable at ∞ (value 0).
    - e^{1/z}/(z² − 1): simple poles at ±1; essential at 0; removable at ∞, with a zero of order 2.
  - *Marking scheme:* 1 mark per point correctly classified, with a reason (a limit or the Laurent series). Deduct for "∞ is a pole" in the first function.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1:** Type of ∞ for z³ − z, sin z, 1/(z² + 1), z sin(1/z).
    - *Answers:* pole of order 3; essential; removable (zero of order 2); removable (value 1).
  - **A2:** Can a non-constant meromorphic function on ℂ ∪ {∞} have no poles?
    - *Answer:* no. It would be entire with ∞ removable, hence constant.
  - **B1:** Find the partial-fraction decomposition of 1/(z⁴ − 1).
    - *Answer:* Σ_{k=0}^3 1/(4ζₖ³(z − ζₖ)) with ζₖ = iᵏ; equivalently, (1/4)[1/(z − 1) − 1/(z + 1) + i/(z − i) − i/(z + i)].
  - **B2:** Show that an entire function f with |f(z)| ≤ A + B|z|^{5/2} is a polynomial of degree ≤ 2.
    - *Answer:* the coefficient estimate at ∞ (or `thm-cauchy-estimates`) kills cₙ, n ≥ 3.
  - **C1:** Let R = p/q be rational, with p and q coprime, and let d = max(deg p, deg q). Show that R has exactly d poles in ℂ ∪ {∞}, counted with order, and exactly d zeros. Deduce that R − w has d zeros for every w ∈ ℂ, counted with order, when ∞ is counted.
    - *Answer:* the poles in ℂ are the zeros of q, which have total order deg q. There is a pole at ∞ of order deg p − deg q when that is positive. The total is d. For the zeros, apply the same count to 1/R. For R − w, the degree is unchanged unless w = R(∞), and then the zero at ∞ makes up the difference.
    - This is the analytic counterpart of CA.10's §02 C1 count (prose pointer).

## 04-residues.md — Residues and the residue theorem

- **Hook (failure-motivated definition).** Cauchy's theorem says ∮_C f = 0 when f is holomorphic inside C. What survives when f has an isolated singularity inside?
  - Integrate the Laurent series term by term over C(a; ρ). Every (z − a)ⁿ with n ≠ −1 has a primitive, so it contributes 0. Only c₋₁ survives: ∮ f = 2πi c₋₁.
  - One coefficient out of infinitely many carries all the integral information. Name it.
  - Needham-style picture, described only: the loop "sees" only the z⁻¹ term.
- **`def-residue`.** Res(f, a) = c₋₁, the coefficient on D*(a; R).
  - `exm-residue-zero-pole`: Res(1/z², 0) = 0, a pole with residue 0. Res(e^{1/z}, 0) = 1, an essential singularity with residue 1.
  - The residue does not detect the type of the singularity.
- **`thm-residue-theorem`** (circle and toy-contour version).
  - Statement: let Ω be open, let a₁, …, a_N ∈ Ω, and let f be holomorphic on Ω ∖ {a₁, …, a_N}. Let γ be a toy contour in Ω (`def-toy-contour`) whose trace avoids every aₖ. Then ∮_γ f = Σₖ Res(f, aₖ) ∮_γ dz/(z − aₖ).
  - In particular, ∮_γ f = 2πi Σₖ Res(f, aₖ) when every aₖ satisfies ∮_γ dz/(z − aₖ) = 2πi. That holds for a circle around aₖ (`lem-circle-kernel`) and for the boundary of a convex set containing aₖ in its interior (`lem-convex-index`).
  - The keyhole and the indented contours of §5 compute ∮_γ dz/(z − a) directly in each example.
  - Isolated singularities of any type are allowed, essential ones included.
  - *Proof:* subtract the principal parts.
    - Each Pₖ(z) = Σ_{n≥1} c₋ₙ⁽ᵏ⁾(z − aₖ)^{−n} converges for all z ≠ aₖ, uniformly on γ. It is a power series in 1/(z − aₖ) with infinite radius, by `lem-laurent-series-converges`.
    - So ∮_γ Pₖ = c₋₁⁽ᵏ⁾ ∮_γ dz/(z − aₖ) = 2πi c₋₁⁽ᵏ⁾. Termwise integration; the n ≥ 2 terms have primitives. The factor ∮_γ dz/(z − aₖ) is left as it is in the statement.
    - The function f − ΣPₖ has removable singularities only, so it extends holomorphically to Ω. Then `thm-cauchy-toy` gives 0.
  - For an essential singularity, Pₖ is an infinite series, and that is why the uniform convergence on γ must be stated. S2a item 3.
  - Remark: for a general closed curve the factor 2πi·Res becomes 2πi·n(γ, aₖ)·Res, with the winding number. That is CA.4, prose only.
- **Computing residues.**
  - `prp-residue-simple-pole`: at a simple pole, Res(f, a) = lim_{z→a} (z − a)f(z).
  - `prp-residue-quotient`: if g, h are holomorphic at a, g(a) ≠ 0, h(a) = 0, h′(a) ≠ 0, then Res(g/h, a) = g(a)/h′(a).
  - `prp-residue-higher-order`: at a pole of order ≤ m, Res(f, a) = (1/(m − 1)!) lim_{z→a} d^{m−1}/dz^{m−1}[(z − a)^m f(z)].
  - Scratch Work for the last one only: multiply the Laurent series by (z − a)^m, so that c₋₁ becomes the coefficient of (z − a)^{m−1} in a Taylor series. That coefficient is read off by differentiating.
  - Remark: when m is large, expanding the Laurent series directly is usually faster than the formula. Show both on e^z/z³: Res = 1/2.
- **Worked examples:**
  1. Res(1/(e^z − 1), 2πik) = 1, by the quotient formula.
  2. Res(cot z, 0) = 1.
  3. Res(1/(z² + 1)², i) = −i/4. By the formula: d/dz (z + i)^{−2} = −2(z + i)^{−3}, which at i is −2/(2i)³.
  4. Res(e^{1/z}/(z² − 1), 1) = e/2.
- **`def-residue-at-infinity`.** Res(f, ∞) = −(1/2πi) ∮_{C(0;ρ)} f for large ρ. Equivalently, minus the coefficient of z⁻¹ in the Laurent series on |z| > ρ.
  - Warning: Res(f, ∞) can be nonzero when ∞ is removable. For f = 1/z, Res(f, ∞) = −1.
- **`prp-residues-sum-zero`.** If f is holomorphic on ℂ except at finitely many points, the sum of all its residues, ∞ included, is 0.
  - *Proof:* `thm-residue-theorem` on a large circle, plus the definition.
- **Quick checks:**
  - Res(z³ e^{1/z}, 0) = ? *Answer:* 1/24.
  - Hook toward CA.4: if f has a zero of order m at a, what is Res(f′/f, a)? *Answer:* m, since f = (z − a)^m g gives f′/f = m/(z − a) + g′/g. Remark in prose: CA.4 turns this into a zero-counting formula.
- **Warning.** `prp-residue-simple-pole` applied at a double pole gives ∞ or nonsense. Check the order first. Example: z⁻² e^z at 0 has residue 1, but lim z·f(z) does not exist.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1:** Residues of z/(z² + 9) at ±3i, of 1/(z³ − 1) at 1, of sin z/z⁴ at 0.
    - *Answers:* 1/2, 1/2; 1/3; −1/6.
  - **A2:** True or false: if Res(f, a) = 0, then a is removable. *Answer:* false; 1/z².
  - **B1:** Evaluate ∮_{|z|=2} (z + 1)/(z(z − 1)(z − 3)) dz.
    - *Answer:* the poles inside are 0 and 1. Res at 0 = 1/3; Res at 1 = 2/(1·(−2)) = −1. So the integral is 2πi(−2/3) = −4πi/3.
  - **B2:** Evaluate ∮_{|z|=1} e^{1/z} sin(1/z) dz.
    - *Answer:* 2πi times the coefficient of z⁻¹, which is 1 (from 1 · (1/z)). So 2πi.
  - **B3:** Evaluate ∮_{|z|=2} dz/(z⁵ − 1) using `prp-residues-sum-zero`.
    - *Answer:* 0. Res(f, ∞) = 0, since f = O(z^{−5}).
  - **B4:** Show that Res(f, a) = 0 whenever f is even about a, i.e. f(a + h) = f(a − h).
    - *Answer:* the Laurent series has only even powers.
  - **B5 (faded, last step blank).** Prove the residue theorem for f = 1/(z²(z − 1)) on C(0; 2) by subtracting principal parts.
    - Given: P₀ = −1/z² − 1/z and P₁ = 1/(z − 1).
    - Blank: *Name the move, then complete it.* Show f − P₀ − P₁ ≡ 0, hence the integral is 2πi(−1 + 1) = 0.
  - **C1:** Let f have a simple pole at a with residue r, and let g be holomorphic at a. Find Res(fg, a), and Res(f², a) when f = r/(z − a) + c₀ + ⋯.
    - *Answers:* r·g(a); 2rc₀.
  - **C2:** Prove: Res(f, a) is the unique complex number c such that f(z) − c/(z − a) has a primitive on some D*(a; ε).
    - *Answer:* the term c₋₁/(z − a) is the only obstruction to a termwise primitive; uniqueness follows from ∮ dz/(z − a) ≠ 0.

## 05-real-integrals.md — Real integrals by residues

- **Hook paid.** This section opens with `exm-hook-pi-over-e` itself, worked to the end as the model of the method.
  - f(z) = e^{iz}/(1 + z²) on the upper half-disc of radius R > 1.
  - Res(f, i) = e^{−1}/(2i), so the contour integral is π/e.
  - On the arc, |e^{iz}| ≤ 1 because Im z ≥ 0, and |1 + z²| ≥ R² − 1. So the arc contributes ≤ πR/(R² − 1) → 0.
  - The real segment tends to ∫ e^{ix}/(1 + x²) dx. Take the real part.
  - Then name the four steps as the named move "close the contour and kill the arc". Every later example is graded against them: choose f, choose the contour, list the residues inside, estimate the extra pieces.
- **`def-principal-value`.**
  - Failure-motivated: ∫_{−∞}^{∞} x/(1 + x²) dx diverges, because each half diverges. Yet lim_{R→∞} ∫_{−R}^{R} = 0 by oddness. Semicircle contours compute exactly these symmetric limits.
  - So define p.v. ∫_{−∞}^{∞} f = lim_{R→∞} ∫_{−R}^{R} f, and the analogous symmetric excision around a point singularity on ℝ.
  - Rule stated in a box: **when the improper integral converges, it equals the principal value**. So a contour computation proves the value of a convergent integral, and only the existence must be checked separately, by a comparison test from AN1.8.
- **Family 1 — trigonometric integrals** (`thm-trig-integrals`).
  - Statement: if R(x, y) is a rational function with no pole on the circle x² + y² = 1, then ∫₀^{2π} R(cos θ, sin θ) dθ = ∮_{|z|=1} R((z + z⁻¹)/2, (z − z⁻¹)/(2i)) dz/(iz) = 2π Σ_{|a|<1} Res(R(…)/z, a).
  - Scratch Work (first family): z = e^{iθ} turns the interval into the circle, and the dictionary cos θ = (z + 1/z)/2, dθ = dz/(iz).
  - Worked example: ∫₀^{2π} dθ/(a + cos θ) = 2π/√(a² − 1) for a > 1. The root of z² + 2az + 1 = 0 inside the disc is −a + √(a² − 1). Check: their product is 1 and only one lies inside.
- **Family 2 — rational integrals** (`thm-rational-integrals`, with `lem-semicircle-decay`).
  - `lem-semicircle-decay`: if |f(z)| ≤ M/|z|² on |z| = R for large R, then the arc integral → 0.
  - Statement: if P, Q are polynomials with deg Q ≥ deg P + 2 and Q has no real zeros, then ∫_{−∞}^{∞} P/Q = 2πi Σ_{Im a>0} Res(P/Q, a).
  - Scratch Work: the estimate |Q(z)| ≥ |q_d| R^d/2 for large R. The leading term dominates. Write the explicit R₀.
  - Worked example: ∫_{−∞}^{∞} dx/(1 + x⁴) = π/√2. The poles inside are e^{iπ/4} and e^{3iπ/4}. With `prp-residue-quotient`, the residue at a is 1/(4a³) = −a/4.
- **Family 3 — Fourier-type integrals** (`thm-fourier-integrals`, with `lem-jordan`).
  - Failure-motivated: for x sin x/(x² + 1), the integrand decays like 1/x only, so the decay lemma's ML bound gives πR · R/(R² − 1) ↛ 0. The arc has to be estimated better, using the decay of e^{iz} in the upper half-plane.
  - `lem-jordan`: for R > 0, ∫₀^π e^{−R sin t} dt < π/R.
    - *Proof:* sin t ≥ 2t/π on [0, π/2] (concavity), plus symmetry.
    - Scratch Work: why the naive bound e^{−R sin t} ≤ 1 loses, and where the mass of the integral sits (near t = 0 and t = π).
  - Statement: if deg Q ≥ deg P + 1, Q has no real zeros and λ > 0, then p.v. ∫_{−∞}^{∞} e^{iλx} P(x)/Q(x) dx = 2πi Σ_{Im a>0} Res(e^{iλz}P/Q, a). (When deg Q = deg P + 1, only the principal value is asserted in general.)
  - Worked example: ∫_{−∞}^{∞} x sin x/(x² + 1) dx = π/e. Res(z e^{iz}/(z² + 1), i) = e^{−1}/2.
  - Remark: for λ < 0, close in the lower half-plane. The orientation flips the sign.
- **Indented contours** (`lem-small-arc`).
  - Statement: if f has a simple pole at a and Γ_ε is an arc of C(a; ε) of angle θ, oriented counterclockwise, then ∫_{Γ_ε} f → iθ Res(f, a) as ε → 0.
  - *Proof:* f = r/(z − a) + (holomorphic, bounded near a). The first term integrates exactly to iθr. The second is O(ε).
  - Weird example: the lemma fails at a double pole. For 1/z² the semicircle integral has modulus 2/ε, which diverges; p.v. ∫ dx/x² = +∞.
  - Prose: CA.2's computation of ∫₀^∞ sin x/x dx = π/2 is this lemma with θ = π. Revisit only, with no new proof.
  - Worked example: p.v. ∫_{−∞}^{∞} e^{ix}/(x(x² + 1)) dx = πi(1 − 1/e). The indentation at 0 contributes πi·Res at 0 = πi·1, and the residue at i is e^{−1}/(i·2i) = −e^{−1}/2, giving 2πi(−1/(2e)) = −πi/e. The total is πi − πi/e.
  - Imaginary part: ∫_{−∞}^{∞} sin x/(x(x² + 1)) dx = π(1 − 1/e). Sidecar verifies.
- **Family 4 — branch cuts** (`exm-keyhole`).
  - ∫₀^∞ x^{α−1}/(1 + x) dx = π/sin(πα) for 0 < α < 1.
  - Plan line (fading: no Scratch Work):
    - take the branch of z^{α−1} on ℂ ∖ [0, ∞) with 0 < arg z < 2π (`def-branch-log`);
    - use the keyhole toy contour of §1 (`def-toy-contour`) in Ω = ℂ ∖ [0, ∞), where f − P is holomorphic with P = Res/(z + 1);
    - the keyhole has edges at arg z = δ and 2π − δ. For dz/(z + 1), the keyhole integral equals ∮_{C(0;R)} − ∮_{C(0;ε)} minus the integral over the boundary of the thin wedge |arg z| ≤ δ. That wedge lies in a convex set avoiding −1, so the total is 2πi − 0 − 0;
    - the residue at −1 is e^{iπ(α−1)};
    - the small and large circles vanish because 0 < α < 1;
    - the two edges give (1 − e^{2πi(α−1)}) ∫₀^∞. Solve.
  - Remark: this integral is Γ(α)Γ(1 − α). That identity is CA.13's reflection formula. Prose pointer.
- **Quick check.** Which contour would you use for ∫₀^∞ dx/(1 + x³)?
  - *Answer:* the sector 0 ≤ arg z ≤ 2π/3, which contains one pole, e^{iπ/3}. The value is 2π/(3√3).
- **Warning.** Q must have no real zeros, or the integral is not even improper-convergent. Indent at simple real zeros (principal value), and give up at double ones.

## 06-summing-series.md — Summing series with residues

- **Hook.** §5 turned integrals into residues. Can sums be turned into residues too?
  - We need a function with a simple pole at every integer, with residue 1, that is bounded far away. π cot πz is that function, because Res(π cot πz, n) = 1 by `prp-residue-quotient`.
  - So ∮ f(z)·π cot πz picks up f(n) at every integer inside the contour.
- **`lem-cot-bounded-squares`.**
  - Statement: on the boundary of the square S_N with vertices (N + 1/2)(±1 ± i), N ≥ 0 an integer, |cot πz| ≤ coth(π/2).
  - Proof Idea only (fading):
    - on the vertical sides cot πz = −i tanh(πy), so the modulus is ≤ 1;
    - on the horizontal sides use |cot π(x + iy)| ≤ coth(π|y|) ≤ coth(π/2);
    - the full computation is a B exercise.
- **`thm-cot-summation`.**
  - Statement: let f = P/Q be rational with deg Q ≥ deg P + 2, and with poles a₁, …, a_M none of which is an integer. Then Σ_{n∈ℤ} f(n) = −Σ_k Res(π cot(πz) f(z), aₖ).
  - The sum converges absolutely by comparison with Σ 1/n².
  - *Proof:* `thm-residue-theorem` on S_N for N large. The contour integral is ≤ 4(2N + 1) · coth(π/2) · max_{∂S_N} |f| = O(1/N). Let N → ∞.
- **`cor-csc-summation`.** The same with π/sin πz: Σ (−1)ⁿ f(n) = −Σ Res(π csc(πz) f(z), aₖ). The proof is identical; the bound |csc πz| ≤ 1 on ∂S_N is an exercise.
- **Worked examples:**
  1. Σ_{n∈ℤ} 1/(n² + a²) = (π/a) coth(πa) for a > 0. Hence Σ_{n≥1} 1/(n² + a²) = (πa coth(πa) − 1)/(2a²).
  2. `exm-basel`: Σ_{n≥1} 1/n² = π²/6.
     - Route 1: let a → 0 in example 1, using πa coth πa = 1 + (πa)²/3 + O(a⁴). Justify the limit under the sum by the M-test (AN1.9).
     - Route 2: here f has its pole at an integer, so the theorem as stated does not apply. The modified version with f(z) = 1/z² includes 0 among the poles of π cot(πz) f(z). From π cot πz = 1/z − π²z/3 − ⋯, Res(π cot(πz)/z², 0) = −π²/3. So Σ_{n≠0} 1/n² = π²/3.
     - State this modified version as a remark with its one-line proof.
  3. Σ_{n∈ℤ} (−1)ⁿ/(n² + 1) = π/sinh π, by `cor-csc-summation`.
- **Quick check.** Why must deg Q ≥ deg P + 2? What goes wrong for f(z) = 1/z?
  - *Answer:* Σ 1/n is not absolutely convergent, and the contour bound is O(1), not O(1/N).
- **Warning.** The theorem's sum is over all of ℤ. To get Σ_{n≥1}, use evenness of f, and remember the n = 0 term.
- **[C3: Exit check.]** Show that Σ_{n≥1} 1/(n² + 1) = (π coth π − 1)/2. Then compute Σ_{n≥1} 1/n⁴.
  - *Answers:* the first from example 1 with a = 1; π⁴/90 for the second, using the coefficient −π⁴/45 of z³ in π cot πz.
  - *Marking scheme:*
    - correct poles and residues (2);
    - the square-contour bound quoted correctly, with its hypothesis checked (2);
    - the passage from ℤ to n ≥ 1 (1);
    - the Taylor coefficient of cot, derived and not quoted (2).
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1:** True or false: Res(π cot πz, n) = 1 for every n ∈ ℤ. *Answer:* true.
  - **A2:** Which kernel, π cot πz or π csc πz, sums Σ (−1)ⁿ/n² ? *Answer:* π csc πz.
  - **B1:** Prove `lem-cot-bounded-squares` in full.
  - **B2:** Σ_{n∈ℤ} 1/(n² + 4). *Answer:* (π/2) coth 2π.
  - **B3:** Σ_{n≥1} (−1)^{n+1}/n² = π²/12, from the csc version at the pole 0.
    - *Answer:* Res(π csc(πz)/z², 0) = π²/6. Hence Σ_{n≠0}(−1)ⁿ/n² = −π²/6, so Σ_{n≥1}(−1)ⁿ/n² = −π²/12, and the alternating sum with sign (−1)^{n+1} is π²/12.
  - **C1:** Show that Σ_{n≥1} 1/(n²(n² + 1)) = π²/6 − (π coth π − 1)/2.
    - *Answer:* partial fractions, 1/n² − 1/(n² + 1).
  - **C2:** Prove that ζ(2k) is a rational multiple of π^{2k}, by reading off the Taylor coefficients of π cot πz at 0. No closed form with Bernoulli numbers is required.
    - *Answer:* Res(π cot(πz)/z^{2k}, 0) is the coefficient of z^{2k−1} in π cot πz, a rational multiple of π^{2k}. Here πz cot πz has a Taylor series with coefficients in ℚ·π^{2j}, by the series of cos and sin and long division.
  - **C3 (faded, last half blank; the third stage of the triple).** For fixed z ∉ ℤ, apply `thm-residue-theorem` on S_N to g(w) = π cot(πw)/(w² − z²) to prove π cot πz = 1/z + Σ_{n≥1} 2z/(z² − n²).
    - The setup and the residues at w = ±z and at w = n are given.
    - Blank: *Name the move, then complete it.* (Bound the contour integral, then let N → ∞.)
    - *Answer:* the contour integral → 0. The residues at ±z sum to π cot(πz)/z, and at n the residue is 1/(n² − z²). Rearrange.
    - Remark (hook to CA.12): the series is the sum of the principal parts 1/(z − n), grouped in pairs to make them converge.
