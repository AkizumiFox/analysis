**Purpose.**
- Real parts of holomorphic functions satisfy Laplace's equation. The chapter asks the converse question (which solutions of Δu = 0 are real parts?) and then the physical one: given temperatures on the edge of a plate, is there exactly one steady state inside?
- For the disc the answer is complete and explicit: the Poisson integral. It is derived from the Cauchy formula and a Möbius automorphism, and shown to attain continuous boundary data (Schwarz's theorem).
- The mean value property is shown to *characterize* harmonic functions among continuous ones. That characterization is what survives limits and what CA.5's reflection proof and CA.11's Perron method run on.

## Chapter hook and where it is paid

- **Main hook (index.md).** A thin plate shaped like a region Ω has its edge held at a prescribed temperature g. Physics says the steady temperature u satisfies Δu = 0 and equals g on the edge. Two questions: *is there such a u, and is there only one?* Show three plates: a disc (data cos t), a half-plane (data 1 on (−1, 1), 0 elsewhere), and a punctured disc (0 on the circle, 1 at the centre).
  - **Paid in §2** (only one, on bounded regions), **§4** (the disc: yes, explicitly), **§5** (half-plane and strip: transplant to the disc), **§6** (the punctured disc: *no* solution, `exm-punctured-disc-dirichlet`).
  - **Left open, paid later:** a general simply connected region needs a conformal map onto the disc (CA.5, Riemann mapping theorem; boundary behaviour is outside the volume, so CA.5 pays this only for regions where the map extends, as a remark) and a general region needs Perron's method (CA.11). Say both in the index's last paragraph, in prose.
- **Second hook (end of §1).** f is holomorphic on the upper half-disc and Im f(z) → 0 as z approaches the real diameter. Can f be continued across? Morera (CA.2) would need f itself to be continuous up to the line with real values; here only Im f is controlled. **Paid in CA.5** by harmonic continuation, using §6. §6 ends with a one-line "the tool is now in hand" pointer.
- **Internal hook (§2 → §6).** The condition Δu = 0 involves second derivatives, so it says nothing about a uniform limit of harmonic functions; the mean value property involves no derivatives and passes to limits at once. *Is the mean value property enough?* Paid in §6 (`thm-mvp-implies-harmonic`).

**Named moves** (index.md "Named moves"):
- **Conjugate by primitive.** To find v with u + iv holomorphic, integrate g = u_x − i u_y, which is holomorphic.
- **Move the point to the centre.** Precompose with a disc automorphism φ_a so that a statement at 0 becomes a statement at a.
- **Near and far.** Split an integral against a kernel into the arc near the target point (where the data are almost constant) and the rest (where the kernel is uniformly small). The volume's first use; HA.2's version is a look back.
- **Log perturbation.** Add ε log(|z|/r) to push a function to −∞ at a puncture, apply the maximum principle on an annulus, let ε → 0.

## 01-harmonic-functions.md — Harmonic functions and their conjugates (budget ≤ 4,200 words)

- **Hook.** Re f for f holomorphic: write f = u + iv, apply Cauchy–Riemann twice, get u_xx + u_yy = 0. Needham's picture in two sentences: u is a potential whose level curves cross the level curves of v at right angles (flow lines versus equipotentials). The question: *does every solution of Laplace's equation arise this way?*
- `def-laplacian`, `def-harmonic`: real-valued, C² on an open set, Δu = 0. Give the symbolic form. Remark: "C²" is required in the definition; §6 shows it can be weakened to "continuous with the mean value property", and that is the definition that survives limits.
- `thm-real-part-harmonic`: Re f and Im f are harmonic for f holomorphic. Proof: C^∞ from CA.2, then CR twice and equality of mixed partials (b AN3.1, Schwarz–Clairaut, cited).
- **Failure-motivated definition.** Try to reverse: given u = log|z| on ℂ∖{0}, look for v. Locally v = arg z works; globally arg z has no continuous branch. So "u is a real part" is a property with content, and gets a name:
  - `def-harmonic-conjugate`: v harmonic with u + iv holomorphic on the same open set.
  - `exm-log-modulus-no-conjugate`: proof by contradiction: f′ = u_x − i u_y = 1/z, and ∮_{|z|=1} 1/z dz = 2πi ≠ 0 contradicts f being a primitive of 1/z.
- `thm-conjugate-disc`: on a disc (more generally on any open set where every holomorphic function has a primitive; CA.2 gives discs, so state for discs and convex sets) every harmonic u has a conjugate.
  - **Scratch Work (first appearance of "conjugate by primitive").** Before the proof, show where g = u_x − i u_y comes from: if u + iv = f, then f′ = u_x + iv_x = u_x − i u_y by CR; so g is the only candidate for f′. Check CR for g using Δu = 0 and mixed partials; g is C¹, hence holomorphic. Take a primitive F (CA.2), observe (Re F)_x = u_x and (Re F)_y = u_y, so Re F − u is constant; adjust.
  - **Proof** (separate, typeset, no motivation): the four lines in logical order.
- `cor-harmonic-smooth`: harmonic ⇒ C^∞ (locally a real part; CA.2).
- `thm-conjugate-unique`: on a connected open set, two conjugates differ by a real constant. One paragraph.
- **Remark (see CA.4).** On a simply connected region the same proof works, because the global Cauchy theorem gives primitives there. Prose pointer only; nothing later in this chapter uses it.
- `lem-laplacian-composition`: for f = p + iq holomorphic and u C², Δ(u ∘ f) = |f′|² (Δu) ∘ f. Prove by chain rule and CR; give the computation as a two-column display.
- `thm-harmonic-conformal-invariance`: u harmonic on V, f: U → V holomorphic ⇒ u ∘ f harmonic on U. Two proofs: from the lemma; and locally, u = Re h on a disc so u ∘ f = Re(h ∘ f). Say why the second one is the complex analyst's proof and the first works for Δu ≠ 0 too (Needham 12.2.2 picture: the Laplacian scales by the local magnification squared).
- **Worked examples.** (1) u = x³ − 3xy² → v = 3x²y − y³ by "conjugate by primitive", then recognise z³. (2) u = e^x cos y + xy: conjugate e^x sin y + (y² − x²)/2. (3) u = log|z| on the slit plane ℂ∖(−∞, 0]: conjugate Arg z, and why the slit matters.
- **Warning.** "Harmonic" is not "has zero second derivative in each direction separately": u = x² − y² has u_xx = 2. And a product of harmonic functions is usually not harmonic (u = x, x² is not).
- **End of section: the second hook** (holomorphic on the upper half-disc, Im f → 0 on the diameter; can f be continued?), stated, with "CA.5 answers this; the tool comes in §6."
- **Quick checks.** Is x² + y² harmonic? (No: Δ = 4.) Is xy harmonic, and what is its conjugate? (Yes; (y² − x²)/2.) Is u ∘ f harmonic for u(w) = log|w| and f(z) = z² on ℂ∖{0}? (Yes: it is 2 log|z|.)
- **Exercises (answers folded, all original).**
  - **A1.** Which of x² − y² + 3x, x³ + y³, e^{−y} cos x, log(x² + y²) are harmonic, and where? *Ans:* yes; no (Δ = 6x + 6y); yes; yes on ℂ∖{0}.
  - **A2.** For u = 2xy − y on ℂ find a conjugate by the "conjugate by primitive" move. *Ans:* g = 2y − i(2x − 1) = −2iz + i, primitive F = −iz² + iz with Re F = u, so v = y² − x² + x + c.
  - **B1.** Prove: if u and u² are harmonic on a connected open set, u is constant. *Ans:* Δ(u²) = 2|∇u|².
  - **B2.** Prove `thm-conjugate-unique` and deduce: if u and v are both harmonic and u + iv and u − iv are both holomorphic, then u and v are constant.
  - **B3.** Show u(z) = log|z − 1| − log|z + 1| has a conjugate on ℂ∖[−1, 1]. *Ans:* u = log|(z − 1)/(z + 1)|, and the Möbius map z ↦ (z − 1)/(z + 1) (CA.10) sends [−1, 1] onto [−∞, 0], hence ℂ∖[−1, 1] into ℂ∖(−∞, 0]; so v = Arg((z − 1)/(z + 1)) is a conjugate.
  - **C1.** Let A = {1 < |z| < 2}. Show every harmonic u on A can be written u = c log|z| + Re F with F holomorphic on A and c real; find c as a circle integral of the normal derivative. *Ans:* c = (1/2π)∫_0^{2π} r ∂u/∂r(re^{it}) dt (independent of r); subtract c log|z| to kill the period of g, then conjugate by primitive along the annulus.
  - **C2.** (proof verification, **true conclusion**) "*Claim: a harmonic u ≥ 0 on ℂ is constant. Proof: (1) ℂ is convex, so u has a conjugate v. (2) f = u + iv is entire with Re f ≥ 0. (3) Hence f takes values in a half-plane, so f is bounded. (4) By Liouville f is constant.*" Find the line. *Ans:* line (3). A set contained in a half-plane need not be bounded (counterexample to the step: the half-plane {Re w ≥ 0} itself). Repair: 1/(1 + f) is entire and bounded by 1, so it is constant, and so is f. The conclusion is true (`thm-harmonic-liouville`, §4).

## 02-mean-value-maximum.md — The mean value property and the maximum principle (budget ≤ 3,800 words)

- **Opening frame (spiral).** CA.2 proved Gauss's mean value theorem for holomorphic f and the maximum modulus principle. Taking real parts gives the harmonic versions at once; the new point is that the maximum principle needs **only** the averaging property, and that is worth isolating.
- `thm-harmonic-mvp`: u harmonic on an open set containing \(\overline{D}(a, r)\) ⇒ u(a) = (1/2π)∫_0^{2π} u(a + re^{it}) dt. Proof: u = Re f on a slightly larger disc (`thm-conjugate-disc`); take real parts in Gauss's theorem.
- `def-mean-value-property`: a **continuous** u on Ω has the (local) mean value property if every a ∈ Ω has some δ_a > 0 with u(a) equal to its circle averages for all 0 < r < δ_a. Motivate by the internal hook: this is the property that passes to uniform limits; Δu = 0 is not visibly so.
- `thm-strong-max-principle-mvp`: Ω connected, u continuous with the mean value property, u attains a maximum in Ω ⇒ u constant.
  - **Proof Idea only, as a repeat of CA.2 §7:** the set where u = M is closed (continuity) and open (if u(a) = M, the average of M − u ≥ 0 over each small circle is 0, so M − u ≡ 0 on that circle, by continuity of the integrand), hence everything. Then the proof.
- `thm-weak-max-principle-mvp`: Ω **bounded**, u continuous on \(\overline{\Omega}\) with the mean value property in Ω ⇒ max over \(\overline{\Omega}\) is attained on ∂Ω. And the min version.
- `cor-harmonic-max-principle`: both statements for harmonic functions (by `thm-harmonic-mvp`).
- **Weird examples, then the definition they force.**
  - `exm-unbounded-max-failure`: u = y on ℍ (0 on ∂ℍ, unbounded); u = e^x sin y = Im e^z on the strip 0 < Im z < π. Name the hypothesis that fails in each case: boundedness of Ω.
  - `exm-poisson-kernel-boundary-zero`: u(z) = (1 − |z|²)/|1 − z|² = Re((1 + z)/(1 − z)) on 𝔻. Positive, harmonic; for every ζ ∈ 𝕋∖{1}, u(z) → 0 as z → ζ. If "u = 0 on the boundary" meant "radial limits 0 except at finitely many points", uniqueness would fail (u and 0 are both solutions). The fix: demand continuity on the closed region.
  - `def-dirichlet-problem` (failure-motivated by the two examples): given Ω and g ∈ C(∂Ω), find u ∈ C(\(\overline{\Omega}\)) harmonic in Ω with u = g on ∂Ω.
- `cor-dirichlet-uniqueness`: on a bounded Ω the Dirichlet problem has at most one solution. Proof: the difference has max and min 0. One paragraph.
- **Worked example.** On D(0, 2) find max and min of u = x² − y² + x over the closed disc. By the maximum principle look only at the boundary: u(2cos t, 2sin t) = 8c² + 2c − 4 with c = cos t; max 6 at z = 2, min −33/8 where cos t = −1/8.
- **Quick check.** Can a non-constant harmonic function on 𝔻 have a local minimum? (No: minimum principle, applied on a small disc.) What does `thm-weak-max-principle-mvp` say for u = Re z on the closed square [0, 1]²?
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Compute (1/2π)∫_0^{2π} log|1 − re^{it}| dt for 0 ≤ r < 1. *Ans:* 0, since log|1 − z| is harmonic on 𝔻 and vanishes at 0.
  - **A2.** Give a continuous function on 𝔻 that is not harmonic but satisfies the mean value property at the single point 0 for every r < 1. *Ans:* u = x³: odd in x, so every circle average about 0 is 0 = u(0), while Δu = 6x ≢ 0. Smoothness is not the issue; the property must hold at every point.
  - **A3 (comprehension, `thm-strong-max-principle-mvp`).** Where is connectedness used? What is the conclusion on Ω = D(0,1) ∪ D(3,1) for u = 0 on one disc and 1 on the other?
  - **B1.** Prove the minimum principle from the maximum principle; deduce that a harmonic function on a bounded region, continuous on the closure and constant on ∂Ω, is constant.
  - **B2.** Let u be harmonic on 𝔻, continuous on \(\overline{\mathbb D}\), with u(e^{it}) = sin² t. Without solving, show 0 < u < 1 in 𝔻 and u(0) = ½.
  - **B3.** Show that the maximum principle fails on ℍ even for bounded boundary data, unless u is assumed bounded: give an unbounded harmonic u on ℍ, continuous on the closure, with u = 0 on ℝ. *Ans:* u = y; or Im z³ = 3x²y − y³, which also changes sign.
  - **C1.** Prove the maximum principle for **bounded** harmonic u on ℍ continuous on the closure with u ≤ 0 on ℝ: *Ans:* log|z + i| is harmonic on ℍ, ≥ 0 on the closure (|z + i| ≥ 1) and → ∞. If u ≤ M, then on the half-disc of radius R > 1 + e^{M/ε}, u − ε log|z + i| ≤ 0 on the segment and on the arc; weak principle; let R → ∞, then ε → 0. (Previews "log perturbation" without naming it; §6 names it.)

## 03-poisson-integral.md — Poisson's formula (budget ≤ 4,500 words)

- **Opening.** The mean value property recovers u(0) from the boundary circle. *Can every interior value be recovered from the boundary?* Yes, by moving the point to the centre (CA.10's automorphisms).
- **Scratch Work (first appearance of "move the point to the centre").**
  - φ_a(z) = (z + a)/(1 + āz) maps 𝔻 onto 𝔻, 𝕋 onto 𝕋, and 0 to a (cite CA.10). u ∘ φ_a is harmonic on a neighbourhood of \(\overline{\mathbb D}\) (`thm-harmonic-conformal-invariance`), so u(a) = (u ∘ φ_a)(0) = (1/2π)∫ u(φ_a(e^{is})) ds.
  - Change variables e^{it} = φ_a(e^{is}). Work out ds/dt as |(φ_a^{−1})′(e^{it})| = (1 − |a|²)/|e^{it} − a|², showing the computation of the derivative of the inverse map z ↦ (z − a)/(1 − āz) on 𝕋. This is the step readers cannot invent; do it slowly, with the geometric reading (Needham 12.7.3): the circle is stretched near the points close to a and compressed far from it.
- `def-poisson-kernel`: P(z, ζ) = (1 − |z|²)/|ζ − z|², and P_r(θ) = (1 − r²)/(1 − 2r cos θ + r²).
- `thm-poisson-formula`: u harmonic on a neighbourhood of \(\overline{\mathbb D}\) ⇒ u(z) = (1/2π)∫_0^{2π} P(z, e^{it}) u(e^{it}) dt for |z| < 1. Scaled version for \(\overline{D}(a, R)\) stated (substitute).
  - Proof (typeset, logical order) from the Scratch Work. **Key proof.** Self-explanation prompt in the Idea.
  - **Alternatively** (one paragraph, the CA.2-only route): apply the Cauchy formula to f = u + iv at z and observe that the integrand at the reflected point 1/z̄ integrates to 0 (Cauchy's theorem, since 1/z̄ is outside the closed disc); subtract and take real parts. This route uses no Möbius maps, and the constraint "derive Poisson from the Cauchy integral formula" is visible in both.
- `thm-schwarz-integral`: f holomorphic on a neighbourhood of \(\overline{\mathbb D}\), u = Re f ⇒ f(z) = (1/2π)∫ (e^{it} + z)/(e^{it} − z) u(e^{it}) dt + i Im f(0). Proof: the integral is holomorphic in z (CA.2, functions defined by integrals), has real part the Poisson integral = u, so differs from f by an imaginary constant; evaluate at 0. Note P(z, ζ) = Re((ζ + z)/(ζ − z)).
- `lem-poisson-kernel-properties`: (i) P > 0; (ii) (1/2π)∫ P(z, e^{it}) dt = 1 (apply the formula to u ≡ 1); (iii) for ζ₀ ∈ 𝕋 and δ > 0, sup{P(z, e^{it}) : |e^{it} − ζ₀| ≥ δ} → 0 as z → ζ₀; (iv) z ↦ P(z, ζ) is harmonic in 𝔻. Proofs short; (iii) by |e^{it} − z| ≥ δ/2 once |z − ζ₀| < δ/2.
- `def-poisson-integral`: for g ∈ C(𝕋) (later: bounded and Riemann integrable), P[g](z) = (1/2π)∫ P(z, e^{it}) g(e^{it}) dt.
- `rem-poisson-fourier` (the HA.2 look back, ≤ 150 words, prose, revisits edge): expanding (ζ + z)/(ζ − z) as a geometric series gives P_r(θ) = Σ_{n∈ℤ} r^{|n|} e^{inθ}, uniformly in θ for fixed r < 1 (AN1.9). So P[g] = Σ ĝ(n) r^{|n|} e^{inθ}: the Abel means of the Fourier series of g. Readers who did HA.2 recognise P_r as a good kernel; nothing here depends on it.
- **Worked example.** u = Re(z²) = x² − y² checked against the formula at z = ½ numerically (the drafter's sidecar computes the integral; the text shows the setup and the value ¼).
- **Warning.** The formula needs u harmonic on a neighbourhood of the **closed** disc (or continuous on the closed disc and harmonic inside, which §4 gives). For u = P(·, 1) itself the formula with its own "boundary values" (0 a.e.) gives 0 ≠ u: the kernel example of §2 again.
- **Quick checks.** P_r(0) and P_r(π) for r = ½ (3 and ⅓). Is P(z, ζ) bounded on 𝔻 for fixed ζ? (No; it is 0 nowhere but tends to ∞ as z → ζ radially.)
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1 (comprehension, `thm-poisson-formula`).** (a) Why does φ_a map 𝕋 onto 𝕋? (b) Run the proof for a = 0: what is ds/dt? (c) State the proof's idea in two sentences. *Ans:* (a) |e^{is} + a| = |e^{is}|·|1 + a e^{−is}| = |1 + ā e^{is}|; (b) ds/dt = 1 and P ≡ 1; (c) the mean value property gives the value at the centre; an automorphism of the disc makes any point the centre and turns the uniform average into a weighted one.
  - **A2.** Verify P_r(θ) = Re((1 + re^{iθ})/(1 − re^{iθ})) by direct computation.
  - **B1.** Use `thm-schwarz-integral` to find the holomorphic f on 𝔻 with Re f(e^{it}) = cos t + 2 sin 2t on 𝕋 and f(0) = 0. *Ans:* f(z) = z − 2i z².
  - **B2.** Prove the scaled formula on \(\overline{D}(a, R)\): u(a + re^{iθ}) = (1/2π)∫ (R² − r²)/(R² − 2Rr cos(θ − t) + r²) u(a + Re^{it}) dt.
  - **B3.** Derive the series of `rem-poisson-fourier` and check that its sum at r = ½, θ = 0 is 3.

## 04-dirichlet-problem-disc.md — The Dirichlet problem on the disc (budget ≤ 4,800 words)

- **Opening.** §3 *represented* a harmonic function by its boundary values. Now reverse: start with any continuous g on 𝕋. Is P[g] harmonic, and does it take the value g on 𝕋? This is the disc plate of the hook.
- `thm-schwarz-dirichlet-disc` (Ahlfors' "Schwarz's theorem"): for g ∈ C(𝕋), the function u = P[g] on 𝔻, u = g on 𝕋, is continuous on \(\overline{\mathbb D}\) and harmonic in 𝔻. **Key proof.**
  - Harmonic: u = Re of (1/2π)∫ (e^{it} + z)/(e^{it} − z) g(e^{it}) dt, holomorphic in z (CA.2). No smoothness of g is needed.
  - Continuity at ζ₀ ∈ 𝕋: **Scratch Work (first appearance of "near and far").** Write u(z) − g(ζ₀) = (1/2π)∫ P(z, e^{it})(g(e^{it}) − g(ζ₀)) dt using kernel property (ii). Choose δ from uniform continuity of g (AN2.3) so the near arc contributes < ε/2 using (i) and (ii); then choose how close z must be so the far arc contributes < ε/2 using (iii) and boundedness of g. Point out the order of choices: ε, then δ, then the neighbourhood of ζ₀; and that δ does not depend on ζ₀ (uniform continuity), which gives the uniform version for free. Also handle z ∈ 𝕋 near ζ₀ (trivial: continuity of g).
  - Proof (typeset). Self-explanation prompt in the Idea.
- `cor-dirichlet-disc`: the Dirichlet problem on any disc D(a, R) with continuous data has exactly one solution, P[g] scaled. (Existence: this theorem; uniqueness: `cor-dirichlet-uniqueness`.) Pays the disc plate of the hook. Also: a function continuous on \(\overline{D}\), harmonic inside, satisfies the Poisson formula (removes the "neighbourhood of the closed disc" hypothesis of §3).
- **Worked examples.** (1) g = cos t → u = x. (2) g = cos² t → u = (1 + x² − y²)/2. (3) g = |cos t|: no closed form needed; show u(0) = 2/π and the Fourier look back gives the series (optional, one line).
- `exm-step-data` (weird example): g = 1 on the upper semicircle, 0 on the lower (not continuous, but the integral makes sense). P[g](z) = ½ + (1/π) arctan(2y/(1 − |z|²)) = ½ + (1/π) Arg((1 + z)/(1 − z)). Harmonic; boundary values 1 and 0 off ±1; at z → 1 along the curve 2y = c(1 − |z|²) the limit is ½ + (1/π) arctan c, every value in (0, 1). Picture: the level curves are circular arcs through ±1 (Möbius, CA.10).
- `thm-harmonic-liouville`: a harmonic function on ℂ bounded above (or below) is constant. Proof: conjugate on ℂ, then exp of f (or 1/(1 + f) after a sign) and Liouville. This pays §1's C2.
- **Quick checks.** What is P[g](0) in general? (The mean of g.) If g ≥ 0 and g ≢ 0, why is P[g] > 0 in 𝔻? (Kernel positive, g positive on an arc.)
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1 (comprehension, `thm-schwarz-dirichlet-disc`).** (a) Which kernel property is used on the near arc, and which on the far arc? (b) Where is uniform continuity used, and what would go wrong with mere continuity at ζ₀? (c) Run the proof for g ≡ c. *Ans:* (a) positivity and total mass 1 near; concentration far; (b) continuity at ζ₀ alone suffices for the limit at ζ₀ (that is B2); uniform continuity makes δ independent of ζ₀ and gives P[g](rζ) → g(ζ) uniformly in ζ; (c) u ≡ c, both pieces 0.
  - **A2.** Solve the Dirichlet problem on 𝔻 with g(e^{it}) = sin 3t. *Ans:* Im(z³) = 3x²y − y³.
  - **B1 (`ex-poisson-kernel-bounds`; CA.11 cites it).** For |z| = r < 1 and ζ ∈ 𝕋 prove (1 − r)/(1 + r) ≤ P(z, ζ) ≤ (1 + r)/(1 − r). Deduce: if g ≥ 0, then ((1 − r)/(1 + r)) P[g](0) ≤ P[g](z) ≤ ((1 + r)/(1 − r)) P[g](0). Do **not** name the inequality (it is Harnack's, named in CA.11).
  - **B2 (faded "near and far", last step blank).** g is bounded and Riemann integrable on 𝕋 and continuous at ζ₀. The near-arc estimate is written out; "*Name the move, then complete it*" for the far arc. *Ans:* P[g](z) → g(ζ₀) as z → ζ₀.
  - **B3.** Solve the Dirichlet problem on D(1, 2) with data g(z) = Re(z)² on the circle. *Ans:* transplant to 𝔻 by z = 1 + 2w; data (1 + 2cos t)² = 1 + 4cos t + 2 + 2cos 2t; u = 3 + 4 Re w + 2 Re w², back-substitute w = (z − 1)/2: u = 3 + 2(x − 1) + ((x − 1)² − y²)/2.
  - **C1.** Let g ∈ C(𝕋) and u = P[g]. Prove that sup_𝔻 |u| ≤ max_𝕋 |g|, and that u_n = P[g_n] → P[g] uniformly on \(\overline{\mathbb D}\) when g_n → g uniformly on 𝕋.
  - **C2.** Show that uniqueness in `cor-dirichlet-disc` fails if "continuous on the closed disc" is weakened to "radial limit g(ζ) at every ζ ∈ 𝕋 except one". *Ans:* g = 0: both 0 and P(·, 1) qualify. For **bounded** u the uniqueness survives; that is §6 C2.

## 05-transplanting.md — Transplanting: the half-plane and the strip (budget ≤ 3,500 words)

- **Opening.** The hook's second plate is a half-plane, not a disc. A conformal map carries harmonic functions to harmonic functions (§1) and boundaries to boundaries; so a Dirichlet problem moves with it.
- `thm-dirichlet-transplant`: if F: \(\overline{U}\) → \(\overline{\mathbb D}\) is a homeomorphism, holomorphic from U onto 𝔻, then u solves the Dirichlet problem on U with data g iff u ∘ F^{−1} solves it on 𝔻 with data g ∘ F^{−1}. One paragraph; the content is §1 plus continuity.
- **The half-plane** is not bounded, and the Cayley map C(z) = (z − i)/(z + i) (CA.10) extends to the boundary only if ∞ is included. State honestly: for g continuous on ℝ with a finite limit at ±∞ (so g ∘ C^{−1} is continuous on 𝕋), the transplant works.
- `thm-poisson-half-plane`: for such g, u(x + iy) = (1/π)∫_ℝ y/((x − t)² + y²) g(t) dt is harmonic on ℍ, bounded, and tends to g(t₀) at each t₀ ∈ ℝ. Proof: change variables e^{is} = C(t) in the disc formula; ds = 2 dt/(1 + t²); P(C(z), C(t)) · 2/(1 + t²) = 2y/((x − t)² + y²). Say what the transplant computation reuses ("move the point to the centre" is now "move the region to the disc").
- `exm-half-plane-nonuniqueness` (weird example, paired with §2's): u = y and u = y/|z|² = −Im(1/z) are positive harmonic on ℍ with zero boundary values on ℝ∖{0}; the first is continuous on the closure with g = 0, so even with continuous data the problem on ℍ has many solutions unless u is required to be bounded. Uniqueness among **bounded** solutions: by transplanting, a bounded solution on ℍ gives a bounded solution on 𝔻 continuous except at one boundary point; that uniqueness is proved in §6 C2 (log perturbation at a boundary point). §5 claims existence only and says in prose that §6 C2 supplies uniqueness; no forward `@ref` is needed for any proof here.
- **Worked example: the hook's half-plane plate.** g = 1 on (−1, 1), 0 elsewhere on ℝ: u = (1/π)(Arg(z − 1) − Arg(z + 1)), the angle subtended by the segment, divided by π. Level curves: circular arcs through ±1. Ties to `exm-step-data` by the Cayley map.
- `exm-dirichlet-strip` (S&S 8.1.3 coverage, original data): the strip S = {0 < Im z < π}, data 0 on Im z = 0 and 1 on Im z = π. Solution u = y/π. Then the same strip with data 1 on {Im z = 0, x < 0} and 0 on the rest of the boundary: e^z carries S to ℍ and the data to 1 on (0, 1), 0 elsewhere, so u = (1/π)(Arg(e^z − 1) − y). Check the three boundary pieces in the text.
- **Remark (look ahead).** Every simply connected region other than ℂ is conformally the disc (CA.5). Turning that into a solution of the Dirichlet problem needs the map to extend to the boundary, which this volume does not prove; CA.5 records it as a remark for regions where it does. General regions: CA.11.
- **Quick checks.** Under the Cayley map, which point of 𝕋 corresponds to ∞? (1.) What is the solution on ℍ with g ≡ c? (u ≡ c, kernel mass 1.)
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Verify by differentiation that y/((x − t)² + y²) is harmonic in (x, y) on ℍ, and identify it as the imaginary part of a holomorphic function. *Ans:* −Im(1/(z − t)).
  - **A2.** Find the bounded harmonic u on ℍ with boundary values g(t) = 1 for t > 0, 0 for t < 0 (two-valued data, accept the discontinuity at 0). *Ans:* 1 − Arg(z)/π.
  - **B1.** Solve the Dirichlet problem on the quadrant {x > 0, y > 0} with u = 0 on the positive real axis and u = 1 on the positive imaginary axis (bounded solution). *Ans:* transplant by z² to ℍ: u = (2/π) Arg z.
  - **B2 (faded "near and far", last half blank).** Prove directly (without transplanting) that the half-plane Poisson integral of a bounded continuous g tends to g(t₀) as z → t₀: kernel properties stated, the whole estimate left. *Ans:* as in §4 with the far set {|t − t₀| ≥ δ}, where the kernel mass tends to 0.
  - **C1.** Find the half-plane Poisson integral of g(t) = t/(1 + t²) in closed form. *Ans:* g(t) = Re(1/(t + i)), and 1/(z + i) is holomorphic and bounded on ℍ (|z + i| ≥ 1), so by uniqueness of bounded solutions u = Re(1/(z + i)) = x/(x² + (y + 1)²). (Choosing 1/(z − i) instead fails: pole at i ∈ ℍ.)
  - **C2.** For the annulus {1 < |z| < 2} with data 0 on the inner and 1 on the outer circle, find a solution (log|z|/log 2). Why does transplanting to the disc not apply? (An annulus is not conformally a disc: it is not simply connected; a conformal homeomorphism would preserve that, CA.4.) Pointer: general regions in CA.11.

## 06-mean-value-characterization.md — The mean value property characterizes harmonic functions (budget ≤ 4,500 words)

- **Opening.** Pay the internal hook: in §2 the maximum principle used nothing but averaging. Now the converse.
- `thm-mvp-implies-harmonic` (Ahlfors 6.3.1): a continuous u on Ω with the mean value property is harmonic. **Key proof.**
  - Proof Idea: on a closed disc in Ω, compare u with the Poisson integral h of u's values on the circle (§4). w = u − h is continuous on the closed disc, has the mean value property inside, is 0 on the circle; the maximum principle gives w ≤ 0 and the minimum principle gives w ≥ 0. Self-explanation prompt.
  - Proof (typeset).
- `cor-harmonic-uniform-limit`: a locally uniform limit of harmonic functions is harmonic (averages pass to the limit; continuity of the limit). Contrast in a remark: for real-differentiable functions the analogous statement fails badly (AN2's Weierstrass-type examples, prose pointer); harmonic functions behave like holomorphic ones (CA.2's Weierstrass theorem).
- **Remark: what CA.5 will use.** A continuous function on a region symmetric in ℝ, harmonic off ℝ, with u(z̄) = −u(z), has the mean value property on small circles centred on ℝ (the two halves cancel), hence is harmonic. Stated **without proof** and without the holomorphic conclusion: "CA.5 completes this into the reflection principle." This pays the setup of §1's second hook. *(It is a remark, not a labelled result, so that the reflection principle has exactly one home, CA.5.)*
- `thm-harmonic-removable-singularity`: u harmonic and bounded on 0 < |z − a| < ρ ⇒ u extends harmonically to a.
  - **Scratch Work (first appearance of "log perturbation").** Let h = Poisson integral on |z − a| = r of u, v = u − h, bounded on the punctured disc, 0 on the circle. For ε > 0, v + ε log(|z − a|/r) is harmonic on the punctured disc, ≤ 0 on the circle, and → −∞ at a; so on every annulus δ ≤ |z − a| ≤ r it is ≤ 0 on both boundary circles once δ is small (boundedness of v). Weak maximum principle on the annulus; let δ → 0, then ε → 0: v ≤ 0. Same for −v. So u = h off a; define u(a) = h(a).
  - Proof (typeset).
- `exm-punctured-disc-dirichlet` (pays the hook's third plate): on Ω = 𝔻∖{0}, ∂Ω = 𝕋 ∪ {0}, data 0 on 𝕋 and 1 at 0. A solution would be bounded and harmonic on Ω, so it extends harmonically to 𝔻 (theorem above), equals 0 on 𝕋, hence is 0 everywhere (maximum principle), contradicting u(z) → 1 as z → 0. The isolated boundary point is invisible to harmonic functions. One sentence: CA.11 names such a point **irregular**.
- **Close of the chapter.** The plate questions now stand: one solution on bounded regions (§2); existence on discs (§4), regions transplantable to discs (§5); failure on the punctured disc (§6); general regions in CA.11. The tool for CA.5's reflection is `thm-mvp-implies-harmonic`.
- **Quick checks.** Is the uniform limit on 𝔻 of the harmonic polynomials Re(Σ_{k ≤ n} z^k/k) harmonic, and what is it? (Yes: −log|1 − z|, on compact subsets.) Does `thm-harmonic-removable-singularity` hold for u = log|z|? (No: unbounded; the hypothesis matters.)
- **Exercises.**

  - **A2.** Give a continuous function on ℝ² with the mean value property on circles centred on the x-axis but not harmonic. *Ans:* any function odd in y that is not harmonic, e.g. y³; the remark above needs harmonicity off ℝ as a hypothesis.
