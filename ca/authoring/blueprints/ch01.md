**Purpose.**
- On ℝ, one derivative says little. A function can be differentiable once and not twice, and C^∞ without being a power series (e^{−1/x²}, AN1.10). This chapter asks what changes when the limit of the difference quotient must exist **as h → 0 through ℂ**, from every direction at once.
- **First answer (§§01–02).** The derivative must be multiplication by a complex number: a rotation followed by a dilation. That is the Cauchy–Riemann system, and geometrically it is conformality.
- **Second answer (§03).** Power series are holomorphic, and infinitely differentiable, in their disc of convergence. The converse, "holomorphic ⇒ locally a power series", is the chapter hook. CA.2 pays it.
- **The elementary functions (§§04–05).** exp, sin and cos on ℂ, and the logarithm. Since exp is not injective, the logarithm has to be chosen. "Branch" is introduced as what survives that failure, and no branch exists on ℂ ∖ {0}.
- **Contour integrals (§06).** The ML-inequality, and the fact that a primitive makes closed-curve integrals vanish. Then ∮_{|z|=1} dz/z = 2πi: the integral detects the hole. This is the second hook, paid in CA.2 (Cauchy's theorem) and CA.4 (winding numbers).

## Named moves (index.md)

1. **Test two directions.** To refute complex differentiability at z₀, let h → 0 along ℝ and along iℝ (or along any two rays). If the two limits of the difference quotient differ, there is no derivative. This is the chapter's **one new move** and gets the faded triple (§01 example, §01 B2, §02 C1).
2. **Read the derivative as a number.** An ℝ-linear map ℂ → ℂ is ℂ-linear exactly when its matrix has the form [[a, −b], [b, a]]. Then it is multiplication by a + ib, a rotation-dilation (`lem-complex-linear`).
3. **Split the series at N.** For a power series inside its disc, choose r < R, then an N independent of h, and estimate the tail uniformly and the head as a polynomial (`thm-power-series-holomorphic`). It is AN1.9's ε/3 argument in a new place.
4. **Differentiate and use connectedness.** Two holomorphic functions on a region with the same derivative and one common value are equal. Proved in `thm-derivative-zero-constant`, used for the addition law of exp, for Log(1+z) = Σ(−1)^{n+1}zⁿ/n, and for "branches differ by 2πik".
5. **Rotate to make it real.** To bound |w| for w = ∫ g, multiply by e^{−iθ} with θ = arg w, so that e^{−iθ}w = |w| is real (`lem-complex-integral-modulus`).

## Hooks and where they are paid

- **Paid here, from AN1.10 (§03, `exm-arctan-radius-explained`).** AN1.10: 1/(1 + x²) is C^∞ on ℝ, yet its Taylor series at 0 has radius 1. Nothing visible on ℝ explains this. CA.1: the series Σ(−1)ⁿz^{2n} sums to 1/(1 + z²) on 𝔻. If its radius exceeded 1, its sum would be continuous at i (`thm-power-series-holomorphic`), but 1/(1 + (ti)²) = 1/(1 − t²) → ∞ as t → 1⁻. The obstruction sits at ±i, off the real line.
- **Opened here: the chapter hook (§01, "one derivative or all?").** On ℝ, e^{−1/x²} is C^∞ with Taylor series 0. In ℂ, e^{−1/z²} is not even bounded near 0: on the imaginary axis it equals e^{1/y²} (`exm-flat-function-complex`, §03). §03 proves one direction: power series are infinitely complex-differentiable. The converse, that every holomorphic function is locally a power series and hence infinitely differentiable, is **paid in CA.2** (Cauchy integral formula; CA.2's "holomorphic ⇒ analytic" theorem, expected label thm-holomorphic-analytic†). §03 states it as an open question in a boxed remark, with no forward `@ref`.
- **Opened here: "the radius is the distance to the nearest singularity" (§03, end).** One sentence: the 1/(1 + z²) example suggests it. **Paid in CA.2** (Taylor expansion in the largest disc; CA.2's own hook on the radius of x/(eˣ − 1)).
- **Opened here: "∮ dz/z counts turns" (§06, `exm-integral-power-circle`, `exm-multiple-circle`).** ∮_{C(0,1)} dz/z = 2πi, and over C_k(0, 1) it is 2πik. **Paid in CA.2** (Cauchy's theorem: on a disc where f is holomorphic, ∮ = 0, so the 2πi comes from the hole) and **in CA.4** (winding number n(γ, a) = (1/2πi)∮ dz/(z − a)). §06 C2 computes ∮_{C(0,1)} dz/(z − a) for |a| ≠ 1 by series, as a preview.
- **Opened here: Liouville (§04 remark, §06 C1).** sin is bounded on ℝ but unbounded on ℂ. §06 C1 proves that a power series with R = ∞ bounded on ℂ is constant (Cauchy estimates for power series). The general theorem for entire functions is **CA.2**.

## 01-complex-differentiability.md — Complex differentiability on open sets (target 3,000 words)

- **Hook (opening frame).** e^{−1/x²} versus e^{−1/z²}: state both facts, prove neither yet (§03 does the complex one). Then ask the chapter question.
- **Recall from AN1.7, with no re-proof** (prose pointer, Apostol 5.15):
  - the complex derivative f′(z₀) = lim_{h→0, h∈ℂ∖{0}} (f(z₀ + h) − f(z₀))/h;
  - differentiable ⇒ continuous;
  - the sum, product, quotient and chain rules.

  One paragraph says what is *the same* as on ℝ: the proofs, because they use only field operations and |·|. One paragraph says what is *new*: h ranges over a disc of directions, so there is no mean value theorem (R3 in §06 shows why) and no "f′ > 0".
- **`lem-caratheodory-form`.** f is complex differentiable at z₀ iff there is φ: Ω → ℂ, continuous at z₀, with f(z) − f(z₀) = φ(z)(z − z₀) for z ∈ Ω. Then f′(z₀) = φ(z₀).
  - Proof: φ(z) = the difference quotient for z ≠ z₀, and φ(z₀) = f′(z₀).
  - Its use: the chain rule along curves below, and the inverse-function lemma of §05, both without ε-δ. One clause says AN1.7's chain rule follows in two lines from it (C1).
- **Failure-motivated definition.** "Differentiable at a point" is too weak to build on: |z|² is complex differentiable at 0 and nowhere else (proved below). Nothing can be done with a derivative at a single point. Demand it on an open set:
  - `def-holomorphic`: f: Ω → ℂ is *holomorphic on Ω* if it is complex differentiable at every point of the open set Ω. f is *holomorphic at z₀* if it is holomorphic on some disc D(z₀, r). An *entire* function is one holomorphic on ℂ.
  - `def-region`: a non-empty connected open subset of ℂ.
  - **Remark (conventions).** Apostol (16.1) calls f *analytic* when f′ exists and is continuous on Ω. Ahlfors uses "analytic" for holomorphic. This book uses **holomorphic** for complex differentiable on an open set, with no continuity of f′ assumed. "Analytic" means locally a power series (`def-analytic`, §03). That the three notions agree is a theorem of CA.2.
- **Examples** (`exm-rational-holomorphic`):
  - polynomials are entire, with (zⁿ)′ = nzⁿ⁻¹ by the product rule and induction;
  - a rational function p/q is holomorphic on {q ≠ 0};
  - z ↦ 1/z on ℂ ∖ {0}.
- **Worked example, the faded triple in full (named move 1)** (`exm-conjugate-nowhere`): f(z) = z̄ is nowhere complex differentiable, although as a map ℝ² → ℝ² it is linear.
  - The difference quotient is h̄/h. For h = t real it is 1; for h = it it is −1. Write h = ρe^{iθ}: then h̄/h = e^{−2iθ}, which depends only on the direction.
  - Name the move: **test two directions**.
- **Further non-examples** (`exm-modulus-squared`): |z|² is complex differentiable at 0, with derivative 0, and at no other point. At 0 the quotient is h̄ → 0. At z₀ ≠ 0 it is z̄₀ + h̄ + z₀h̄/h, and the last term depends on direction (B2 is the faded step). Re z and Im z are nowhere complex differentiable (A2).
- **`lem-chain-rule-curve`.** Let γ: (α, β) → Ω be differentiable at t₀ (as a map into ℂ = ℝ², componentwise), and f complex differentiable at γ(t₀). Then f ∘ γ is differentiable at t₀ and (f ∘ γ)′(t₀) = f′(γ(t₀)) γ′(t₀), a product of complex numbers.
  - Proof by `lem-caratheodory-form`: f(γ(t)) − f(γ(t₀)) = φ(γ(t))(γ(t) − γ(t₀)). Divide by t − t₀ and use the continuity of φ ∘ γ at t₀.
  - Its use: conformality (§02), `thm-derivative-zero-constant` (§02), `thm-primitive-closed-curve` (§06).
- **Quick check.** Where is f(z) = z̄² complex differentiable? *Answer:* only at 0, with f′(0) = 0. The quotient is (2z̄₀h̄ + h̄²)/h. At z₀ = 0 this is h̄²/h → 0; elsewhere 2z̄₀h̄/h depends on direction.
- **Warning.** "f′(z₀) exists" at one point says almost nothing about f near z₀: |z|². Holomorphy is a property of an **open set**. Do not write "f is holomorphic at z₀" for a function differentiable only at z₀.
- **Exercises.**

## 02-cauchy-riemann.md — Real and complex differentiability; the Cauchy–Riemann equations; conformality (target 4,600 words)

- **Opening frame.** AN1.7 showed that if f′(z₀) exists, the four partial derivatives exist at z₀ and u_x = v_y, u_y = −v_x (Apostol 5.22). It did not answer the converse, and z⁵/|z|⁴ (below) shows that the equations alone are not enough. The right comparison is with **real** differentiability of f as a map ℝ² → ℝ², which this section defines in place (constraint CA.1).
- **In-place definitions** (chapter constraint: point to AN3.1 for ℝⁿ, in prose):
  - `def-frechet-local`: identify ℂ with ℝ². f: Ω → ℂ is *real differentiable* at z₀ if there is an ℝ-linear T: ℝ² → ℝ² with |f(z₀ + h) − f(z₀) − T(h)|/|h| → 0 as h → 0. Then T is unique, and Df(z₀) := T. Uniqueness: two such T agree on every h = tv as t → 0⁺.
  - `def-partial-local`: the partial derivatives u_x, u_y, v_x, v_y and the Jacobian matrix J_f(z₀) = [[u_x, u_y], [v_x, v_y]]. Write f_x = u_x + iv_x and f_y = u_y + iv_y.
  - `prp-frechet-partials`: if f is real differentiable at z₀, the four partials exist and Df(z₀) has matrix J_f(z₀). Proof: take h = t, then h = it, in the definition.
- **`lem-complex-linear`** (named move 2). For an ℝ-linear T: ℂ → ℂ, the following are equivalent:
  - (i) T(λh) = λT(h) for all λ, h ∈ ℂ;
  - (ii) T(i) = iT(1);
  - (iii) T(h) = ch for some c ∈ ℂ, namely c = T(1);
  - (iv) T's matrix is [[a, −b], [b, a]], with c = a + ib.

  If c ≠ 0, T is rotation by arg c followed by dilation by |c| (prose pointer to F4.10: rotation by θ is multiplication by e^{iθ}).
  - Proof: (iii) ⇒ (i) ⇒ (ii) are clear. (ii) ⇒ (iii): T(x + iy) = xT(1) + yT(i) = (x + iy)T(1). (iii) ⇔ (iv): write out c(x + iy) in coordinates.
  - Example: h ↦ h̄ has matrix [[1, 0], [0, −1]], not of form (iv). This is §01's failure, read off the matrix.
- ***Try it first (ten minutes):*** *Suppose f′(z₀) exists. Let h → 0 first through real values, then through purely imaginary values, and compare.*
- **`thm-cauchy-riemann`** (key proof 1). Let f = u + iv: Ω → ℂ and z₀ ∈ Ω. Then f is complex differentiable at z₀ **iff** f is real differentiable at z₀ and u_x = v_y, u_y = −v_x at z₀. In that case f′(z₀) = u_x + iv_x = v_y − iu_y = f_x(z₀) = −if_y(z₀).
  - Idea: complex differentiability says that the best linear approximation is multiplication by f′(z₀). By `lem-complex-linear`, this means the real derivative exists and has the matrix [[a, −b], [b, a]], which is exactly CR.
  - Proof. (⇒): T(h) := f′(z₀)h is ℝ-linear, and the remainder |f(z₀ + h) − f(z₀) − f′(z₀)h|/|h| → 0 is the definition of f′(z₀) rearranged. So f is real differentiable, and `lem-complex-linear` (iv) gives CR. (⇐): Df(z₀) has matrix J_f(z₀), which has form (iv) by CR. So Df(z₀)h = ch with c = u_x + iv_x. Divide the remainder by h, using |·/h| = |·|/|h|.
  - Aftermath: the formula f′ = f_x = −if_y. Say that this both revisits and completes AN1.7's necessity statement.
- **`thm-cr-sufficient`.** Suppose u and v have first partial derivatives on a disc D(z₀, ρ) ⊆ Ω, all four continuous at z₀, and satisfying CR at z₀. Then f is complex differentiable at z₀. **Corollary** `cor-cr-holomorphic`: if the partials exist and are continuous on Ω and satisfy CR throughout Ω, then f is holomorphic on Ω.
  - **Scratch Work (technique 1, first appearance).** The goal is u(x₀ + s, y₀ + t) − u(x₀, y₀) = u_x(z₀)s + u_y(z₀)t + o(|h|). Walk an L-shaped path: first change x with y = y₀ + t fixed, then change y with x = x₀. On each leg the one-variable MVT gives the partial at an intermediate point. Continuity of the partials **at z₀** turns "intermediate point" into "z₀ plus an error that → 0". Do the same for v. Then CR assembles u_x s + u_y t + i(v_x s + v_y t) into (u_x + iv_x)(s + it). Why a neighbourhood is needed: the MVT needs the partials along the whole leg, not just at z₀.
  - Proof (typeset, forward): as in the scratch work. Then combine with `thm-cauchy-riemann` (⇐), or finish directly. Point to AN3.1, in prose, for the ℝⁿ version ("continuous partials ⇒ differentiable").
- **Weird examples: the hypotheses are needed** (rows of `tab-holomorphic-weird`).
  - `exm-cr-at-point-not-differentiable`: f(z) = z⁵/|z|⁴ for z ≠ 0, f(0) = 0.
    - f is continuous: |f(z)| = |z|.
    - CR hold at 0: on ℝ, f(x) = x, and on iℝ, f(iy) = iy. So u_x = 1, v_x = 0, u_y = 0, v_y = 1 at 0.
    - f is not complex differentiable at 0, because f(h)/h = (h/|h|)⁴ = e^{4iθ}.
    - The partials are not continuous at 0. That is the hypothesis of `thm-cr-sufficient` that fails, and C1 (faded step 3) asks the reader to find the direction.
  - `exm-cr-everywhere-discontinuous`: f(z) = e^{−1/z⁴} for z ≠ 0, f(0) = 0.
    - Off 0, f is holomorphic by the chain rule, once exp is known to be entire (§04 proves this; place the example after a forward sentence, or move it into §04's exercises. Prefer stating it here with "exp is entire, §04" as a promise paid two sections later).
    - At 0: on ℝ, f = e^{−1/x⁴}, and on iℝ, z⁴ = y⁴, so all four partials at 0 are 0 and CR hold.
    - Along z = te^{iπ/4}, z⁴ = −t⁴ and f = e^{1/t⁴} → ∞. So CR hold at every point of ℂ and f is not even continuous at 0.
    - Moral: CR plus existence of the partials, without real differentiability, is not enough. A remark says, in one sentence and without proof or name-dropping beyond the statement, that "continuous + partials everywhere + CR everywhere ⇒ holomorphic" is true but much harder. Point to no chapter.
- **Wirtinger notation (one subsection, notation only).** Define ∂f/∂z̄ = ½(f_x + if_y). Then for real differentiable f, CR at z₀ ⇔ ∂f/∂z̄(z₀) = 0, and then f′(z₀) = ∂f/∂z(z₀). Examples: ∂z̄/∂z̄ = 1 and ∂|z|²/∂z̄ = z. Prose: this is the form in which CA.2's Green's-theorem motivation is written.
- **Conformality.**
  - **Lead-in, failure first.** A general real differentiable map distorts angles: (x, y) ↦ (x, 2y) changes the angle between the directions 1 and 1 + i. Which maps preserve them?
  - `def-conformal`: for a curve γ through z₀ = γ(t₀) with γ′(t₀) ≠ 0, the tangent direction is γ′(t₀). f is *conformal at z₀* if f is real differentiable at z₀ and, for every pair of C¹ curves γ₁, γ₂ through z₀ with nonzero tangents, f ∘ γ₁ and f ∘ γ₂ have nonzero tangents and the oriented angle from (f ∘ γ₁)′ to (f ∘ γ₂)′ equals the oriented angle from γ₁′ to γ₂′. The oriented angle from v to w is arg(w/v) mod 2π (F4.10, prose pointer).
  - **`thm-holomorphic-conformal`.** If f is complex differentiable at z₀ and f′(z₀) ≠ 0, then f is conformal at z₀. Every tangent vector is turned through arg f′(z₀) and stretched by |f′(z₀)|.
    - Proof: `lem-chain-rule-curve` gives (f ∘ γ_k)′ = f′(z₀)γ_k′, and the ratio (f′γ₂′)/(f′γ₁′) = γ₂′/γ₁′.
    - Picture (Needham's amplitwist, described in our own words and figure): an infinitesimal square goes to an infinitesimal square.
  - **`prp-angle-preserving-is-complex-linear`.** An invertible ℝ-linear T: ℂ → ℂ that preserves oriented angles between all pairs of nonzero vectors is h ↦ ch with c ≠ 0. Proof sketch (C3): T(1) = c. The angle from 1 to i is π/2, so T(i) = λic with λ > 0. The angle from 1 to 1 + i is π/4, which forces λ = 1. Then (ii) of `lem-complex-linear`. **Corollary** (one line): a real differentiable f with Df(z₀) invertible and conformal at z₀ satisfies CR at z₀.
  - **Critical points.** At z₀ = 0, f(z) = z² sends the rays at angles θ₁, θ₂ to rays at 2θ₁, 2θ₂, so angles are doubled; zᵏ multiplies them by k. Prose: near a zero of f′ of order k − 1, every holomorphic function behaves this way. CA.4 proves it (local mapping theorem; prose pointer to CA.4, no `@ref`).
  - Exponential grid picture: postponed to §04, once exp is in hand.
- **`thm-derivative-zero-constant`** (named move 4, regions). If f is holomorphic on a region Ω and f′ ≡ 0, then f is constant.
  - Proof: fix z₀ and let A = {z ∈ Ω : f(z) = f(z₀)}. A is non-empty, and closed in Ω by continuity.
  - A is open: for z₁ ∈ A and D(z₁, r) ⊆ Ω, take z ∈ D(z₁, r) and g(t) = f(z₁ + t(z − z₁)), t ∈ [0, 1]. By `lem-chain-rule-curve`, g′(t) = f′(…)(z − z₁) = 0. Apply the MVT (AN1.7) to Re g and Im g, so g(1) = g(0).
  - Conclude by connectedness (AN2.3: no non-trivial clopen subset).
  - Remark: AN1.7 did this on a disc (Apostol 5.23). Name what was added: the clopen argument, which carries "locally constant" to "constant" on any region. §06 gives a second proof by curves.
- **`thm-constant-modulus`.** If f is holomorphic on a region Ω and Re f, Im f or |f| is constant on Ω, then f is constant.
  - Proof route: on each disc in Ω this is AN1.7's disc result (Apostol 5.23, recalled), so f′ = 0 on every disc, hence on Ω. Conclude by `thm-derivative-zero-constant`.
  - Self-contained route for |f| (exit check C2): differentiate u² + v² = c and use CR to get the system [[u, −v], [v, u]](u_x, u_y)ᵀ = 0, whose determinant u² + v² = c is non-zero when c ≠ 0.
  - Name the hypothesis "region": on two disjoint discs, with f = 1 on one and f = 2 on the other, the modulus is not constant, but f is locally constant and not constant. Use this as the Warning.
- **`rem-harmonic-parts`** (remark, no proof dependence). If u and v are C², CR give Δu = Δv = 0, using equality of mixed partials (prose pointer to AN3.1). CA.2 shows that C² is automatic, and CA.6 builds on this. One paragraph only.

## 03-power-series.md — Power series are holomorphic (target 4,200 words)

- **Opening frame.** Recall AN1.10 (prose pointer): Σaₙ(z − a)ⁿ has radius R = 1/lim sup|aₙ|^{1/n}, converges absolutely on D(a, R), diverges outside D̄(a, R), and converges uniformly on each D̄(a, r) with r < R (M-test). AN1.10 differentiated termwise **on the real interval**. That is not enough here, because h must be allowed to approach from every direction in ℂ.
- **`def-analytic`.** f is *analytic* on Ω if each z₀ ∈ Ω has a disc D(z₀, r) ⊆ Ω on which f is the sum of a convergent power series centred at z₀. Remark: that the sum of a power series is analytic on its whole disc (re-centring) is proved in CA.2 by Cauchy's formula. A direct proof uses double series (AN2.12), which this chapter does not assume.
- **`lem-derived-series-radius`.** Σnaₙ(z − a)^{n−1} has the same radius as Σaₙ(z − a)ⁿ. Proof: n^{1/n} → 1 (AN1.2), and multiplying by z − a does not change convergence.
- **`thm-power-series-holomorphic`** (key proof 2; named move 3). If f(z) = Σaₙ(z − a)ⁿ has radius R > 0 (R = ∞ allowed), then f is holomorphic on D(a, R) and f′(z) = Σnaₙ(z − a)^{n−1} there.
  - **Scratch Work (technique 2, first appearance).**
    - The obstacle: the difference quotient of a series is a series of difference quotients, and we must pass h → 0 through an infinite sum.
    - The tool: the telescoping bound |uⁿ − wⁿ| ≤ n r^{n−1}|u − w| for |u|, |w| ≤ r (from uⁿ − wⁿ = (u − w)Σu^k w^{n−1−k}). It gives the uniform bound |((w + h)ⁿ − wⁿ)/h − nw^{n−1}| ≤ 2n r^{n−1} once |w|, |w + h| < r.
    - So: pick r with |z − a| < r < R (room inside the disc). Pick N so that the tail Σ_{n>N} 2n|aₙ|r^{n−1} < ε/3, which is possible by `lem-derived-series-radius` and absolute convergence at r. **N must not depend on h.** Then the head is a polynomial, whose quotient converges, so choose δ for it.
    - Also bound the tail of the derived series at z by ε/3. Total: < ε.
  - Proof (typeset, forward, three displayed estimates).
  - Aftermath: (i) this answers the question AN1.10 could not ask; (ii) the same proof works for every derivative, so:
- **`cor-power-series-smooth`.** f is infinitely complex differentiable on D(a, R), with f^{(k)}(z) = Σ_{n≥k} n(n − 1)⋯(n − k + 1)aₙ(z − a)^{n−k}.
- **`cor-power-series-coefficients`.** aₖ = f^{(k)}(a)/k!. Consequently two power series centred at a that agree on some D(a, ρ), ρ > 0, have the same coefficients.
- **`prp-power-series-isolated-zeros`.** If f(z) = Σaₙ(z − a)ⁿ on D(a, R) and f(z_j) = 0 for a sequence z_j → a with z_j ≠ a, then every aₙ = 0.
  - Proof: if not, let m be least with a_m ≠ 0. Then f(z) = (z − a)^m g(z) with g(z) = Σ_{n≥m}aₙ(z − a)^{n−m}, which is continuous at a by the theorem (or by uniform convergence) with g(a) = a_m ≠ 0. So g ≠ 0 near a, while f(z_j) = 0 forces g(z_j) = 0.
  - Prose: CA.2 spreads this from a disc to a region (identity theorem).
  - **Warning attached:** the zero sequence must accumulate at a point **inside** the disc where the series converges. sin(π/z) vanishes at 1/k → 0, but it is not given by a power series at 0 (§04 A3 asks why).
- **Paying AN1.10's hook** (`exm-arctan-radius-explained`).
  - Σ(−1)ⁿz^{2n} = 1/(1 + z²) on 𝔻 (geometric series).
  - Claim: its radius is exactly 1, and the reason is at ±i. If the radius were R > 1, the sum S would be holomorphic, hence continuous, on D(0, R) ∋ i. But S(ti) = 1/(1 − t²) for 0 ≤ t < 1, which → ∞ as t → 1⁻.
  - Compare: Cauchy–Hadamard gives radius 1 at once. What is new is the *explanation*: 1/(1 + x²) is smooth on ℝ, and the obstruction is invisible there.
  - Then **open the next hook** in one sentence: "the radius looks like the distance to the nearest point where the function misbehaves"; CA.2.
- **The chapter hook revisited** (`exm-flat-function-complex`). g(z) = e^{−1/z²} for z ≠ 0 (exp on ℂ is §04; state "exp is entire, §04" as a promise). On iℝ, g(iy) = e^{1/y²} → ∞, so g has no continuous extension at 0, let alone a power series there. On ℝ the same formula is AN1.10's flat function. **Boxed open question** (no forward `@ref`): is every holomorphic function given locally by a power series? (CA.2: yes.)
- **Worked examples.**
  - (a) Radius of Σ_{n≥1} z^{n!}: the coefficients are 0 or 1 (2 for z¹ if n starts at 0; choose n ≥ 1), and infinitely many are non-zero, so lim sup = 1 and R = 1.
  - (b) Σnⁿzⁿ/n!: the ratio a_{n+1}/aₙ = (1 + 1/n)ⁿ → e, so R = 1/e.
  - (c) Σn²zⁿ = z(1 + z)/(1 − z)³ on 𝔻: apply z·d/dz twice to Σzⁿ = 1/(1 − z).
- **Weird example** (`exm-lacunary-boundary`; table row). F(z) = Σ_{n≥0} z^{2ⁿ} has R = 1.
  - For ζ with ζ^{2^m} = 1, F(rζ) = (a polynomial in r) + Σ_{n≥m} r^{2ⁿ} → ∞ as r → 1⁻.
  - Such ζ are dense in the unit circle. So F has no continuous extension to any arc of the circle, and no holomorphic extension to any disc centred on it.
  - Contrast: 1/(1 − z) fails only at z = 1.
  - Full proof as §03 C2.

## 04-exponential-and-trigonometric.md — The exponential and trigonometric functions on ℂ (target 3,400 words)

- **Opening frame.** AN1.10 defined exp(z) = Σzⁿ/n! for complex z, proved e^{iθ} = cos θ + i sin θ, and defined π (prose pointers). Two questions remain:
  - which complex numbers does exp send to 1;
  - and so, how far is exp from being injective?
- **`prp-exp-entire`.** exp is entire with exp′ = exp. This is `thm-power-series-holomorphic` with R = ∞.
- **`thm-exp-addition`** (named move 4). exp(z + w) = exp z · exp w for all z, w ∈ ℂ.
  - Proof: for fixed c, g(z) = exp(z) exp(c − z) has g′ = 0 on ℂ, so g ≡ g(0) = exp c by `thm-derivative-zero-constant`. Put c = z + w.
  - Remark: AN1.10 may have proved this by the Cauchy product. This is a second proof, and it shows the move.
- **`cor-exp-basic`.**
  - exp z ≠ 0, and 1/exp z = exp(−z);
  - |exp z| = e^{Re z};
  - exp(x + iy) = eˣ(cos y + i sin y);
  - conj(exp z) = exp(z̄).
- **`thm-exp-kernel`.** exp z = 1 ⇔ z ∈ 2πiℤ. Hence exp z = exp w ⇔ z − w ∈ 2πiℤ, and exp has period 2πi.
  - Proof: |exp z| = 1 ⇒ Re z = 0. Then e^{iy} = 1 ⇔ cos y = 1 (and sin y = 0) ⇔ y ∈ 2πℤ (AN1.10).
- **`thm-exp-onto`.** exp maps ℂ onto ℂ ∖ {0}, and maps each strip {a < Im z ≤ a + 2π} bijectively onto ℂ ∖ {0}.
  - Proof: for w ≠ 0 write w = |w|e^{iθ} (polar form, AN1.10/F4.10) and take z = ln|w| + iθ. Injectivity on the strip comes from `thm-exp-kernel`.
- **Geometry (Needham 2.4, our own figure).** exp sends vertical lines to circles and horizontal lines to rays. Since exp′ ≠ 0, the orthogonal grid goes to the orthogonal polar grid (`thm-holomorphic-conformal`, §02).
- **`def-complex-trig`.** cos z = (e^{iz} + e^{−iz})/2 and sin z = (e^{iz} − e^{−iz})/(2i); cosh z and sinh z likewise. They agree with AN1.10's series. All are entire, with derivatives as on ℝ. The addition formulas and cos² + sin² = 1 follow from `thm-exp-addition`.
- **`thm-sin-zeros`.** sin z = 0 ⇔ z ∈ πℤ, and cos z = 0 ⇔ z ∈ π/2 + πℤ.
  - Proof: sin z = 0 ⇔ e^{2iz} = 1 ⇔ 2iz ∈ 2πiℤ (`thm-exp-kernel`). For cos, use cos z = sin(z + π/2).
  - Aftermath: no new zeros off the real axis. This matters for CA.3 (poles of cot and csc).
- **`prp-sin-modulus`.** |sin(x + iy)|² = sin²x + sinh²y, so sin is unbounded on ℂ (take x = π/2 and y → ∞).
- **Weird examples** (table rows):
  - sin is bounded on ℝ and unbounded on ℂ (preview of Liouville, §06 C1 and CA.2);
  - exp omits exactly the value 0 and takes every other value infinitely often (`thm-exp-onto` with periodicity). Prose: CA2 studies how few values a non-constant entire function can omit (prose pointer to CA.14, no `@ref`).
  - tan omits exactly ±i (C2).
- **Worked example.** Solve cos z = 2. Put w = e^{iz}: w² − 4w + 1 = 0, so w = 2 ± √3, and z = 2πk ∓ i ln(2 ± √3) = 2πk ± i ln(2 + √3), k ∈ ℤ. Moral: "|cos| ≤ 1" is a real fact.
- **Quick check.** Solve e^z = −1. *Answer:* z = iπ + 2πik, k ∈ ℤ.
- **Warning.** exp is not injective on ℂ: "exp a = exp b ⇒ a = b" is false (a = 0, b = 2πi). The correct statement is a − b ∈ 2πiℤ. This is the failure that §05 starts from.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Compute exp(2 + iπ/3) and |exp(iz)| for z = x + iy. *Answer:* e²(½ + i√3/2); e^{−y}.
  - **A2.** Find all z with exp z = 1 + i. *Answer:* ½ln 2 + i(π/4 + 2πk).
  - **A3.** Why does no power series with R > 0 agree with sin(π/z) on D(0, R) ∖ {0}, although sin(π/z) vanishes at every 1/k → 0? *Answer:* such a series would vanish at 1/k for large k, so it would be identically 0 by `prp-power-series-isolated-zeros`. But at z = 2/(4k + 1) → 0, sin(π/z) = sin((4k + 1)π/2) = 1. The zeros accumulate at 0 only because the function is not given by a power series there.
  - **B1.** Prove `thm-sin-zeros` in full, including the cos case.
  - **B2.** Prove `prp-sin-modulus` from sin(x + iy) = sin x cosh y + i cos x sinh y. *Answer:* sin²x cosh²y + cos²x sinh²y = sin²x(1 + sinh²y) + cos²x sinh²y.
  - **B3.** Show cos(z + w) = cos z cos w − sin z sin w on ℂ in two ways: (a) by `thm-exp-addition`; (b) for real w, by noting that both sides are power series in z with R = ∞ that agree for real z (AN1.10's real formula), then applying `prp-power-series-isolated-zeros` to their difference. *Answer:* (a) is direct. (b) the difference vanishes at 1/k → 0, so all its coefficients are 0. Extending (b) to complex w needs one more application of the same argument in w.
  - **C1.** Show that the only entire f with f′ = f and f(0) = 1 is exp. *Answer:* (f · exp(−z))′ = 0. Then `thm-derivative-zero-constant`.
  - **C2.** Show that tan z = sin z/cos z is holomorphic on ℂ ∖ (π/2 + πℤ) and takes every value except ±i. *Answer:* tan z = w ⇔ e^{2iz} = (i − w)/(i + w). This needs w ≠ −i for the quotient to be defined and w ≠ i for it to be non-zero. Then apply `thm-exp-onto`.

## 05-logarithms-and-branches.md — Logarithms, arguments and branches (target 4,200 words)

- **Opening frame (failure-motivated definition).** We want log as the inverse of exp. By `thm-exp-kernel`, the solutions of e^z = w (w ≠ 0) are a coset z₀ + 2πiℤ, so "log w" is a set. Choosing one value per point is easy; choosing **continuously** is the problem.
  - Picture (Needham 2.6, our own figure): follow w = e^{it} once around the circle and track a continuous choice of arg. It returns increased by 2π.
- **Definitions.** Let Ω ⊆ ℂ ∖ {0} be open.
  - `def-branch-arg`: a continuous θ: Ω → ℝ with z = |z|e^{iθ(z)}.
  - `def-branch-log`: a continuous L: Ω → ℂ with exp(L(z)) = z on Ω.
  - Relation: L is a branch of log iff L = ln|z| + iθ with θ a branch of arg. Proof via `cor-exp-basic`: Re L = ln|z|.
- **`def-principal-log`.** Arg z ∈ (−π, π] is the principal argument, and Log z = ln|z| + i Arg z on ℂ ∖ {0} (Apostol 1.30, recalled).
- **`prp-arg-continuous-slit`.** Arg is continuous on the slit plane ℂ⁻ := ℂ ∖ (−∞, 0], and discontinuous at each point of (−∞, 0).
  - Proof: for z = x + iy ∈ ℂ⁻, Arg z = 2 arctan(y/(|z| + x)), with |z| + x > 0. Check it by the half-angle identity: for z = re^{iθ} with θ ∈ (−π, π), y/(r + x) = tan(θ/2). The right side is continuous (AN1.10: real arctan).
  - On (−∞, 0), the limits from above and below are π and −π.
  - So Log restricted to ℂ⁻ is a branch of log; on ℂ ∖ {0}, Log is not continuous.
- **`lem-inverse-holomorphic`.** Let V and Ω be open, g: V → ℂ holomorphic, L: Ω → V continuous with g(L(z)) = z on Ω, and g′(L(z₀)) ≠ 0. Then L is complex differentiable at z₀ with L′(z₀) = 1/g′(L(z₀)).
  - Proof: for h ≠ 0 small, L(z₀ + h) ≠ L(z₀), because g takes different values at the two points. Put k = L(z₀ + h) − L(z₀) → 0 by continuity. Then (L(z₀ + h) − L(z₀))/h = k/(g(L(z₀) + k) − g(L(z₀))) → 1/g′(L(z₀)).
  - Revisits AN1.7's inverse-derivative theorem. Name the hypothesis that does the work, **continuity of L**: without it, k need not → 0.
- **`thm-log-holomorphic`.** Every branch L of log on Ω is holomorphic with L′(z) = 1/z. In particular Log is holomorphic on ℂ⁻.
  - Proof: `lem-inverse-holomorphic` with g = exp, since exp′(L(z)) = z ≠ 0.
- **`thm-branches-differ`.** If L₁ and L₂ are branches of log on a region Ω, then L₁ − L₂ ≡ 2πik for one fixed k ∈ ℤ.
  - Proof: exp(L₁ − L₂) = 1, so (L₁ − L₂)(Ω) ⊆ 2πiℤ. A continuous function from a connected set into a discrete set is constant (AN2.3: the image is connected). Alternative: (L₁ − L₂)′ = 0, then `thm-derivative-zero-constant`.
  - Corollary: on ℂ ∖ ray_α, where ray_α = {re^{iα} : r ≥ 0}, the branches are exactly ln|z| + iθ_α(z) + 2πik, with θ_α ∈ (α, α + 2π). "Branch cut" is named in prose.
- ***Try it first (ten minutes):*** *suppose L were a branch of log on ℂ ∖ {0}. On ℂ⁻ compare L with Log, and look at what happens above and below −1.*
- **`thm-no-log-punctured-plane`.** There is no branch of log on ℂ ∖ {0}. More strongly, there is no continuous θ on the unit circle with e^{iθ(z)} = z, so there is no branch of log or arg on any open set containing the unit circle.
  - Proof 1 (the try-it-first route, for ℂ ∖ {0}): suppose L is one. On ℂ⁻, L − Log = 2πik (`thm-branches-differ`; ℂ⁻ is a region, since it is star-shaped about 1). As z → −1 through Im z > 0, Log z → iπ; through Im z < 0, Log z → −iπ. Continuity of L at −1 gives L(−1) = iπ + 2πik = −iπ + 2πik, a contradiction.
  - Proof 2 (the strong form): given such θ, put φ(t) = θ(e^{it}) − t on [0, 2π]. Then e^{iφ(t)} = 1, so φ is continuous with values in 2πℤ, hence constant on the connected [0, 2π] (AN2.3). But φ(2π) = θ(1) − 2π = φ(0) − 2π. A branch L on an open set containing the circle would give θ = Im L on the circle.
  - Aftermath: §06 gives a second proof, by integration (`cor-no-primitive-one-over-z`).
- **`exm-log-series`.** Log(1 + z) = Σ_{n≥1} (−1)^{n+1}zⁿ/n on 𝔻.
  - Both sides are holomorphic on 𝔻, since Re(1 + z) > 0 puts 1 + z in ℂ⁻. Both have derivative 1/(1 + z), by `thm-log-holomorphic` with the chain rule, and by the geometric series. Both vanish at 0.
  - Conclude by `thm-derivative-zero-constant` (named move 4). This pays §03 B3.
- **`def-complex-power`.** For a branch L on Ω and α ∈ ℂ, z^α := exp(αL(z)), holomorphic with (z^α)′ = αz^α/z.
  - The principal power uses Log.
  - For α = n ∈ ℤ the value does not depend on the branch.
  - For α = 1/n there are n branches on a region, differing by the factors e^{2πik/n}.
  - `prp-no-sqrt-punctured-plane` (C1): there is no continuous square root on ℂ ∖ {0}.
- **Weird examples and failures** (table rows):
  - Log(zw) ≠ Log z + Log w: z = w = −1 gives 0 versus 2πi.
  - Log(z²) ≠ 2 Log z: z = e^{3πi/4} gives −iπ/2 versus 3πi/2.
  - i^i = e^{−π/2} for the principal power, a real number. The set of all values is {e^{−π/2 − 2πk} : k ∈ ℤ}.
  - (z^a)^b ≠ z^{ab} in general (B2).
  - √(z²) ≠ z for Re z < 0 (principal root).
- **Quick check.** Compute Log(−i) and the principal (−1)^i. *Answer:* −iπ/2; e^{−π}.
- **Warning.** "log" without a branch is not a function. In every formula, name the branch and the open set. Log is holomorphic on ℂ⁻, **not** on ℂ ∖ {0}.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Compute Log(1 − i), Log(−2) and Log(3i). *Answer:* ½ln 2 − iπ/4; ln 2 + iπ; ln 3 + iπ/2.
  - **A2.** For which z is Log(z̄) = conj(Log z)? *Answer:* exactly for z ∉ (−∞, 0). On (−∞, 0) both have Arg = π, so Log z̄ = Log z ≠ conj(Log z).
  - **A3.** On which open set is z ↦ Log(1 − z) holomorphic, and what is its derivative? *Answer:* ℂ ∖ [1, ∞), with derivative −1/(1 − z) = 1/(z − 1).
  - **B1.** Show that the n branches of z^{1/n} on ℂ⁻ are ω^k exp((1/n)Log z), where ω = e^{2πi/n} and k = 0, …, n − 1, and that they are distinct. *Answer:* each is a continuous s with sⁿ = z. Two of them differ by a continuous function with values in the n-th roots of unity, constant by connectedness. They are distinct because the ω^k are.
  - **B2.** Find principal values with (z^a)^b ≠ z^{ab}. *Answer:* z = −1, a = 2, b = ½. Then z^a = 1 and (z^a)^b = 1, while z^{ab} = (−1)¹ = −1.
  - **B3 (find the error; the conclusion is true).** "Claim: Log(zw) = Log z + Log w when Re z > 0 and Re w > 0. Proof: exp(Log z + Log w) = exp(Log z) exp(Log w) = zw = exp(Log(zw)), hence Log z + Log w = Log(zw)." *Answer:*
    - The conclusion is true.
    - The last step uses "exp a = exp b ⇒ a = b", which is false (a = 0, b = 2πi; `thm-exp-kernel`).
    - Repair: the two sides differ by 2πik, and their imaginary parts Arg z + Arg w ∈ (−π, π) and Arg(zw) ∈ (−π, π] differ by less than 2π, so k = 0.
  - **C1.** Prove `prp-no-sqrt-punctured-plane`. *Hint 1:* compare with the principal root p(z) = exp(½Log z) on ℂ⁻. *Hint 2:* s/p takes values in {±1}. *Answer:* s/p is constant, ε say, on the region ℂ⁻. As z → −1 from above, p → i; from below, p → −i. So s(−1) = εi = −εi, a contradiction.
  - **C2.** Show that f(z) = z·exp(½Log(1 − 1/z²)) is holomorphic on ℂ ∖ [−1, 1], that f(z)² = z² − 1, and that f(z)/z → 1 as |z| → ∞. *Answer:*
    - 1 − 1/z² ∈ (−∞, 0] ⇔ z² ∈ (0, 1] ⇔ z ∈ [−1, 1] ∖ {0}, and z = 0 is excluded anyway. So the composition is holomorphic there.

## 06-integration-along-curves.md — Curves and contour integrals (target 4,800 words, including the R group, the weird-examples table and the List of Results)

- **Opening frame.** Apostol 16.2–16.3. The tool that CA.2 turns into Cauchy's theorem. Remark (once): Apostol integrates along rectifiable paths with Riemann–Stieltjes integrals (AN2.9, AN2.10; prose pointer). CA uses piecewise C¹ paths throughout, which suffices for every theorem through CA.5.
- **`def-curve`.** A *curve* (piecewise C¹ path) is a continuous γ: [a, b] → ℂ for which some partition a = t₀ < ⋯ < t_m = b makes γ C¹ on each [t_{j−1}, t_j], with one-sided derivatives at the ends.
  - closed if γ(a) = γ(b);
  - trace γ*;
  - reverse γ⁻(t) = γ(a + b − t);
  - concatenation γ₁ + γ₂ when γ₁ ends where γ₂ starts;
  - segment [z₁, z₂];
  - circles C(a, r) and C_k(a, r);
  - *equivalent* parametrizations: γ ∘ φ for a C¹ bijection φ: [c, d] → [a, b] with φ′ > 0.
- **Complex-valued integrals.** For g: [a, b] → ℂ piecewise continuous, ∫_a^b g := ∫Re g + i∫Im g (AN1.8, componentwise). It is ℂ-linear, and the FTC and substitution hold componentwise.
- **`lem-complex-integral-modulus`** (named move 5). |∫_a^b g| ≤ ∫_a^b |g|.
  - **Scratch Work (technique 3, first appearance).** Splitting into real and imaginary parts loses a factor (A2 shows the trap). Instead, let w = ∫g and θ = arg w. Then |w| = e^{−iθ}w = ∫e^{−iθ}g, which is real. So it equals ∫Re(e^{−iθ}g) ≤ ∫|g|.
  - Proof (typeset). The case w = 0 is trivial.
- **`def-contour-integral`.** For f continuous on γ*, ∫_γ f(z) dz := Σ_j ∫_{t_{j−1}}^{t_j} f(γ(t))γ′(t) dt, and L(γ) := ∫_a^b |γ′(t)| dt.
- **`prp-contour-integral-properties`.**
  - (i) ℂ-linear in f;
  - (ii) unchanged under equivalent reparametrization (substitution, AN1.8);
  - (iii) ∫_{γ⁻} = −∫_γ;
  - (iv) ∫_{γ₁+γ₂} = ∫_{γ₁} + ∫_{γ₂}.

  Proofs: (ii) and (iii) in full, (i) and (iv) one line each.
- **`thm-ml-inequality`.** |∫_γ f dz| ≤ max_{γ*}|f| · L(γ). The max exists because γ* is compact (AN2.3: continuous image of [a, b]) and |f| is continuous.
  - Proof: `lem-complex-integral-modulus`.
- **`thm-primitive-closed-curve`** (key proof 3). Let F be holomorphic on an open Ω ⊇ γ*, with F′ = f continuous on Ω. Then ∫_γ f dz = F(γ(b)) − F(γ(a)). In particular ∫_γ f dz = 0 for every closed curve γ in Ω.
  - Proof: on each piece, (F ∘ γ)′(t) = f(γ(t))γ′(t) by `lem-chain-rule-curve`. This is continuous, so the FTC applies componentwise. Then telescope over the partition.
  - Remark: the continuity of f = F′ is assumed here so that the Riemann integral applies. CA.2 shows that it is automatic.
- **`exm-integral-power-circle`.** For n ∈ ℤ and r > 0: ∮_{C(a,r)} (z − a)ⁿ dz = 0 for n ≠ −1, and = 2πi for n = −1.
  - For n ≠ −1: by `thm-primitive-closed-curve` with F = (z − a)^{n+1}/(n + 1) on ℂ (n ≥ 0) or on ℂ ∖ {a} (n ≤ −2).
  - For n = −1: compute directly, ∫_0^{2π} (re^{it})^{−1} ire^{it} dt = 2πi.
- **`cor-no-primitive-one-over-z`.** 1/z has no primitive on ℂ ∖ {0}. Hence a second proof of `thm-no-log-punctured-plane`: a branch of log would be a primitive (`thm-log-holomorphic`).
- **`exm-multiple-circle`** (the hook). ∮_{C_k(0,1)} dz/z = 2πik. The integral counts how many times the curve goes around 0.
  - Open the hook in prose: what does ∮_γ dz/(z − a) count for a general closed γ? Why is it 0 when the integrand is holomorphic on a disc containing γ? (CA.2; CA.4.)
- **Non-holomorphic integrands** (`exm-conjugate-integrals`).
  - ∮_{C(0,1)} z̄ dz = 2πi, because z̄ = 1/z on the circle.
  - ∮_{C(0,1)} Re z dz = πi.
  - Path dependence: ∫ z̄ dz over [0, 1 + i] is 1, and over [0, 1] + [1, 1 + i] it is 1 + i.
  - By contrast, ∫ z dz is i over both, by the primitive z²/2.
- **`thm-uniform-limit-contour`.** If f_n → f uniformly on γ* with f_n continuous, then ∫_γ f_n → ∫_γ f. Proof: ML.

  - **Cauchy estimate:** |aₘ| ≤ M(r)/r^m with M(r) = max_{C(a,r)}|f|.
  - Proof: `cor-power-series-contour` and `exm-integral-power-circle`.
  - Prose: CA.2 proves the same formula for **every** holomorphic function, which is how it pays the chapter hook.
- **Second proof of `thm-derivative-zero-constant`** (by curves). Any two points of a region are joined by a polygonal curve in it (B4). Then f(z₁) − f(z₀) = ∫_γ f′ = 0. Note: this uses the continuity of f′ = 0, which is trivial here.
