## Chapter hook and where it is paid

**Hook (index.md).** The plane ℂ and the disc 𝔻 are homeomorphic: z ↦ z/(1 + |z|) is a continuous bijection with continuous inverse. To a holomorphic function, though, they are different worlds. Every bounded holomorphic function on ℂ is constant (Liouville), while 𝔻 carries the bounded holomorphic function z ↦ z. So "the same shape" must mean more than homeomorphic. Three questions follow.

1. **Which regions are conformally the same as 𝔻?** A strip, a sector and the plane minus a ray are, by explicit formulas (§2). The plane is not (§1). The chapter's answer: *every* simply connected region other than ℂ is, however wild its boundary. **Paid in §5** (`thm-riemann-mapping`).
2. **Is conformal type just topology, apart from ℂ?** No. The annuli r < |z| < 1 are all homeomorphic, but two of them are conformally equivalent only when their radii agree. **Paid in §5** (`thm-annuli-inequivalent`).
3. **What does a conformal map do at the boundary?** Where the boundary is a segment or a circular arc, the map continues holomorphically across it. **Paid in §6** (`thm-boundary-arc-extension`). The general Jordan-curve answer (Carathéodory) is stated only.

**Hooks opened here and paid later** (named in the text as forward pointers in prose, no `@ref`):

- Montel's theorem for families that omit two values (normality into Ĉ) → **CA.14** (Bloch–Landau–Schottky–Picard). §4 states the question after `thm-montel`.
- The Riemann map gives the Green's function of a simply connected region, g(z, z₀) = −log|f(z)| → **CA.11**. Mentioned at the end of §5.
- The proof of `thm-riemann-mapping` uses simple connectivity only through square roots. Hence "every non-vanishing holomorphic function has a square root" already forces conformal equivalence to 𝔻 or ℂ, and the list of equivalent forms of simple connectivity → **CA.12**. Remark at the end of §5.
- The Cayley map and Aut(𝔻) → **CA.9** (optional, CA × FA: Herglotz on the disc, then the half-plane).

**Hooks paid here, opened earlier:** the CA.4 corollary "a locally uniform limit of injective holomorphic maps is injective or constant" is used in §5. The CA.6 Dirichlet problem on a disc becomes the engine of §6. CA.10's φ_a maps become all of Aut(𝔻) in §3.

## 01-conformal-maps.md — Angles, injectivity, and conformal equivalence

**Budget:** ≤ 3,500 main-line words.

- **Hook (section).** Apostol's question: an analytic function sends two segments crossing at c to two curves crossing at f(c). When is the angle between them kept? The linear model is multiplication by f′(c), a rotation followed by a dilation, so the answer should be "when f′(c) ≠ 0".
- **Angle** between two smooth curves at a common point: arg γ₂′(t₂) − arg γ₁′(t₁), modulo 2π. Assume γᵢ′(tᵢ) ≠ 0. (F4.10's argument.)
- **`thm-conformal-angle`.** Let f be holomorphic near c with f′(c) ≠ 0. Then for every pair of smooth curves through c with non-zero tangents, the image curves have non-zero tangents at f(c), and the oriented angle between them equals the original.
  - *Proof sketch:* chain rule, (f∘γ)′(t) = f′(c)γ′(t), and arg of a product is the sum of args modulo 2π (F4.10).
- **`prp-angle-multiplication`.** If f − f(c) has a zero of order k ≥ 2 at c, the angle between any two curves at c is multiplied by k.
  - *Proof sketch:* f(z) − f(c) = (z − c)^k g(z) with g(c) ≠ 0. The tangent direction of f∘γ at c is the k-th power of that of γ, times g(c). Get it from the leading term of (f∘γ)(t) − f(c) = (γ(t) − c)^k g(γ(t)).
  - Example: z ↦ z² doubles the right angle between the axes, sending them to the positive and negative real axes.
- **`cor-injective-inverse-holomorphic`.** Let f be holomorphic and injective on an open set U. Then f′(z) ≠ 0 for every z ∈ U, f(U) is open, and f⁻¹: f(U) → U is holomorphic with (f⁻¹)′(w) = 1/f′(f⁻¹(w)).
  - *Proof:* f′ ≠ 0 is the corollary of `thm-inverse-holomorphic` (CA.4; it rests on `thm-local-mapping`), recalled with its one-line reason: a zero of f′ makes f k-to-1 near the point, with k ≥ 2. f(U) is open by `thm-open-mapping`. The local inverses from `thm-inverse-holomorphic` agree with the global f⁻¹, so f⁻¹ is holomorphic. CA.4 proves the local statement; the content here is the global one, and its proof is three lines.
- **Failure-motivated definition.** First try: call two regions "the same" if they are homeomorphic. That fails, because ℂ ≅ 𝔻 topologically, and yet the hook shows that holomorphic function theory on the two is different.
  - **`def-conformal-map`.** A conformal map (biholomorphism) of a region Ω onto a region Ω′ is a holomorphic bijection f: Ω → Ω′. By `cor-injective-inverse-holomorphic` its inverse is holomorphic, so the definition is symmetric.
  - **`def-conformally-equivalent`.** Ω and Ω′ are conformally equivalent if such a map exists.
  - **`def-automorphism-group`.** Aut(Ω) is the group of conformal self-maps of Ω.
  - State, with a two-line proof, that conformal equivalence is an equivalence relation. A conformal map is a homeomorphism, so it preserves simple connectivity.
  - **Terminology warning.** Some books call f "conformal" when f′ ≠ 0 pointwise (Apostol 16.27 does: "conformal at c"). This book says *conformal at c* for that local property, and *conformal map* only for the bijection.
- **`thm-plane-not-disc`.** ℂ and 𝔻 are not conformally equivalent.
  - *Proof:* a conformal map ℂ → 𝔻 is a bounded entire function, so it is constant (CA.2: Liouville), which contradicts injectivity.
- **Weird examples** (go into the table):
  - exp has non-zero derivative everywhere, but exp(z + 2πi) = exp z. So "conformal at every point" does not imply injective. Contrast this with `cor-injective-inverse-holomorphic`, whose converse is false.
  - z ↦ z̄ keeps the size of angles and reverses their orientation. It is anticonformal and not holomorphic.
- **Worked example.** f(z) = z³ − 3z. Here f′(z) = 3(z² − 1), so f is conformal at every point except ±1, and at ±1 angles double. Find a largest disc about 0 on which f is injective. Answer: |z| < 1.
  - *Argument:* f(z) − f(w) = (z − w)(z² + zw + w² − 3), and |z² + zw + w²| ≤ |z|² + |z||w| + |w|² < 3 when |z|, |w| < 1, so f is injective on 𝔻. No disc of radius > 1 about 0 works, because it contains the critical point 1 (`cor-injective-inverse-holomorphic`). Verify both halves with sympy.
- **Quick check.** Is z ↦ z² conformal at 0? Is it a conformal map of the right half-plane onto ℂ ∖ (−∞, 0]? *Answer:* no; yes. On Re z > 0, arg z ∈ (−π/2, π/2), so z² is injective there, and its image is everything with arg ∈ (−π, π).
- **Warning.** "f′ ≠ 0 on Ω" does not make f a conformal map of Ω onto f(Ω). Witness: exp on the strip |Im z| < 2π.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** At which points is f(z) = z⁴ + 4z conformal? What happens to angles at the others? *Answer:* f′ = 4(z³ + 1) = 0 at the three cube roots of −1. There f − f(c) has a zero of order exactly 2 (f″(c) = 12c² ≠ 0), so angles double.
  - **A2.** Show z ↦ z + 1/z is injective on 𝔻 ∖ {0}. *Answer:* z + 1/z = w + 1/w ⇔ (z − w)(1 − 1/(zw)) = 0, and zw = 1 is impossible when |z|, |w| < 1.
  - **B1.** Let f be a conformal map of Ω onto Ω′, and let g be holomorphic on Ω′. Show that g is injective on Ω′ if and only if g∘f is injective on Ω, and that (g∘f)′ never vanishes if g′ never vanishes. *Answer:* f is a bijection; the chain rule, with f′ ≠ 0 by `cor-injective-inverse-holomorphic`.
  - **B2.** Show that the Joukowski map J(z) = z + 1/z sends the upper half of 𝔻 onto the lower half-plane. *Answer:* for z = re^{iθ} with 0 < r < 1 and 0 < θ < π, Im J = (r − 1/r) sin θ < 0. Onto: for Im w < 0, the roots of z² − wz + 1 = 0 have product 1 and are not on the unit circle (otherwise w = 2 cos θ would be real), so exactly one root lies in 𝔻. For it, Im J(z) = Im w < 0 forces sin θ > 0.
  - **C1.** Let F: U → ℝ² be real-differentiable at c with invertible derivative. Suppose F preserves the oriented angle between every pair of smooth curves through c. Show that F, as a function of z, is complex-differentiable at c. *Answer:* the derivative L = DF(c) (b AN3.1) preserves oriented angles between all vectors, so L sends an orthonormal positive basis to an orthogonal positive pair of equal lengths. Then L is a rotation times a dilation, that is, multiplication by a complex number, and that is the Cauchy–Riemann condition (CA.1).

## 02-gallery.md — A gallery of conformal maps

**Budget:** ≤ 4,000 main-line words.

- **Lead-in.** Before the general theorem, build the maps by hand. Each tool comes with what it does to edges:
  - Möbius maps send circles and lines to circles and lines (CA.10);
  - z ↦ z^α (principal branch) opens a sector of angle θ to one of angle αθ;
  - exp sends a horizontal strip of height π onto a half-plane;
  - log goes back.
- **`prp-standard-maps`** (one proposition, a list, each item with a one-line verification):
  1. Cayley: C(z) = (z − i)/(z + i) maps ℍ onto 𝔻, and its inverse is i(1 + w)/(1 − w) (recalled from CA.10).
  2. z ↦ z^{π/θ} maps the sector {0 < arg z < θ} (0 < θ ≤ 2π) onto ℍ.
  3. exp maps the strip {0 < Im z < π} onto ℍ.
  4. (1 + z)/(1 − z) maps 𝔻 onto the right half-plane, (−1, 1) onto (0, ∞), and the upper half-disc onto the first quadrant.
  5. Principal √z maps ℂ ∖ (−∞, 0] onto the right half-plane.
  - *Proof sketch:* in each case show the map is injective on the region, maps it into the target, and hits every target point. For the sector maps use arg; for exp use e^{x+iy} = eˣe^{iy} with 0 < y < π.
- **Named move: Straighten, then open.** Introduce it here, with the composition diagram drawn as a sequence of small pictures (TikZ figure, one panel per step).
- **Worked example 1 (half-disc).** Map {|z| < 1, Im z > 0} onto 𝔻:
  - z ↦ (1 + z)/(1 − z) gives the first quadrant;
  - squaring gives ℍ;
  - then Cayley.
  - So f(z) = (w − i)/(w + i) with w = ((1 + z)/(1 − z))². Check one interior value numerically (sympy) and say where the corners ±1 go.
- **Worked example 2 (slit disc).** Map 𝔻 ∖ [0, 1) onto 𝔻:
  - z ↦ −z gives 𝔻 ∖ (−1, 0];
  - principal √ gives the right half-disc;
  - multiplying by i gives the upper half-disc;
  - then worked example 1.
- **Worked example 3 (slit plane).** ℂ ∖ (−∞, 0] → 𝔻 by z ↦ (√z − 1)/(√z + 1). This is the region the RMT proof starts from in §5, so mark it.
- **Weird example.** The Joukowski map on 𝔻 ∖ {0} is injective (§1 A2), and its image is ℂ ∖ [−2, 2], which is not simply connected. So 𝔻 ∖ {0} and ℂ ∖ [−2, 2] are conformally equivalent, while neither is equivalent to 𝔻.
  - Prove the image claim in one paragraph: |z| = 1 maps onto [−2, 2], and the argument of B2 extends.
- **Transplanting the Dirichlet problem.** By CA.6 (harmonic ∘ holomorphic is harmonic), a conformal map moves solutions of the Dirichlet problem, boundary data and all, when the map extends continuously to the boundary pieces involved.
  - CA.6 proved this as `thm-dirichlet-transplant`, with the half-plane, the quadrant and the strip as examples (CA.6 §5). Here the new point is composing several transplants. Do **not** repeat CA.6's examples.
  - **Worked example 4 (half-disc).** Find the bounded harmonic function on the upper half-disc with value 1 on the diameter (−1, 1) and 0 on the open semicircle.
    - (1 + z)/(1 − z) sends the half-disc to the first quadrant, the diameter to (0, ∞) and the semicircle to the positive imaginary axis.
    - On the quadrant the solution is 1 − (2/π)Arg w (CA.6 §5). So u(z) = 1 − (2/π)Arg((1 + z)/(1 − z)).
    - Check: at z = i/2, w = (1 + i/2)/(1 − i/2) = (3 + 4i)/5, so u = 1 − (2/π)arctan(4/3) ≈ 0.410. The drafter verifies this with sympy and states it as a check, not as a claim of symmetry.
  - **Remark.** The data jump at ±1, where `thm-dirichlet-transplant` (continuous data on the closure) does not apply as stated. Uniqueness among bounded solutions is CA.6 §6 C2's argument, cited in prose.
- **Quick check.** Which standard map sends the strip {|Im z| < π/2} onto the right half-plane? *Answer:* exp.
- **Warning.** z ↦ z^α is a conformal map of a sector only while the opened angle stays ≤ 2π, that is, α·θ ≤ 2π. Beyond that it wraps around and stops being injective.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Map the sector {0 < arg z < π/3} onto 𝔻. *Answer:* (z³ − i)/(z³ + i).
  - **A2.** Map the strip {0 < Im z < 1} onto 𝔻. *Answer:* (e^{πz} − i)/(e^{πz} + i).
  - **B1.** Find a bounded harmonic function on the sector {0 < arg z < π/3} equal to 1 on the edge arg z = 0 and 0 on the edge arg z = π/3. *Answer:* z³ sends the sector to ℍ and the edges to (0, ∞) and (−∞, 0); with the half-plane solution 1 − Arg(w)/π, u = 1 − 3Arg(z)/π.
  - **B2.** Find a conformal map of the region between the circles |z − 1| = 1 and |z − 2| = 2 (the crescent that touches at 0) onto the strip {0 < Re w < 1}. *Answer:* w = 1/z sends |z − 1| = 1 to Re w = 1/2 and |z − 2| = 2 to Re w = 1/4. The crescent goes to {1/4 < Re w < 1/2}, and then w ↦ 4w − 1 finishes. S2a: verify the orientation and the image strip with sympy.
  - **C1.** Show that no Möbius map sends the upper half-disc onto 𝔻. *Answer:* a Möbius map is a homeomorphism of Ĉ that is conformal at every point, including the corners. It would carry the boundary of the half-disc onto the unit circle, sending the right angle at 1 between the two boundary arcs to an angle π/2 between two arcs of the unit circle at one point. But two arcs of one circle through a point meet at angle π.

## 03-schwarz.md — Schwarz's lemma and the automorphisms of the disc

**Budget:** ≤ 4,500 main-line words.

- **Hook.** §2 produced many maps onto 𝔻. Are they unique? If f and g both map Ω conformally onto 𝔻, then g∘f⁻¹ is an automorphism of 𝔻. So the question becomes: what is Aut(𝔻)? CA.10 gave the rotations and the maps φ_a. The answer is that there are no others, and the tool is a lemma about maps 𝔻 → 𝔻 fixing 0.
- **Scratch Work (before the proof; first appearance of the "divide out the zero" technique).** The question is how f(0) = 0 together with |f| < 1 constrains f.
  - Try g(z) = f(z)/z. It is holomorphic after removing the singularity at 0 (CA.3).
  - On |z| = r < 1 we have |g| ≤ 1/r, so by the maximum modulus principle |g| ≤ 1/r on |z| ≤ r.
  - Now let r → 1.
  - Write this as scratch, with the dead end "apply maximum modulus directly on |z| = 1" shown failing: f is not defined there.
- **`thm-schwarz-lemma`.** Let f: 𝔻 → 𝔻 be holomorphic with f(0) = 0. Then:
  - |f(z)| ≤ |z| for all z ∈ 𝔻, and |f′(0)| ≤ 1;
  - if |f(z₀)| = |z₀| for some z₀ ≠ 0, or |f′(0)| = 1, then f(z) = e^{iθ}z for some real θ.
  - *Proof:* the scratch made formal. The equality case: |g| attains its maximum 1 at an interior point, so g is a constant of modulus 1 (CA.2: maximum modulus).
  - All hypotheses are needed: without f(0) = 0, take a constant 1/2; without |f| < 1, take 2z.
- **Named move: Move the point to the origin.**
- **`thm-schwarz-pick`.** Let f: 𝔻 → 𝔻 be holomorphic. For z, w ∈ 𝔻:
  - |φ_{f(w)}(f(z))| ≤ |φ_w(z)|;
  - |f′(z)| ≤ (1 − |f(z)|²)/(1 − |z|²);
  - equality in either at a single point (with z ≠ w in the first) forces f ∈ Aut(𝔻).
  - *Proof:* apply `thm-schwarz-lemma` to φ_{f(w)} ∘ f ∘ φ_{−w}. Differentiate at w for the derivative form, using φ_a′(a) = 1/(1 − |a|²) and φ_a′(0) = 1 − |a|².
  - Proof Idea only for the algebra. The drafter writes the computation in a sidecar.
  - *Remark (prose):* the quantity |φ_w(z)| is a distance on 𝔻 (pseudo-hyperbolic) that holomorphic self-maps do not increase. Needham 7.7 is the picture. No hyperbolic geometry is developed.
- **`thm-aut-disc`.** Aut(𝔻) = {z ↦ e^{iθ}φ_a(z) : θ ∈ ℝ, a ∈ 𝔻}. Its stabilizer of 0 is the rotations, and it acts transitively on 𝔻.
  - *Proof:* let h ∈ Aut(𝔻) and a = h⁻¹(0). Then k = h∘φ_{−a} fixes 0, and so does k⁻¹. Schwarz gives |k′(0)| ≤ 1 and |(k⁻¹)′(0)| ≤ 1, and the chain rule gives (k⁻¹)′(0) = 1/k′(0). So |k′(0)| = 1 and k is a rotation.
  - Scratch Work is *not* repeated (fading); give a one-line Proof Idea before the proof.
- **`thm-aut-half-plane`.** Aut(ℍ) = {z ↦ (az + b)/(cz + d) : a, b, c, d ∈ ℝ, ad − bc = 1}, and two matrices give the same map iff they differ by sign. So Aut(ℍ) ≅ PSL₂(ℝ).
  - *Proof sketch:* conjugate by the Cayley map, Aut(ℍ) = C⁻¹ Aut(𝔻) C. Each real-matrix map preserves ℍ, because Im((az + b)/(cz + d)) = Im z/|cz + d|². The stabilizer of i corresponds to the rotations: z ↦ (z cos θ + sin θ)/(−z sin θ + cos θ). Transitivity on ℍ: z ↦ yz + x sends i to x + iy. A transitive subgroup containing the full stabilizer is the whole group.
- **`thm-aut-plane`.** Aut(ℂ) = {z ↦ az + b : a ≠ 0}.
  - *Proof:* h ∈ Aut(ℂ) is entire and injective. If ∞ were an essential singularity, then by Casorati–Weierstrass (CA.3) the image of {|z| > 1} would be dense. That image is disjoint from the open set h(𝔻), a contradiction. So h is a polynomial, and injectivity forces degree 1 (a degree-n polynomial has n roots of h(z) = w counted with multiplicity, and for n ≥ 2 some w has two distinct preimages, because h′ has only finitely many zeros).
  - This is the closure of Apostol 16.43. Do not reuse its wording.
- **Uniqueness of Riemann maps (`cor-riemann-map-unique`).** If f, g: Ω → 𝔻 are conformal maps with f(z₀) = g(z₀) = 0 and f′(z₀), g′(z₀) > 0, then f = g.
  - *Proof:* g∘f⁻¹ ∈ Aut(𝔻) fixes 0, so it is a rotation, and its derivative at 0, g′(z₀)/f′(z₀), is positive. So the rotation is the identity.
  - This is half of §5's theorem, proved before the existence half.
- **Quick checks.**
  - Q1. f: 𝔻 → 𝔻 holomorphic with f(0) = 0: how large can |f(1/2)| be, and which f achieve it? *Answer:* 1/2; the rotations.
  - Q2. Compute φ_{1/2}(0) and φ_{1/2}(1/2). *Answer:* −1/2, 0.
- **Warning.** Schwarz's lemma needs f(0) = 0. For a general self-map of 𝔻 use Schwarz–Pick, not |f(z)| ≤ |z|.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Can a holomorphic f: 𝔻 → 𝔻 have f(0) = 0 and f(1/2) = 3/4? *Answer:* no, since |f(1/2)| ≤ 1/2.
  - **A2.** Write down the automorphism of 𝔻 sending 1/3 to 0 with positive derivative at 1/3. *Answer:* φ_{1/3}(z) = (z − 1/3)/(1 − z/3), with φ_{1/3}′(1/3) = 1/(1 − 1/9) = 9/8 > 0.
  - **B1.** Let f: 𝔻 → 𝔻 be holomorphic with f(0) = 1/2. Show |f′(0)| ≤ 3/4 and find every f attaining equality. *Answer:* Schwarz–Pick at z = 0 gives |f′(0)| ≤ 1 − 1/4. Equality forces f ∈ Aut(𝔻) with f(0) = 1/2, so f = φ_{−1/2}∘(rotation): f(z) = (e^{iθ}z + 1/2)/(1 + e^{iθ}z/2).
  - **B2.** Find every automorphism of ℍ fixing i. *Answer:* the maps z ↦ (z cos θ + sin θ)/(−z sin θ + cos θ).
  - **B3.** Let f: 𝔻 → 𝔻 be holomorphic with f(0) = 0 and f(a) = 0 for some a ∈ 𝔻 ∖ {0}. Show |f(z)| ≤ |z||φ_a(z)| on 𝔻, and deduce |f′(0)| ≤ |a|. *Answer:* g = f/(zφ_a) is holomorphic after removing two singularities. φ_a is continuous on the closed disc with |φ_a| = 1 on the circle, so m(r) = min_{|z|=r}|zφ_a(z)| → 1 as r → 1. By maximum modulus, |g| ≤ 1/m(r) on |z| ≤ r; let r → 1 to get |g| ≤ 1. Then f′(0) = g(0)φ_a(0) = −a·g(0).
  - **C1.** Show that a holomorphic f: 𝔻 → 𝔻 with two distinct fixed points is the identity. *Answer:* let the fixed points be a and b. Then h = φ_a∘f∘φ_{−a} fixes 0 and β = φ_a(b) ≠ 0. Since |h(β)| = |β|, Schwarz makes h a rotation, and a rotation fixing β ≠ 0 is the identity.
  - **C2.** Show that a holomorphic f: 𝔻 → 𝔻 with f(0) = 0 that is not a rotation has iterates fⁿ → 0 locally uniformly on 𝔻. *Answer:* on |z| ≤ r, the function g = f(z)/z has max_{|z|≤r}|g| = c_r < 1 by Schwarz (strict, since f is not a rotation). Then |f(z)| ≤ c_r|z| on |z| ≤ r, f maps that disc into itself, and |fⁿ(z)| ≤ c_rⁿ r.
  - **C3** (only if CA.10 does not prove it). Aut(Ĉ) is the group of Möbius maps. *Answer:* compose with a Möbius map so that ∞ is fixed. Then the restriction lies in Aut(ℂ), so it is affine by `thm-aut-plane`.

## 04-normal-families.md — The space H(Ω) and Montel's theorem

**Budget:** ≤ 4,500 main-line words.

- **Hook.** §5 will prove existence by choosing the "best" map among infinitely many and needs a limit of a maximizing sequence to exist. In ℝⁿ, Bolzano–Weierstrass supplies such limits; this section supplies the complex version for spaces of holomorphic functions. Arzelà–Ascoli (AN2.5) did the same job for C(K), and this is its complex node. In one line: holomorphy turns a bound into equicontinuity, so bounded families of holomorphic functions behave like bounded sets in ℝⁿ.
- **Failure-motivated topology.** What should "fₙ → f" mean on an open set Ω?
  - Uniform convergence on Ω fails. zⁿ → 0 pointwise on 𝔻, but sup_𝔻 |zⁿ| = 1 for every n. Yet on each disc |z| ≤ r < 1 it converges uniformly, and every theorem of CA.2 (Weierstrass) only needs that.
  - The sup norm itself is not even defined on all of H(𝔻): 1/(1 − z) is unbounded.
  - So use **locally uniform convergence** (uniform on each compact subset), which CA.2 already uses.
- **`lem-compact-exhaustion`.** Every open Ω ⊂ ℂ has compact sets K₁ ⊂ int K₂ ⊂ K₂ ⊂ int K₃ ⊂ ⋯ with ⋃ Kₙ = Ω, such that every compact K ⊂ Ω lies in some Kₙ. Take Kₙ = {z ∈ Ω : |z| ≤ n, dist(z, ℂ ∖ Ω) ≥ 1/n}.
  - *Proof:* closed and bounded (AN2.3); the interiors cover Ω, so a compact K is covered by finitely many and lies in one.
- **`def-locally-uniform-metric`.** For f, g ∈ C(Ω), ρ(f, g) = Σₙ 2^{−n} min(1, ‖f − g‖_{Kₙ}).
- **`prp-metric-locally-uniform`.** ρ is a metric on C(Ω), and ρ(fₖ, f) → 0 iff fₖ → f uniformly on every compact subset of Ω.
  - *Proof sketch:* given ε, the tail Σ_{n>N} 2^{−n} < ε/2, and uniform convergence on K_N handles the head. Conversely, a compact K lies in some K_N, and min(1, ‖·‖_{K_N}) ≤ 2^N ρ.
  - Say that the metric depends on the exhaustion but its convergent sequences do not. That is all the chapter uses.
- **`thm-hol-closed`.** H(Ω), the holomorphic functions on Ω, is a closed subset of the complete metric space (C(Ω), ρ). Hence it is complete, and f ↦ f′ is continuous H(Ω) → H(Ω).
  - *Proof:* completeness of C(Ω) as for C(K) (AN2.2: a ρ-Cauchy sequence is uniformly Cauchy on each Kₙ). Closedness and the derivative statement are CA.2's Weierstrass theorem.
- **Failure-motivated compactness.** In ℝⁿ, closed and bounded means compact. In C([0, π]) it does not: sin(nx) is bounded by 1 and has no uniformly convergent subsequence (AN2.5's example). So what replaces "bounded"?
  - **`def-normal-family`.** A family ℱ ⊂ H(Ω) is normal if every sequence in ℱ has a subsequence converging locally uniformly on Ω (to a function in H(Ω), not necessarily in ℱ).
  - **`def-locally-bounded`.** ℱ is locally bounded if for every compact K ⊂ Ω, sup_{f∈ℱ} ‖f‖_K < ∞ (equivalently, each point has a neighbourhood where ℱ is uniformly bounded).
  - Normal means relatively compact in (H(Ω), ρ). State it; the proof is one line using sequential compactness (AN2.3).
- **`lem-local-bound-equicontinuous`.** A locally bounded ℱ ⊂ H(Ω) is equicontinuous on each compact K ⊂ Ω.
  - *Proof:* let δ = dist(K, ℂ ∖ Ω)/2 and K′ = {z : dist(z, K) ≤ δ}, compact in Ω. Cauchy's estimate (CA.2) gives |f′| ≤ M_{K′}/δ on K. For |z − w| < δ with z, w ∈ K, the segment lies in K′, so |f(z) − f(w)| ≤ (M_{K′}/δ)|z − w| after integrating f′ along it, using the bound of f′ on the δ/2-neighbourhood. **Drafter:** set up the radii so the segment stays where the derivative bound holds; S2a checks the constants.
- **`thm-montel`.** A family ℱ ⊂ H(Ω) is normal iff it is locally bounded.
  - *Proof Idea (fading: the diagonal argument had full Scratch Work in AN2.5):* on each Kₙ, Arzelà–Ascoli (AN2.5) applies by `lem-local-bound-equicontinuous`. Extract nested subsequences for K₁, K₂, … and take the diagonal. The limit is holomorphic by `thm-hol-closed`.
  - *Converse:* if ℱ is not bounded on some compact K, pick fₖ with ‖fₖ‖_K > k. No subsequence converges uniformly on K, since a uniform limit on a compact set is bounded.
- **`thm-vitali`.** Let ℱ ⊂ H(Ω) be locally bounded, Ω a region, and (fₖ) ⊂ ℱ. If fₖ(z) converges for every z in a set with a limit point in Ω, then fₖ converges locally uniformly on Ω.
  - *Proof:* by Montel, every subsequence has a further subsequence converging locally uniformly. All these limits agree on the set, so they are equal (CA.2: identity theorem). A sequence in a metric space all of whose subsequences have sub-subsequences converging to the same g converges to g.
  - Make that last step `lem-subsubsequence`, a two-line lemma.
- **Recall `cor-hurwitz-injective` (CA.4).** If the fₖ are injective on a region and fₖ → f locally uniformly, then f is injective or constant. CA.4 stated it in the form needed here; §5 uses it.
- **Weird examples** (table):
  - {zⁿ} is normal on 𝔻 (bounded by 1, converges to 0). On a disc around 1 it is not: inside 𝔻 it tends to 0, outside it is unbounded, so no subsequence converges uniformly on any compact set that meets both sides. It is not even normal in the extended sense that allows convergence to ∞.
  - {sin(nx)} on [0, π] is bounded with no convergent subsequence. The holomorphic extensions sin(nz) satisfy |sin(niy)| = |sinh(ny)| → ∞, so they are not locally bounded on any disc meeting the real segment. Boundedness on a real segment says nothing about a complex neighbourhood.
- **Named move: Normality from a bound.**
  - **Worked example.** ℱ = {f ∈ H(𝔻) : Re f > 0, f(0) = 1} is normal. Here (f − 1)/(f + 1) maps 𝔻 → 𝔻 and fixes 0, so Schwarz gives |(f − 1)/(f + 1)| ≤ |z|, and hence |f(z)| ≤ (1 + |z|)/(1 − |z|). Then Montel.
- **Forward hook (prose, to CA.14).** {nz} is not normal on 𝔻, but {f ∈ H(𝔻) : f omits 0 and 1} turns out to be normal once convergence to ∞ is allowed. That is a far stronger theorem, and the route to it goes through Bloch's theorem in CA.14. The bound no longer comes from Schwarz alone.
- **Quick checks.**
  - Q1. Is {z/n} normal on ℂ? Is {nz²} normal on 𝔻? *Answer:* yes (→ 0, locally bounded); no (unbounded at every z ≠ 0).
  - Q2 (quantifier swap, STYLE §10). Write "ℱ is locally bounded" with quantifiers, then swap "∀f ∈ ℱ" and "∃M". What notion do you get, and which family on 𝔻 separates the two? *Answer:* "each f is bounded on K", which every continuous f satisfies on a compact set. {nz} separates them.
- **Warning.** A normal family's limits need not belong to the family. {z/n} has limit 0, and z/n is injective while 0 is not. This is why `cor-hurwitz-injective` allows "or constant", and why §5 must rule the constant out.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Show ρ(zᵐ, 0) → 0 in H(𝔻) as m → ∞, while sup_𝔻 |zᵐ| = 1. *Answer:* each Kₙ lies in |z| ≤ 1 − 1/n, where ‖zᵐ‖ ≤ (1 − 1/n)ᵐ → 0; the tail is small.
  - **A2.** Is {f ∈ H(𝔻) : |f(0)| ≤ 1} normal? *Answer:* no; take fₖ(z) = kz.
  - **B1.** Let fₖ: 𝔻 → 𝔻 be holomorphic with fₖ(x) → 0 for every x ∈ (0, 1). Show fₖ → 0 locally uniformly. *Answer:* `thm-vitali` with the bound 1.
  - **B2.** Show {f ∈ H(𝔻) : f(𝔻) ⊂ ℂ ∖ (−∞, 0], |f(0)| ≤ 1} is normal.
    - *Answer:* g = √f has Re g > 0 and |g(0)| ≤ 1. With c = g(0), h = (g − c)/(g + c̄) maps into 𝔻 and fixes 0. [Check: |g − c|² − |g + c̄|² = −4 Re c Re g < 0.]
    - Schwarz gives |h| ≤ |z|. Then g = (c + c̄h)/(1 − h), so |g| ≤ (1 + |z|)/(1 − |z|) and |f| ≤ ((1 + |z|)/(1 − |z|))².
  - **B3.** Is the family in B2 still normal without |f(0)| ≤ 1? *Answer:* no; the constants k ∈ ℕ. (Normal into Ĉ: the CA.14 hook.)
  - **C1.** Show {f ∈ H(𝔻) : ∬_𝔻 |f|² dA ≤ 1} is normal.
    - *Answer:* area mean value: f(z) = (1/πr²)∬_{D(z,r)} f dA. Integrate the circle mean value property (CA.2) in polar coordinates (b AN3.4).
    - Cauchy–Schwarz gives |f(z)| ≤ 1/(√π r). On |z| ≤ 1 − δ take r = δ, then apply Montel.
  - **C2.** Let ℱ ⊂ H(Ω) be locally bounded. Show {f′ : f ∈ ℱ} is locally bounded, and that the converse fails. *Answer:* Cauchy estimates; the constants {k}.

## 05-riemann-mapping.md — The Riemann mapping theorem

**Budget:** ≤ 5,000 main-line words (the longest section; the proof is the chapter's key proof, STYLE §11.2).

- **Hook paid.** Question 1 of the chapter hook.
- **`thm-riemann-mapping`.** Let Ω ⊊ ℂ be a simply connected region and z₀ ∈ Ω. Then there is a unique conformal map f of Ω onto 𝔻 with f(z₀) = 0 and f′(z₀) > 0.
  - Uniqueness is `cor-riemann-map-unique` (§3).
  - **Hypotheses**, each with what fails without it:
    - Ω ≠ ℂ: `thm-plane-not-disc`;
    - simply connected: 𝔻 ∖ {0} (below);
    - open and connected: built into "region".
- **Scratch Work (first appearance of the extremal-problem method).** Among all injective holomorphic g: Ω → 𝔻 with g(z₀) = 0, the onto one should be the one that "spreads Ω out most" near z₀, so maximize |g′(z₀)|. Three things must hold:
  1. there is at least one such g;
  2. the supremum is attained, by compactness (§4);
  3. a maximizer that misses a point of 𝔻 can be beaten.
  - Write the beating step as scratch first, inside 𝔻. Suppose f(Ω) ⊂ 𝔻 omits α. Then φ_α∘f has no zero and has a square root. Square roots *expand* near 0, because z ↦ z² contracts (Schwarz, non-rotation). Undoing the normalization shows the new map has a larger derivative.
  - Display the chain of maps. The scratch ends with the computation |ψ′(0)| = (1 + |α|)/(2√|α|) > 1, done before the formal proof, so the proof can quote it. **Drafter:** derive this constant in a sidecar and check it with sympy.
- **Proof** (formal, in four steps; each step's name appears as a bold run-in head):
  1. **The family is non-empty.**
     - Pick a ∉ Ω. Then z − a has no zero on Ω, so it has a holomorphic square root q (`cor-simply-connected-roots`, CA.4).
     - q is injective, and q(z) = −q(w) is impossible. (Squaring gives z = w, and then q(z) = 0, which cannot happen.) So q(Ω) and −q(Ω) are disjoint.
     - q(Ω) is open (`thm-open-mapping`), so −q(Ω) contains a disc D(w₀, r) that q(Ω) avoids. Hence z ↦ r/(q(z) − w₀) is injective and bounded by 1. Compose with a φ_b to send z₀ to 0.
  2. **The supremum is finite and attained.**
     - ℱ = {g : Ω → 𝔻 injective holomorphic, g(z₀) = 0} is bounded by 1, so it is normal (`thm-montel`).
     - Cauchy's estimate bounds |g′(z₀)|, so s = sup |g′(z₀)| < ∞ and s > 0 by step 1. Take gₖ with |gₖ′(z₀)| → s and pass to a subsequence with gₖ → f locally uniformly (`thm-montel`).
     - Then f′(z₀) has modulus s > 0 (`thm-hol-closed`), so f is not constant, and f is injective (`cor-hurwitz-injective`).
     - |f| ≤ 1, and f is open (`thm-open-mapping`), so f(Ω) ⊂ 𝔻. f(z₀) = 0. So f ∈ ℱ.
  3. **The maximizer is onto.**
     - Suppose α ∈ 𝔻 ∖ f(Ω). Then φ_α∘f has no zero, so it has a holomorphic square root h (`cor-simply-connected-roots`), injective and into 𝔻.
     - Let β = h(z₀) (so β² = −α) and set G = φ_β∘h ∈ ℱ.
     - Then f = ψ∘G with ψ = φ_{−α}∘sq∘φ_{−β}, where sq(w) = w². ψ maps 𝔻 → 𝔻, fixes 0, and is not injective (sq is 2-to-1), so it is not a rotation.
     - Schwarz gives |ψ′(0)| < 1, so |f′(z₀)| = |ψ′(0)||G′(z₀)| < |G′(z₀)| ≤ s, which is a contradiction.
  4. **Normalize.** Multiply by a unimodular constant to make f′(z₀) > 0.
  - Make step 3 not depend on the scratch constant: strictness from "ψ is not a rotation" suffices. The explicit constant is kept as a remark.
- **Where simple connectivity was used.** Only in steps 1 and 3, and only to take square roots of non-vanishing functions. Remark: so the theorem holds for every region Ω ≠ ℂ on which every non-vanishing holomorphic function has a square root. This is the starting point for the equivalences of simple connectivity in CA.12 (forward pointer, prose).
- **`cor-simply-connected-equivalent`.** Any two simply connected regions, neither equal to ℂ, are conformally equivalent. Every simply connected region is homeomorphic to 𝔻.
  - *Proof:* compose Riemann maps; for ℂ, use the homeomorphism of the hook.
- **Weird example (necessity of simple connectivity): `prp-punctured-disc`.** 𝔻 ∖ {0} is not conformally equivalent to 𝔻 or to any annulus {r < |w| < 1}, 0 < r < 1.
  - *Proof:* suppose f: 𝔻 ∖ {0} → V is conformal with V bounded. Then 0 is removable (CA.3), and f extends to 𝔻.
  - If f(0) ∈ V, then f(0) = f(z₁) for some z₁ ≠ 0. Neighbourhoods of 0 and of z₁ have overlapping open images (CA.4), which contradicts injectivity on 𝔻 ∖ {0}.
  - So f(0) ∈ ∂V. But f(𝔻) is open, contains f(0), and lies in V ∪ {f(0)}. When V is 𝔻 or an annulus, no point of ∂V has a neighbourhood inside V ∪ {that point}, so this is a contradiction.
  - (For V = 𝔻 this is quicker by topology: 𝔻 ∖ {0} is not simply connected. Give both and say which generalizes.)
- **Hook 2 paid: `thm-annuli-inequivalent`.** For 0 < r, s < 1, the annuli A_r = {r < |z| < 1} and A_s are conformally equivalent iff r = s.
  - *Proof sketch:*
    1. **Boundary components go to boundary components.** Let f: A_r → A_s be conformal and L = f⁻¹({|w| = √s}), a compact set, so L ⊂ {r + δ ≤ |z| ≤ 1 − δ}. The connected sets {1 − δ < |z| < 1} and {r < |z| < r + δ} map into A_s ∖ {|w| = √s}, each into one component. They cannot both go to the same component: the other component would then be covered by the image of the compact middle annulus, so its closure in ℂ would lie in A_s, which is false. Replacing f by s/f if necessary, |f(z)| → 1 as |z| → 1 and |f(z)| → s as |z| → r.
    2. **A harmonic function that vanishes at both ends.** u = log|f| − α log|z| with α = log s/log r is harmonic (CA.6) and tends to 0 at both boundary circles. By the maximum principle (CA.6), u ≡ 0.
    3. **The winding number forces α = 1.** From log|f| = α log|z|, differentiate (∂/∂z of log|f| is f′/(2f)) to get zf′(z)/f(z) ≡ α. Then α = (1/2πi)∮_{|z|=ρ} f′/f dz is an integer (CA.4: winding number of f∘circle about 0). Since α > 0, f(z) = cz^α, and injectivity forces α = 1, so s = r.
  - **S2a:** check step 1's topology carefully; it is the delicate part.
  - Say in prose that the conformal invariant is the ratio of radii, the *modulus*. So conformal type keeps a real number that topology forgets.
- **Forward pointer (prose).** For f the Riemann map with f(z₀) = 0, the function −log|f| is harmonic on Ω ∖ {z₀}, tends to 0 at ∂Ω in a sense made precise in CA.11, and has a log singularity at z₀. It is the Green's function, used in CA.11.
- **Quick checks.**
  - Q1. Which of these are conformally equivalent to 𝔻: ℂ ∖ (−∞, 0]; ℂ ∖ {0}; ℂ; 𝔻 ∖ [0, 1); the strip {0 < Im z < 1}? *Answer:* yes, no (not simply connected), no (Liouville), yes, yes.
  - Q2. In the proof, where is Ω ≠ ℂ used? *Answer:* step 1, to pick a ∉ Ω.
- **Warning.** The proof is not constructive. Step 2's maximizer exists by compactness, and no formula comes with it. §2's explicit maps are the only ones written down in this chapter.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Find the unique conformal f: ℍ → 𝔻 with f(i) = 0 and f′(i) > 0. *Answer:* f(z) = i(z − i)/(z + i). The Cayley map has derivative 2i/(z + i)², which is −i/2 at i, and multiplying by i makes it 1/2.
  - **A2.** Find the Riemann map of the strip {|Im z| < π/2}, the inverse direction, f: 𝔻 → strip with f(0) = 0 and f′(0) > 0. *Answer:* f(z) = log((1 + z)/(1 − z)), with f′(0) = 2.
  - **B1.** Let f be the Riemann map of Ω at z₀ (f(z₀) = 0, f′(z₀) > 0). Show |g′(z₀)| ≤ f′(z₀) for every injective holomorphic g: Ω → 𝔻 with g(z₀) = 0, with equality iff g = e^{iθ}f. *Answer:* g∘f⁻¹: 𝔻 → 𝔻 fixes 0; apply Schwarz.
  - **B2.** Let Ω ⊊ ℂ be simply connected and symmetric under z ↦ z̄, and let x₀ ∈ Ω ∩ ℝ. Show that the Riemann map at x₀ commutes with conjugation. *Answer:* z ↦ conj f(z̄) is again a normalized Riemann map at x₀; apply uniqueness. (Original wording required: this is a standard fact and appears in several sources.)
  - **C1** (curriculum exit check). Prove the Riemann mapping theorem unaided from Montel, Hurwitz and Schwarz.
    - *Marking scheme:* non-empty family, 2 marks; normality and finite supremum, 2; limit injective and into 𝔻, 2; competitor via square root with |ψ′(0)| < 1, 3; normalization, 1.
  - **C2.** Show there is no conformal map of 𝔻 ∖ {0} onto V = 𝔻 ∖ {0, 1/2}. *Answer:* suppose f is one, with inverse g. Both are bounded, so both extend across the punctures (CA.3).
    - As in `prp-punctured-disc`, f(0) is not in V. Since f(𝔻) is open and lies in V ∪ {f(0)}, f(0) is an isolated boundary point of V, so f(0) = p ∈ {0, 1/2}. Let q be the other puncture.
    - Then g(p) = lim g(f(z)) = 0. Also g(q) ∈ closure of 𝔻 ∖ {0}, and by the same argument applied to g, g(q) = 0.
    - So the extended g sends p and q to 0. Small discs about p and q map onto open neighbourhoods of 0 (CA.4), which overlap at points g(z₁) = g(z₂) with z₁ ≠ z₂ in V. This contradicts injectivity of g on V.

## 06-reflection.md — The reflection principle and boundary behaviour

**Budget:** ≤ 4,500 main-line words.

- **Hook paid.** Question 3 of the chapter hook. The §2 maps visibly continue across straight edges: the Cayley map is a Möbius map of all of Ĉ. Is that an accident of explicit formulas? The reflection principle says no.
- **Setting.** Ω is a region symmetric under z ↦ z̄. Write Ω⁺ = Ω ∩ ℍ, σ = Ω ∩ ℝ, Ω⁻ = conj(Ω⁺).
- **Scratch Work (first appearance of "reflect and identify").** We want to extend v harmonic on Ω⁺, with v = 0 on σ, by v(z̄) = −v(z).
  - The extension V is obviously harmonic off σ. The question is only at points of σ.
  - V is continuous on Ω (it is 0 on σ from both sides). CA.6 left a tool for exactly this situation: a continuous function with the mean value property is harmonic (`thm-mvp-implies-harmonic`).
  - So check the mean value property on small circles. Off σ it holds because V is harmonic there (`thm-harmonic-mvp`). On a circle centred at x ∈ σ, the upper and lower halves cancel because V is odd, so the mean is 0 = V(x).
  - The scratch names the trick: we never compute ΔV at σ. We identify V as harmonic through a characterization that needs no derivatives.
- **`thm-reflection-harmonic`.** Let v be continuous on Ω⁺ ∪ σ, harmonic on Ω⁺, and zero on σ. Then V, defined as v on Ω⁺ ∪ σ and as −v(z̄) on Ω⁻, is harmonic on Ω.
  - *Proof:* the scratch, written formally, citing `thm-harmonic-mvp` and `thm-mvp-implies-harmonic` (CA.6).
  - **S2a:** confirm that CA.6's `def-mean-value-property` is the *local* one (for each point, all sufficiently small circles), which is what the odd extension has. Discs centred off σ that meet σ are not checked directly. If CA.6 states the global version, either CA.6 adds the local version or this proof uses Ahlfors's route: on a disc D centred at x ∈ σ, the Poisson integral P of V|∂D (`thm-schwarz-dirichlet-disc`) is odd, so it vanishes on the diameter, and P − V = 0 on each half-disc by `cor-harmonic-max-principle`. Coordinator note filed.
  - CA.6's index hook ("holomorphic on the upper half-disc with Im f → 0 on the diameter: can f be continued?") is paid by the next theorem, and the text says so.
  - The hypothesis v = 0 on σ is needed: v = 1 on ℍ has odd extension ±1, which is not continuous on ℝ.
- **`thm-reflection-holomorphic`.** Let f be holomorphic on Ω⁺, with Im f extending continuously to Ω⁺ ∪ σ, and Im f = 0 on σ. Then f extends to a holomorphic function on Ω with f(z̄) = conj f(z).
  - *Proof sketch* (Ahlfors's route):
    - Apply `thm-reflection-harmonic` to v = Im f to get V.
    - On a disc D about x ∈ σ, take a harmonic conjugate −U of V, normalized so that U = Re f at one point of D⁺ (CA.6: conjugates exist on discs). Then F = U + iV is holomorphic on D.
    - F − f has zero imaginary part on D⁺, so it is a real constant (open mapping or Cauchy–Riemann), and the normalization makes it 0.
    - So F extends f across D ∩ ℝ. G(z) = conj F(z̄) is holomorphic on D and agrees with F on the real diameter, because there Im F = V = 0 makes F real. By the identity theorem (CA.2), F(z̄) = conj F(z) on D.
    - The local extensions agree on overlaps (identity theorem), and they glue.
  - **Remark (one paragraph, prose):** if f itself extends continuously to σ with real values, Morera's theorem (CA.2) gives the result more quickly (S&S's route). The harmonic route needs only Im f, and it is the version §6 uses below.
- **`cor-reflection-circle`.** Circle version. Let C be a circle or line, and let Ω be a region symmetric under reflection z ↦ z* in C (CA.10's symmetry). Let f be holomorphic on Ω ∩ (one side of C), and suppose f(z) tends to a second circle or line C′ as z → Ω ∩ C. Then f extends holomorphically to Ω by f(z*) = (f(z))*′, where *′ is reflection in C′.
  - *Proof:* conjugate by Möbius maps sending C and C′ to ℝ (CA.10) and apply `thm-reflection-holomorphic`.
  - The drafter states precisely what "tends to C′" means: the distance from f(z) to C′ → 0, locally uniformly. Under the Möbius normalization this becomes "Im → 0", which is the theorem's hypothesis.
- **Worked example 1.** Reflection across |z − 1| = 1. Since z* = 1 + 1/conj(z − 1), the point 2i reflects to 4/5 + (2/5)i. Check: |z − 1||z* − 1| = 1, and z* lies on the ray from 1 through z.
- **`thm-boundary-arc-extension`.** Let f: Ω → 𝔻 be a conformal map, and let γ ⊂ ∂Ω be an open segment or open circular arc that is a *free boundary arc*: some neighbourhood N of γ has N ∖ γ ⊂ Ω on one side and N ∩ Ω on the other side empty. Then f extends to a holomorphic function on Ω ∪ γ ∪ (the reflection of N ∩ Ω in γ), injective near γ, and it maps γ into the unit circle.
  - *Proof sketch* (Ahlfors 6.1.3; segments only, then Möbius for arcs):
    - Take γ ⊂ ℝ with Ω above it locally. f is proper (as in the annulus proof: f⁻¹ of a compact set is compact), so |f(z)| → 1 as z → γ. Then v = log|f| is harmonic near γ on the Ω-side (f has no zeros near γ) and → 0 at γ.
    - `thm-reflection-harmonic` extends v. A local holomorphic F = log f (CA.4, on a half-disc, which is simply connected) has Re F = v, so iF is holomorphic with Im(iF) = v → 0, and it extends by `thm-reflection-holomorphic`. Then f = exp F extends.
    - Injectivity near γ: f′ ≠ 0 on γ. If f′(x) = 0 for some x ∈ γ, then f would be k-to-1 near x (CA.4) with k ≥ 2, and the half-neighbourhood in Ω would already contain two preimages of some point. **S2a:** check this step; it needs the image of the half-disc under a k-to-1 map to cover a sector of angle kπ.
  - Coverage note: this is Ahlfors's "use of the reflection principle" for analytic arcs, restricted here to segments and circular arcs. The general analytic arc is Exercise C2.
- **Statement only: `rem-caratheodory`.** If ∂Ω is a Jordan curve, the Riemann map extends to a homeomorphism of the closure onto the closed disc. Proved in neither CA nor CA2 (Ahlfors also omits the proof). Say this plainly.
- **Weird example (comb region).** Ω = 𝔻 minus the segments from (1/2)e^{iθₖ} to e^{iθₖ} for a sequence θₖ → 0 (k ≥ 1). This region is simply connected, so it has a Riemann map. Its boundary is not a Jordan curve, and the map does not extend continuously to the closure: points near the slit tips along θ = 0 have no single limit.
  - *Statement only*, one paragraph of intuition.
  - **Drafter:** do not claim more than "does not extend to a homeomorphism of the closure". S2a checks the phrasing.
- **Quick check.** An entire function is real on ℝ. Must f(z̄) = conj f(z) hold? *Answer:* yes: z ↦ conj f(z̄) is entire and agrees with f on ℝ (identity theorem). Reflection is not even needed when f is already entire.
- **Warning.** The reflection principle needs a boundary condition on a *line or circle* segment that the function actually approaches. Holomorphy on Ω⁺ alone gives nothing.
  - Witness: f(z) = √z (principal) on ℍ is real on (0, ∞) and purely imaginary on (−∞, 0). The real-axis reflection extends it across (0, ∞), and only there: not across 0, where it has a branch point.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Find the reflection of 3 in the circle |z| = 2. *Answer:* 4/3.
  - **A2.** Let f be holomorphic on ℍ, continuous on ℍ ∪ ℝ, and real on ℝ, with f(i) = 2 + i. What is f(−i) after reflection? *Answer:* 2 − i.
  - **B1.** Show that the half-disc map of §2 (worked example 1) extends holomorphically to a neighbourhood of the closed half-disc, with derivative non-zero except at ±1, and explain the exceptions by the corners. *Answer:* away from ±1 both edges are free arcs (`thm-boundary-arc-extension`), or argue directly from the formula. Near z = −1, w = ((1 + z)/(1 − z))² ≈ ((1 + z)/2)², which has a double zero. Near z = 1, (w − i)/(w + i) = (1 − i/w)/(1 + i/w), with 1/w = ((1 − z)/(1 + z))² holomorphic and having a double zero at 1. In both cases the composite is holomorphic, and f − f(±1) has a zero of order 2. The right-angle corners are opened to straight angles, which `prp-angle-multiplication` explains (k = 2). S2a: verify both points with sympy.
  - **B2.** Let f be holomorphic on 𝔻, continuous on the closed disc, with |f| = 1 on the circle. Show that f extends to a meromorphic function on ℂ by f(z) = 1/conj f(1/z̄), and conclude that f is rational. *Answer:* `cor-reflection-circle` with C = C′ = the unit circle gives the extension. It is meromorphic on Ĉ (poles at the reflections of the zeros), hence rational (CA.3).
  - **C1.** Continue B2. Show that f is a constant of modulus 1 times a finite Blaschke product ∏ φ_{aⱼ} (zeros aⱼ with multiplicity). *Answer:* f has finitely many zeros in 𝔻. f/B has none and modulus 1 on the circle, so the maximum and minimum modulus principles (CA.2) make it a unimodular constant.
  - **C2.** An *analytic arc* is the image of an interval under a function real-analytic and injective with non-zero derivative (it extends holomorphically and injectively to a neighbourhood of the interval). Extend `thm-boundary-arc-extension` to free analytic arcs. *Answer:* the holomorphic extension maps a symmetric neighbourhood conformally onto a neighbourhood of the arc and sends the real segment to the arc. Compose and apply the segment case.
