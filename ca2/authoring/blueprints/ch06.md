**Purpose.**
- Liouville says a non-constant entire function cannot omit a disc. Casorati–Weierstrass says the image of a punctured neighbourhood of an essential singularity is dense. Both leave room: e^{z} omits one point. This chapter proves the sharp answers. A non-constant entire function omits at most one value (little Picard). Near an essential singularity a function takes every value infinitely often, with at most one exception (great Picard).
- The engine is a quantitative covering theorem: a holomorphic f on the disc with f′(0) = 1 maps some subdisc injectively onto a disc of radius at least 1/12 (Bloch). Combined with a double logarithm that turns "omits 0 and 1" into "omits a set that meets every disc of radius 1", it bounds derivatives, then values (Schottky), then whole families (Montel's fundamental normality test).

**Named moves** (in `index.md`, "Named moves"):
- **Find the good centre.** To find a point where a function behaves as at a normalised point, maximise a weighted size such as (1 − r)·max_{|z|=r}|f′(z)|; at the maximising radius the weight forces a uniform bound on a definite disc around the maximising point.
- **A lower bound on a circle covers a disc.** If g(0) = 0 and |g| ≥ m on |z| = r, then g(B(0, r)) ⊇ B(0, m) (Rouché against the constant w).
- **Logarithms twice.** A function on a simply connected region that omits 0 and 1 is −exp(iπ cosh 2g) for a holomorphic g that omits a set meeting every disc of radius 1.
- **No disc of radius 1 bounds the derivative.** If g is holomorphic on G and g(G) contains no disc of radius 1, Bloch gives |g′(z)| ≤ 12/dist(z, ∂G).
- **Split by the value at a point.** For a family omitting 0 and 1: either |f(a)| ≤ 1, or replace f by 1/f, which also omits 0 and 1 and has |1/f(a)| < 1.
- **Rescale toward the singularity.** Study f near an isolated singularity through the family fₙ(z) = f(z/n) on a fixed annulus or punctured disc; a normal-families conclusion about (fₙ) is a statement about f on a sequence of circles shrinking to the point.

## 01-bloch.md — Bloch's theorem and the constants of Bloch and Landau (≤ 4,500 words)

- **Hook.** If f is holomorphic on 𝔻 and f′(0) = 1, how large a disc must f(𝔻) contain? The open mapping theorem gives *some* disc around f(0), of unknown size. A uniform size would be a strong statement: rescaling an entire function, it would force the image of ℂ to contain discs of every radius. That is what §02 needs.
- **Failure-motivated definition.**
  - First guess: "f(𝔻) contains B(f(0), c) for a universal c > 0." It fails. `exm-bloch-not-centred`: fₙ(z) = (e^{nz} − 1)/n has fₙ(0) = 0, fₙ′(0) = 1 and omits −1/n (e^{nz} ≠ 0), so no disc about 0 of radius ≥ 1/n lies in fₙ(𝔻).
  - Second guess: "f is injective on a disc of universal radius in the domain." It fails too. `exm-bloch-univalent-shrinks`: fₙ(z + 2πi/n) = fₙ(z), and a disc of radius r > π/n contains two points 2πi/n apart, so fₙ is injective on no disc of radius > π/n.
  - So the right question asks for *some* disc in the image, anywhere, and lets the domain disc shrink. `def-bloch-landau-radii`: for f ∈ H(𝔻) with f′(0) = 1, 𝓁(f) = sup{r : f(𝔻) contains an open disc of radius r}; 𝒷(f) = sup{r : f maps some open subset of 𝔻 injectively onto an open disc of radius r}. 𝓛 = inf_f 𝓁(f) (Landau's constant), 𝓑 = inf_f 𝒷(f) (Bloch's constant). Clearly 𝓑 ≤ 𝓛, and f(z) = z gives 𝓛 ≤ 1.
- **`lem-covering-circle` (first appearance of "a lower bound on a circle covers a disc").** Let g be holomorphic on a neighbourhood of B̄(0, r), g(0) = 0, and |g(z)| ≥ m > 0 on |z| = r. Then g(B(0, r)) ⊇ B(0, m). *Proof:* for |w| < m, |(g − w) − g| = |w| < m ≤ |g| on the circle; Rouché (CA.4) gives g − w at least as many zeros in B(0, r) as g, which has one at 0.
- **`lem-injective-convex`.** Let h be holomorphic on a convex open U with Re h′ > 0 on U. Then h is injective on U. *Proof:* h(b) − h(a) = (b − a)∫₀¹ h′(a + t(b − a)) dt, and the integral has positive real part. *Corollary used:* if |h′(z) − c| < |c| on a disc, h is injective there (apply the lemma to h/c).
- **`lem-landau-covering`.** Let g ∈ H(𝔻), g(0) = 0, g′(0) = 1, |g| ≤ M on 𝔻. Then M ≥ 1 and g(B(0, 1/(4M))) ⊇ B(0, 1/(6M)). *Proof sketch:* Cauchy estimates give |aₙ| ≤ M for g(z) = z + Σ_{n≥2} aₙzⁿ, and M ≥ |a₁| = 1. On |z| = r = 1/(4M) ≤ 1/4: |g(z)| ≥ r − Mr²/(1 − r) ≥ r − (4/3)Mr² = 1/(6M). Apply `lem-covering-circle`. (This is the warm-up: no injectivity, but already a definite disc once |g| is bounded. The work in Bloch is to manufacture a bound.)
- **Worked example `exm-sin-covering`.** sin on 𝔻: sin 0 = 0, sin′ 0 = 1, |sin z| ≤ sinh 1 on 𝔻 (from |sin(x + iy)|² = sin²x + sinh²y). So sin(𝔻) ⊇ B(0, 1/(6 sinh 1)), radius ≈ 0.1418. Compare with the true largest disc about 0 in sin(𝔻): its radius is min_{|z|=1}|sin z| (sin is injective on 𝔻, since Re cos z > 0 there by `lem-injective-convex`: Re cos(x + iy) = cos x cosh y > 0 for |x| < 1 < π/2), which a sidecar computes (≈ 0.84, attained at z = ±1). The lemma is crude but uniform. *Sidecar: both numbers.*
- **Scratch Work (first appearance of "find the good centre").** Placed before the proof of `thm-bloch`, separate from it. "Where should the univalent disc be? Near 0 we know only f′(0) = 1, and f′ may be huge nearby (that is what fₙ does). We want a point a where |f′(a)| is large *and* |f′| is not much larger on a disc around a whose radius is comparable to 1/|f′(a)|. Weight |f′| by the distance to the boundary: K(r) = max_{|z|=r}|f′(z)| grows, 1 − r shrinks, and (1 − r)K(r) is 1 at r = 0 and 0 at r = 1. Take the last radius r₀ where it equals 1. Beyond r₀ it is < 1, so on the circle of radius (1 + r₀)/2 we get |f′| < 2/(1 − r₀) = 2|f′(a)|: the bound we wanted, on the disc of radius ρ = (1 − r₀)/2 about a." Then: "Rescale so that disc is 𝔻 and the derivative bound is 1; now Schwarz controls how far g′ strays from g′(0)."
- **`thm-bloch`.** Let f be holomorphic on an open set containing B̄(0, 1), with f′(0) = 1. Then there is an open disc S ⊆ 𝔻 such that f is injective on S and f(S) contains an open disc of radius 1/12. *Proof route (all steps in the typeset register; sidecar checks each inequality):*
  1. K(r) = max_{|z|=r}|f′(z)| is continuous on [0, 1]; φ(r) = (1 − r)K(r) has φ(0) = 1, φ(1) = 0. Let r₀ = max{r ∈ [0, 1] : φ(r) = 1} (a closed non-empty set); r₀ < 1 and, by the intermediate value theorem, φ < 1 on (r₀, 1].
  2. Choose a with |a| = r₀, |f′(a)| = K(r₀) = 1/(1 − r₀). Put ρ = (1 − r₀)/2, so |f′(a)| = 1/(2ρ). For |z − a| < ρ we have |z| < (1 + r₀)/2, so by maximum modulus (CA.2) |f′(z)| ≤ K((1 + r₀)/2) < 1/(1 − (1 + r₀)/2) = 1/ρ.
  3. g(ζ) = f(a + ρζ) − f(a) on 𝔻: g(0) = 0, ψ := g′ satisfies |ψ(0)| = ρ|f′(a)| = 1/2 and |ψ| < 1 on 𝔻. With c = ψ(0): (2/3)(ψ − c) maps 𝔻 into 𝔻 and vanishes at 0, so Schwarz (CA.5) gives |ψ(ζ) − c| ≤ (3/2)|ζ|.
  4. On B(0, 1/3): |ψ − c| < 1/2 = |c|, so g is injective there (`lem-injective-convex`).
  5. For |ζ| = r < 1/3: g(ζ) = cζ + ∫_{[0,ζ]}(ψ − c), so |g(ζ)| ≥ r/2 − (3/4)r². This increases to 1/12 as r ↑ 1/3. Given |w| < 1/12 pick r < 1/3 with r/2 − (3/4)r² > |w|; `lem-covering-circle` puts w in g(B(0, r)). So g(B(0, 1/3)) ⊇ B(0, 1/12).
  6. Undo the rescaling: S = B(a, ρ/3) ⊆ 𝔻 (since r₀ + ρ/3 < 1), f is injective on S, and f(S) = f(a) + g(B(0, 1/3)) ⊇ B(f(a), 1/12).
- **`cor-bloch-open-disc`.** If f ∈ H(𝔻) and f′(0) = 1, then for every t < 1, f maps some open disc in 𝔻 injectively onto a set containing a disc of radius t/12. Hence 𝓑 ≥ 1/12. *Route:* apply `thm-bloch` to f_t(z) = f(tz)/t.
- **`cor-landau-constant`.** 1/12 ≤ 𝓑 ≤ 𝓛 ≤ 1. (Prose: the exact values are unknown; the best known bounds are far from 1/12. No names or history.)
- **`cor-bloch-scaled`.** If f is holomorphic on B(z₀, R) and f′(z₀) ≠ 0, then f(B(z₀, R)) contains open discs of every radius < R|f′(z₀)|/12. *Route:* φ(ζ) = (f(z₀ + Rζ) − f(z₀))/(R f′(z₀)) and `cor-bloch-open-disc`. This is the form §02 and §03 use; give it its own display.
- **Warning.** Bloch says nothing about *where* the disc is: not around f(0) (`exm-bloch-not-centred`), and the domain disc may be tiny (`exm-bloch-univalent-shrinks`). Readers who later write "f(B(z₀, R)) ⊇ B(f(z₀), R|f′(z₀)|/12)" have used a false statement; §02's find-the-error uses a cousin of this slip.
- **Quick checks.**
  1. Why can't we simply apply `lem-landau-covering` to f in `thm-bloch`? *Answer:* f need not be bounded by anything controlled; the lemma's radius 1/(6M) degenerates as M grows. Bloch first *finds* a disc where a bound holds.
  2. In step 2, why is K((1 + r₀)/2) a bound for |f′| on B(a, ρ), when that disc is not a disc about 0? *Answer:* B(a, ρ) ⊆ B(0, (1 + r₀)/2) and maximum modulus on the larger disc.
  3. For f(z) = z, what are 𝓁(f) and 𝒷(f)? For f(z) = 5z, which normalised function do the definitions apply to? *Answer:* both are 1; f(z)/5 = z.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Apply `lem-landau-covering` to g(z) = z + z²/2 on 𝔻. *Answer:* |g| ≤ 3/2, so g(𝔻) ⊇ B(0, 1/9).
  - **A2.** f is holomorphic on B(0, 5) with f′(0) = 3. What does `cor-bloch-scaled` give? *Answer:* f(B(0, 5)) contains discs of every radius < 5/4.
  - **A3.** Show 𝓛 ≤ 1 and 𝓑 ≤ 1. *Answer:* f(z) = z has f(𝔻) = 𝔻, which contains no disc of radius > 1.
  - **B1.** Show that e^{z} is injective on the strip {|Im z| < π}, although Re(e^{−iθ}e^{z}) > 0 fails somewhere on the strip for every θ. So `lem-injective-convex` (even after rotating h′) is sufficient, not necessary. *Answer:* e^{z} = e^{w} ⇒ z − w ∈ 2πiℤ, impossible in the strip unless z = w; arg e^{z} = Im z takes every value in (−π, π).
  - **B2.** Let g ∈ H(𝔻), g(0) = 0, g′(0) = a ≠ 0, |g| ≤ M. Show g(𝔻) ⊇ B(0, |a|²/(6M)). *Answer:* apply `lem-landau-covering` to g/a, which is bounded by M/|a|.
  - **B3.** Fill in step 1 of `thm-bloch`: prove K is continuous and that φ < 1 on (r₀, 1]. *Answer:* K is the maximum over a compact parameter set of a jointly continuous function (|f′(re^{iθ})|), hence continuous; if φ(r₁) ≥ 1 for some r₁ > r₀ then since φ(1) = 0 the IVT gives r ∈ [r₁, 1) with φ(r) = 1, contradicting the maximality of r₀.
  - **C1.** For fₙ(z) = (e^{nz} − 1)/n (n ≥ 1): (a) show that every disc in 𝔻 on which fₙ is injective has radius ≤ π/n; (b) show that fₙ is injective on D = B(1 − 1/n, 1/n) ⊆ 𝔻 and that fₙ(D) contains a disc of radius (e^{n−1} − e^{n−2})/n ≥ 1 − e^{−1} > 1/12. Explain how (a) and (b) square with Bloch's theorem. *Answer:* (a) `exm-bloch-univalent-shrinks`. (b) w = nz maps D onto B(n − 1, 1), where |Im w| < 1 < π, so e^{w} is injective. On |u| = 1, |e^{u} − 1| ≥ 1 − e^{−1} (minimum at u = −1; sidecar), so `lem-covering-circle` gives e^{B(0,1)} ⊇ B(1, 1 − e^{−1}); multiply by e^{n−1}, subtract 1, divide by n: fₙ(D) ⊇ B((e^{n−1} − 1)/n, (e^{n−1} − e^{n−2})/n). ## 02-little-picard.md — Logarithms twice, and the little Picard theorem (≤ 3,500 words)

- **Hook.** Let f be entire and omit 0 and 1. If we could produce an entire g whose image contains no disc of radius 1, `cor-bloch-scaled` on ever larger discs would force g′ ≡ 0. One logarithm is not enough: f = e^{2πiF} and F omits ℤ, but ℤ lies on a line and misses huge discs. We need a function whose omitted set is spread over the whole plane.
- **Try it first (before the construction).** "F omits every integer, so F and F − 1 have square roots. Before reading on: if H = √F − √(F − 1), what is 1/H? What does H omit?" Folded answer: 1/H = √F + √(F − 1), and H never vanishes.
- **Scratch Work (first appearance of "logarithms twice").** "We want g with f expressed through g, and with the values f = 1 corresponding to a 2-dimensional lattice of g-values. cosh is 2-to-1 and periodic in the imaginary direction, so values of cosh 2g pull back to a grid. We need F = (1 + cosh 2g)/2, i.e. cosh 2g = 2F − 1. With H = e^{g}, cosh 2g = (H² + H^{−2})/2, so we need H² + H^{−2} = 4F − 2, i.e. (H + H^{−1})² = 4F. So H + H^{−1} = 2√F, a quadratic for H: H = √F ± √(F − 1)." The proof then runs forward without the search.
- **`lem-double-log`.** Let G be a simply connected region and f ∈ H(G) omit 0 and 1. Then there is g ∈ H(G) with f = −exp(iπ cosh 2g) on G. If f is non-constant, so is g. *Proof:* F = (1/2πi) log f (`cor-roots-and-logs-by-monodromy`, CA.8a), so f = e^{2πiF}; F omits ℤ (F = n ⇒ f = 1). So √F, √(F − 1) exist (same corollary); H = √F − √(F − 1) is zero-free with 1/H = √F + √(F − 1); g = log H. Then H + 1/H = 2√F, cosh 2g = ((H + 1/H)² − 2)/2 = 2F − 1, and f = e^{2πiF} = e^{iπ(1 + cosh 2g)} = −exp(iπ cosh 2g).
- **`lem-omitted-set-dense`.** With g as in `lem-double-log`, g omits Λ = {±aₙ + iπm/2 : n ≥ 1, m ∈ ℤ}, where aₙ = log(√n + √(n − 1)) = arccosh √n. Every open disc of radius 1 meets Λ. *Proof sketch:*
  - If g(z) = ±aₙ + iπm/2 then cosh 2g(z) = (−1)^m cosh 2aₙ = (−1)^m(2n − 1), an odd integer, so f(z) = −exp(iπ·odd) = 1: impossible. (cosh 2aₙ = 2cosh²aₙ − 1 = 2n − 1.)
  - a₁ = 0, aₙ ↑ ∞, and aₙ₊₁ − aₙ ≤ a₂ − a₁ = log(1 + √2) < 0.882, because x ↦ arccosh √x has decreasing derivative 1/(2√(x² − x)) on (1, ∞).
  - So Λ cuts the plane into rectangles of width ≤ 0.882 and height π/2 < 1.571 (the ± covers the left half-plane). Every point is within half a diagonal, √(0.441² + 0.786²) < 0.902 < 1, of a corner. *Sidecar: the bounds.*
- **Remark (CA.8a link, prose).** g is a *lift* of f through the entire function Φ(z) = −exp(iπ cosh 2z): f = Φ ∘ g. CA.8a's `thm-lift-local-homeomorphism` does not apply to Φ, which has critical points (where sinh 2z = 0), so we built g by explicit logarithms and roots, each of which *is* a lift through exp or a power (`cor-roots-and-logs-by-monodromy`). CA.8a §4 C1 (cos g = f) is the same idea with one omitted pair. §05's outline lifts through a true covering, the modular function.
- **`thm-little-picard`.** A non-constant entire function omits at most one complex value. *Proof (the full worked instance of "no disc of radius 1 bounds the derivative"):* if f omits a ≠ b, then (f − a)/(b − a) omits 0 and 1 (`cor-little-picard-two-values` is this reduction stated once). Take g from `lem-double-log` on G = ℂ; g is non-constant, so g′(z₀) ≠ 0 for some z₀. By `cor-bloch-scaled`, g(B(z₀, R)) contains discs of every radius < R|g′(z₀)|/12; take R > 12/|g′(z₀)| to get a disc of radius 1 in g(ℂ), contradicting `lem-omitted-set-dense`.
- **`cor-little-picard-two-values`.** If f is entire and omits two distinct values a, b, it is constant. (The affine reduction, stated so later chapters can cite it.)
- **Worked example `exm-exp-breaks-double-log`.** Run the construction on e^{z}, which omits only 0. F = z/(2πi) takes the value 0 at z = 0 and 1 at z = 2πi, so neither √F nor √(F − 1) exists on ℂ: the construction breaks exactly at the omitted value we do not have.
- **Find-the-error (true conclusion; STYLE §11.3).** "Let f be entire and omit 0 and 1. Then f = e^{h} with h entire, and h omits 2πiℤ because f omits 1. If h were non-constant, `cor-bloch-scaled` would give discs of every radius in h(ℂ); a disc of radius 10 must contain a point of 2πiℤ, so h is constant." The conclusion is true (little Picard). The failing line is "a disc of radius 10 must contain a point of 2πiℤ": the omitted set lies on the imaginary axis, and B(20, 10) avoids it. Counterexample to the step, not to the conclusion: B(20, 10) ∩ 2πiℤ = ∅. The repair is exactly `lem-double-log`: the omitted set must meet *every* disc of a fixed radius.
- **Quick checks.**
  1. Check one point of Λ by hand: for n = 2, m = 1, compute cosh 2g and f. *Answer:* cosh(2a₂ + iπ) = −3, f = −e^{−3πi} = 1.
  2. Does cos z omit any value? *Answer:* no; cos z = w is a quadratic in e^{iz} whose roots have product 1, so a non-zero root exists.
  3. Why does the proof of `thm-little-picard` need G = ℂ only at the last step? *Answer:* `lem-double-log` works on any simply connected G; only "take R as large as we like" uses G = ℂ. §03 runs the same argument with R fixed.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Show that a non-constant polynomial omits no value, and that e^{z} omits exactly 0. Which values does e^{z²} omit? *Answer:* fundamental theorem of algebra; e^{z} = w ≠ 0 solvable; e^{z²} omits exactly 0 (z² takes every value).
  - **A2.** List the points of Λ in the rectangle 0 ≤ Re z ≤ 1.2, 0 ≤ Im z ≤ 2. *Answer:* real parts a₁ = 0, a₂ = log(1 + √2) ≈ 0.881, a₃ = arccosh √3 ≈ 1.146 (sidecar); imaginary parts 0 and π/2; six points.
  - **B1.** Let f, g be entire with e^{f} + e^{g} = 1. Show f and g are constant. *Answer:* e^{f} omits 0, and omits 1 since e^{g} ≠ 0.
  - **B2.** Show without Picard that an entire f with f(ℂ) ∩ (−∞, 0] = ∅ is constant. *Answer:* the principal √f has Re √f > 0, so 1/(1 + √f) is bounded and entire; Liouville.
  - **B3.** Let P be a polynomial with at least two distinct roots and f entire with P(f(z)) ≠ 0 for all z. Show f is constant. What if P = (z − a)^k? *Answer:* f omits the roots; for P = (z − a)^k, f = a + e^{h} works with any entire h.
  - **C1.** Let f be entire and suppose f ∘ f has no fixed point. Show f(z) = z + b with b ≠ 0. *Answer outline:* f has no fixed point, so q = (f(f(z)) − z)/(f(z) − z) is entire, omits 0 (numerator ≠ 0) and 1 (q = 1 ⇒ f(f(z)) = f(z), a fixed point f(z) of f). So q ≡ c ∉ {0, 1}. Then f ∘ f = α²z + (α + 1)b has no fixed point only if α² = 1 and (α + 1)b ≠ 0, i.e. α = 1, b ≠ 0. **Marking scheme.** (10 points) 2: q entire (f has no fixed point). 3: q omits 0 and 1, both reasons. 2: differentiation and f′ ∘ f omits 0 and c. 2: f′ constant via openness and identity theorem. 1: α = 1, b ≠ 0. Fatal errors: concluding f′ constant from "f′ ∘ f constant" without openness of f(ℂ); treating q as omitting 1 without the fixed-point argument.

## 03-schottky-landau.md — Schottky's theorem and Landau's radius (≤ 3,500 words)

- **Hook.** On a disc we cannot let R → ∞; the same argument then gives a *bound*. A function on 𝔻 omitting 0 and 1 cannot be large near the centre unless it is already large at the centre: one value controls all values on a smaller disc. This is the estimate §04 turns into compactness.
- **`lem-bloch-derivative-bound` (worked instance of the faded move, in full).** Let G be open, g ∈ H(G), and suppose g(G) contains no open disc of radius 1. Then |g′(z)| ≤ 12/dist(z, ∂G) for every z ∈ G (dist(z, ∂G) = ∞ allowed only if g′ ≡ 0). *Proof Idea, then Proof:* for R < dist(z, ∂G), `cor-bloch-scaled` on B(z, R) gives discs of every radius < R|g′(z)|/12 in g(G); none has radius 1, so R|g′(z)| ≤ 12; let R ↑ dist(z, ∂G).
- **`thm-schottky`.** For α > 0 and 0 ≤ β < 1 there is a constant C(α, β) such that: if f ∈ H(𝔻) omits 0 and 1 and |f(0)| ≤ α, then |f(z)| ≤ C(α, β) for |z| ≤ β. One admissible choice, with ℓ(α) = max(log α, log 2), C₀(α) = ℓ(α)/(2π) + 1, C₁(α) = log(2√(C₀(α) + 1)) + π:
  C(α, β) = 1 + exp(π exp(2 C₁(max(α, 3/2)) + 24 log(1/(1 − β)))).
  *Proof Idea:* bound g(0) by f(0), bound g′ by `lem-bloch-derivative-bound`, integrate, and read off f = −exp(iπ cosh 2g). *Proof route:*
  1. **Case |f(0)| ≥ 1/2.** Choose log f(0) with imaginary part in [0, 2π): |F(0)| ≤ |log|f(0)||/(2π) + 1 ≤ C₀(α). Then |√F(0)| ≤ √C₀, |√(F(0) − 1)| ≤ √(C₀ + 1), so |H(0)| and |1/H(0)| are both ≤ 2√(C₀ + 1), hence |log|H(0)|| ≤ log(2√(C₀ + 1)). Choose Im g(0) ∈ (−π, π]: |g(0)| ≤ C₁(α).
  2. g(𝔻) misses Λ, so contains no disc of radius 1 (`lem-omitted-set-dense`); `lem-bloch-derivative-bound` gives |g′(z)| ≤ 12/(1 − |z|). Integrating along [0, z]: |g(z) − g(0)| ≤ 12 log(1/(1 − |z|)).
  3. |f(z)| = exp(Re(iπ cosh 2g)) ≤ exp(π|cosh 2g(z)|) ≤ exp(π e^{2|g(z)|}), which gives the bound with C₁(α).
  4. **Case |f(0)| < 1/2.** 1 − f omits 0 and 1, and 1/2 < |1 − f(0)| ≤ 3/2; apply the first case with α replaced by 3/2 and use |f| ≤ 1 + |1 − f|. The stated C(α, β) covers both cases (it uses max(α, 3/2) and adds 1).
- **Worked example `exm-schottky-numbers`.** α = 2, β = 1/2: ℓ = log 2, C₀ ≈ 1.110, C₁ ≈ 4.208, |g| ≤ 4.208 + 12 log 2 ≈ 12.53 on |z| ≤ 1/2, and C(2, 1/2) is about exp(π e^{25.1}): an astronomically bad but honest constant. The point is that it depends only on α and β. *Sidecar: all numbers.* Prose: much better constants exist; we need only existence.
- **`cor-schottky-disc`.** Let f be holomorphic on B(a, r), omit 0 and 1, with |f(a)| ≤ α. Then |f(z)| ≤ C(α, β) for |z − a| ≤ βr. (Rescale z ↦ a + rz. This is the form §04 uses.)
- **`exm-schottky-blowup`.** f(z) = exp((1 + z)/(1 − z)) on 𝔻. Re((1 + z)/(1 − z)) > 0, so |f| > 1 and f omits 0 and 1 (e^{w} = 1 forces Re w = 0). f(0) = e, and f(β) = exp((1 + β)/(1 − β)) → ∞ as β → 1. So any Schottky constant must blow up as β → 1, at least like exp(2/(1 − β)) (compare our bound, a double exponential of (1 − β)^{−24}). And `exm-constants-not-normal` with fₙ ≡ n shows the hypothesis |f(0)| ≤ α cannot be dropped; e^{nz}, which omits only 0, has e^{n·0} = 1 and is unbounded on |z| ≤ β, so "omits two values" cannot be weakened to one.
- **`thm-landau-radius`.** For a₀ ∉ {0, 1} and a₁ ≠ 0 there is R(a₀, a₁) < ∞ such that: if f is holomorphic on B(0, R), omits 0 and 1, f(0) = a₀ and f′(0) = a₁, then R ≤ R(a₀, a₁). *Proof route:* differentiate cosh 2g = 2F − 1: g′ = F′/sinh 2g, with sinh 2g ≠ 0 (else cosh 2g = ±1, F ∈ {0, 1}) and F′ = f′/(2πi f). So |g′(0)| = |a₁|/(2π|a₀||sinh 2g(0)|) ≥ |a₁|/(2π|a₀|e^{2|g(0)|}). `lem-bloch-derivative-bound` on B(0, R) gives R ≤ 12/|g′(0)|. With the branch choices of `thm-schottky` step 1 (if |a₀| ≥ 1/2) or for 1 − f (else, replacing a₀ by 1 − a₀ and a₁ by −a₁): R ≤ 24π|a₀|e^{2C₁(|a₀|)}/|a₁| in the first case. Remark: letting R = ∞ gives little Picard again (translate a point with f′ ≠ 0 to 0).
- **Quick checks.**
  1. For g as in step 2, what bound does `lem-bloch-derivative-bound` give at z = 1/2, and for |g(1/2) − g(0)|? *Answer:* 24; 12 log 2.
  2. Which hypothesis of `thm-schottky` does fₙ ≡ n violate, and which does e^{nz} violate? *Answer:* |f(0)| ≤ α; omitting two values.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** State `cor-schottky-disc` for B(3i, 2) and β = 1/4. *Answer:* |f| ≤ C(α, 1/4) on B̄(3i, 1/2).
  - **A2.** Show that for f as in `exm-schottky-blowup`, |f(z)| ≤ exp((1 + β)/(1 − β)) on |z| ≤ β. *Answer:* Re((1 + z)/(1 − z)) = (1 − |z|²)/|1 − z|², maximal at z = β on the circle |z| = β, then maximum modulus.
  - **B1.** Prove little Picard from `thm-landau-radius`. *Answer:* if f is entire, non-constant and omits 0, 1, translate so f′(0) ≠ 0 and f(0) ∉ {0, 1}; then f is holomorphic on B(0, R) for every R, contradicting R ≤ R(a₀, a₁).
  - **B2 (faded, second appearance: last step blank).** Let g be holomorphic on the right half-plane P = {Re z > 0} with g(P) containing no disc of radius 1. "By `cor-bloch-scaled` on B(z, R) with R < Re z, g(P) contains discs of every radius < R|g′(z)|/12, so … ." *Name the move, then complete it.* *Answer:* no disc of radius 1 bounds the derivative: R|g′(z)| ≤ 12 for all R < Re z, so |g′(z)| ≤ 12/Re z.
  - **C1.** Show that Schottky's theorem implies: the family 𝓕_α = {f ∈ H(𝔻) : f omits 0 and 1, |f(0)| ≤ α} is normal in H(𝔻). *Answer:* locally bounded by `thm-schottky` (every compact set lies in some |z| ≤ β), then Montel (CA.5). **Marking scheme.** (10 points) 4: compact K ⊆ B̄(0, β) for some β < 1, with reason. 3: uniform bound C(α, β) on K for all of 𝓕_α. 3: Montel's theorem with its hypothesis named (local boundedness). Fatal errors: using Schottky without the normalisation |f(0)| ≤ α; claiming a bound on all of 𝔻.

## 04-montel-fundamental.md — Montel's fundamental normality test (≤ 3,500 words)

- **Hook.** §03's C1 gave normality only for families with |f(0)| bounded. Drop that and the constants fₙ ≡ n (`exm-constants-not-normal`) omit 0 and 1 but have no convergent subsequence in H(G). Yet they do converge, to ∞, uniformly. The definition, not the family, is at fault.
- **Failure-motivated definition `def-normal-extended`.** 𝓕 ⊆ H(G) is *normal in the extended sense* if every sequence in 𝓕 has a subsequence that either converges uniformly on compact subsets of G to some function in H(G), or converges to ∞ uniformly on compact subsets: for every compact K ⊆ G and M > 0 there is N with |fₙ| > M on K for n ≥ N. In symbols as well (STYLE §10). Remark (prose, see CA.10): this is normality for maps into the Riemann sphere with the chordal metric, restricted to holomorphic maps; we do not need that language.
  - *Quick check (quantifiers shown):* write "fₙ → ∞ uniformly on compact subsets of G" in symbols and negate it. *Answer:* ∀K compact ⊆ G ∀M > 0 ∃N ∀n ≥ N ∀z ∈ K: |fₙ(z)| > M. Negation: ∃K ∃M ∀N ∃n ≥ N ∃z ∈ K: |fₙ(z)| ≤ M. Then: for fₙ(z) = nz on 𝔻, which K and M witness the negation? (K = {0}, M = 1.)
- **`exm-exp-nz-not-normal`.** {e^{nz}} on 𝔻: on Re z > 0 it tends to ∞, on Re z < 0 to 0, so no subsequence converges, to a holomorphic limit or to ∞, uniformly on any disc about a point of the imaginary axis. One omitted value is not enough.
- **`lem-normal-local`.** Let G be a region and 𝓕 ⊆ H(G). Suppose every a ∈ G has a disc D_a ⊆ G such that every sequence in 𝓕 has a subsequence converging, uniformly on compact subsets of D_a, to a holomorphic function or to ∞. Then 𝓕 is normal in the extended sense on G. *Proof sketch:* countably many D_{a_j} cover G (G is a countable union of compact sets); diagonal extraction (as in CA.5's proof of Montel) gives one subsequence that works on every D_{a_j}. On overlapping discs the two kinds of limit cannot differ (a sequence cannot tend to ∞ and to a finite limit at a common point), and finite limits agree; so the set of points with a finite-limit disc and the set with an ∞-limit disc are disjoint open sets covering G, and connectedness leaves one. A compact K is covered by finitely many closed discs, each inside some D_{a_j}, which gives uniform convergence on K.
- **`thm-montel-fundamental`.** Let G be a region and 𝓕 the family of all f ∈ H(G) that omit 0 and 1. Then 𝓕 is normal in the extended sense. *Proof (worked instance of "split by the value at a point"):* fix a ∈ G and r > 0 with B̄(a, 2r) ⊆ G; by `lem-normal-local` it suffices to work on D_a = B(a, r). Given (fₙ), pass to a subsequence with either |fₙ(a)| ≤ 1 for all n or |fₙ(a)| > 1 for all n.
  - First case: `cor-schottky-disc` on B(a, 2r) with β = 1/2 bounds all fₙ by C(1, 1/2) on D_a; Montel (CA.5) gives a subsequence converging locally uniformly on D_a.
  - Second case: 1/fₙ ∈ H(G) omits 0 and 1 and |1/fₙ(a)| < 1; as above a subsequence 1/fₙ → h locally uniformly on D_a. Hurwitz (CA.4): h ≡ 0 or h has no zeros, since no 1/fₙ vanishes. If h ≡ 0 then fₙ → ∞ uniformly on compact subsets of D_a. If h is zero-free, |h| is bounded below on each compact set, and fₙ → 1/h locally uniformly.
- **`cor-montel-two-values`.** If a ≠ b and every f ∈ 𝓕 omits a and b, then 𝓕 is normal in the extended sense. *Route:* apply the theorem to (f − a)/(b − a); a subsequence of the transformed functions tending to ∞ gives the same for f, a finite limit φ gives a + (b − a)φ.
- **Worked example `exm-cayley-exp-to-infinity`.** fₙ(z) = exp(n(1 + z)/(1 − z)) on 𝔻 omit 0 and 1 (as in `exm-schottky-blowup`). The theorem promises a subsequence with a limit; here the whole sequence tends to ∞: on |z| ≤ r, Re((1 + z)/(1 − z)) ≥ (1 − r)/(1 + r), so |fₙ| ≥ exp(n(1 − r)/(1 + r)). Contrast with fₙ(z) = exp((1 + z)/(n(1 − z))) → 1 locally uniformly.
- **Quick checks.**
  1. Is {zⁿ} normal in the extended sense on {|z| > 1}? On a disc meeting the unit circle? *Answer:* yes (→ ∞); no (limit 0 inside, ∞ outside).
  2. Why may we not apply `cor-schottky-disc` in the second case directly to fₙ? *Answer:* there is no bound on |fₙ(a)|.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Is {nz} normal in the extended sense on 𝔻? On ℂ ∖ {0}? *Answer:* no (0 at z = 0, ∞ elsewhere); yes (→ ∞ uniformly on compacts avoiding 0).
  - **A2.** Is the family of all f ∈ H(𝔻) with f(𝔻) ⊆ ℂ ∖ [0, 1] normal in the extended sense? *Answer:* yes, each omits 0 and 1 (indeed Montel's theorem after a conformal map would also do, but the fundamental test is immediate).
  - **B1.** Let fₙ ∈ H(𝔻) omit 0 and 1, with fₙ(0) → 0. Show fₙ → 0 uniformly on compact subsets of 𝔻. *Answer:* every subsequence has a further subsequence with a limit; not ∞ (fₙ(0) → 0); a holomorphic limit h has h(0) = 0, and by Hurwitz h ≡ 0 (the fₙ are zero-free). A sequence every subsequence of which has a sub-subsequence tending to 0 tends to 0.
  - **B2.** Little Picard via the fundamental test: let f be entire omitting 0 and 1 and fₙ(z) = f(nz) on 𝔻. Show f is bounded. *Answer:* fₙ(0) = f(0) for all n, so no subsequence tends to ∞; a subsequence converges uniformly on |z| ≤ 1/2, so |f| ≤ M on |w| ≤ n_k/2 for all k; Liouville.
  - **B3 (faded, third appearance: last half blank).** Let g be holomorphic on the strip S = {|Im z| < 1} with g(S) containing no disc of radius 1. Show |g′(x)| ≤ 12 for real x, and bound |g(x) − g(0)|. *Name the move, then complete it.* *Answer:* no disc of radius 1 bounds the derivative: dist(x, ∂S) = 1, so |g′(x)| ≤ 12; integrating, |g(x) − g(0)| ≤ 12|x|.
  - **C1.** (Varying omitted values.) Let 𝓕 ⊆ H(G) be such that each f ∈ 𝓕 omits two values a_f, b_f with |a_f|, |b_f| ≤ K and |a_f − b_f| ≥ δ > 0. Show 𝓕 is normal in the extended sense. *Answer:* hₙ = (fₙ − aₙ)/(bₙ − aₙ) omits 0 and 1; pass to a subsequence with aₙ → a, bₙ → b (|b − a| ≥ δ) and hₙ → h or ∞; then fₙ = aₙ + (bₙ − aₙ)hₙ → a + (b − a)h, or → ∞ since |bₙ − aₙ| ≥ δ. **Marking scheme.** (10 points) 2: normalisation and why hₙ omits 0, 1. 2: Bolzano–Weierstrass for (aₙ, bₙ). 3: the finite case, with uniform convergence on compacts justified. 3: the ∞ case using |bₙ − aₙ| ≥ δ. Fatal error: forgetting the lower bound δ (then the ∞ case fails: bₙ − aₙ → 0 can cancel hₙ → ∞).

## 05-great-picard.md — The great Picard theorem (≤ 4,500 words)

- **Hook.** Return to e^{1/z}: Casorati–Weierstrass (CA.3) says its values near 0 are dense; in fact, solving e^{1/z} = w gives z = 1/(log w + 2πik), infinitely many solutions in every punctured disc for every w ≠ 0. Is that typical?
- **`thm-great-picard`.** Let f be holomorphic on B*(a, R) with an essential singularity at a. Then in every punctured disc B*(a, r), r ≤ R, f takes every complex value infinitely often, with at most one exception. *Proof (proof only; Scratch Work is not repeated, support has faded):*
  1. Reduction: it suffices to show f cannot omit two values on any B*(a, r). (If f took two values b, c only finitely often on B*(a, r), shrink r to omit both; the essential singularity is unchanged.) Translate and scale: a = 0, r = 1, omitted values 0 and 1.
  2. Rescale toward the singularity: fₙ(z) = f(z/n) on G = B*(0, 1), a region; each fₙ omits 0 and 1. By `thm-montel-fundamental` some subsequence (f_{n_k}) converges uniformly on the circle |z| = 1/2 to a holomorphic φ or to ∞.
  3. Finite limit: |f_{n_k}| ≤ M on |z| = 1/2, i.e. |f| ≤ M on the circles |z| = 1/(2n_k). Maximum modulus (CA.2) on each annulus between consecutive circles gives |f| ≤ M on 0 < |z| ≤ 1/(2n_1); Riemann's theorem (CA.3) makes 0 removable. Contradiction.
  4. Limit ∞: 1/f is holomorphic on B*(0, 1) (f omits 0), and |1/f| → 0 uniformly on the circles |z| = 1/(2n_k); the same annulus argument bounds 1/f, so 1/f extends holomorphically with value 0 at 0, i.e. f has a pole. Contradiction.
- **Proof ordering (STYLE §11.4).** The eight lines of the proof above, shuffled, plus two distractors: (D1) "By Montel's theorem (CA.5) the fₙ have a subsequence converging in H(G)" (wrong theorem: the fₙ need not be locally bounded); (D2) "|f| ≤ M on the circles |z| = 1/(2n_k), hence |f| ≤ M on B*(0, 1)" (the annulus argument gives the bound only inside the first circle). Folded solution: an admissible order (reduction, rescale, normal family, split into cases, the two case arguments in either order), and why each distractor fails. Prompt: *name the move in step 2.* (Answer: rescale toward the singularity.)
- **`cor-picard-transcendental`.** A non-polynomial entire function takes every complex value infinitely often, with at most one exception. *Route:* f(1/z) has an essential singularity at 0 (CA.3: a pole at 0 would make f a polynomial).
- **`cor-picard-meromorphic`.** A meromorphic function on ℂ that omits three distinct values of ℂ̂ = ℂ ∪ {∞} is constant. *Route:* if ∞ is omitted, f is entire omitting two values. Otherwise, if f omits a, b, c ∈ ℂ, g = 1/(f − c) is entire (poles of f become removable zeros of g, and f − c ≠ 0) and omits 1/(a − c), 1/(b − c).
- **`exm-tan-omits-two`.** tan z = −i(w − 1)/(w + 1) with w = e^{2iz}; tan z = i forces w = 0 and tan z = −i forces −1 = 1, so tan omits exactly ±i (and takes ∞ at its poles). `cor-picard-meromorphic` is sharp.
- **`exm-exp-one-over-z`.** e^{1/z} takes every w ≠ 0 infinitely often near 0 and omits 0; sin(1/z) omits nothing near 0. The exception in great Picard may or may not occur.
- **Closing the chapter hook.** e^{z} misses the one value the little theorem allows; e^{1/z} the one value the great theorem allows. Neither theorem can be improved in the number of exceptions.
- **The other route (outline; prose with displayed statements, no proof obligations; this is where CA.8a enters).** There is a holomorphic λ on the upper half-plane ℍ, the modular function, with λ(ℍ) = ℂ ∖ {0, 1}, such that every point of ℂ ∖ {0, 1} has a disc neighbourhood on which every branch of λ^{−1} is holomorphic (λ is a covering). *Stated, not proved here; proved in CA.8b (optional) by theta functions.* Given an entire f omitting 0 and 1, start a branch of λ^{−1} ∘ f at 0 and continue it along every path in ℂ; continuation never gets stuck because λ is a covering, and the monodromy theorem makes the result single-valued on the simply connected ℂ; together this is CA.8a's `thm-lift-local-homeomorphism` with φ = λ (CA.8a §4 states exactly this motivating instance). The lift φ: ℂ → ℍ is entire, and (φ − i)/(φ + i) is bounded, so φ and f = λ ∘ φ are constant (Liouville). Compare: the Bloch route of §02 builds the lift by hand with the non-covering map Φ(z) = −exp(iπ cosh 2z) and pays with Bloch's theorem; the modular route gets the lift free from a covering and pays with constructing λ.
- **Quick checks.**
  1. Can a non-polynomial entire function take the value 0 only finitely often and the value 1 only finitely often? *Answer:* no (`cor-picard-transcendental`).
  2. f(z) = z e^{1/z}: what does great Picard say near 0, and which value, if any, is exceptional? *Answer:* every value infinitely often with at most one exception; z e^{1/z} = 0 has no solution near 0 (z ≠ 0, e^{1/z} ≠ 0), so 0 is the exception, and every other value is taken infinitely often.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Solve e^{1/z} = −1 and show the solutions accumulate at 0. *Answer:* z = 1/(iπ(2k + 1)), k ∈ ℤ.
  - **A2.** Let f be entire and not a polynomial. Show that f has infinitely many fixed points unless f(z) − z omits the value 0, and give an example of the second case. *Answer:* f(z) − z is entire and not a polynomial, so `cor-picard-transcendental` applies; f(z) = z + e^{z} has no fixed point.
  - **A3.** Show that e^{z} + 1 omits exactly the value 1, and that sin z omits no value. *Answer:* e^{z} = w − 1 is solvable iff w ≠ 1; sin z = w is a quadratic in e^{iz} with root product −1, so a non-zero root exists.
  - **B1.** Let f be holomorphic on B*(0, 1), omitting 0 and 1. Show that 0 is removable or a pole. *Answer:* contrapositive of `thm-great-picard`.
  - **B2.** Let f be entire and f(z) ≠ 0 for all z, and suppose f(z) = 1 only finitely often. Show f is constant. *Answer:* by `cor-picard-transcendental`, if f is not a polynomial it takes 1 infinitely often unless 1 is its exception, but 0 is already exceptional; so f is a polynomial, zero-free, hence constant.
  - **C1.** (Meromorphic great Picard.) Let f be meromorphic on B*(0, r) and suppose f does not extend to a meromorphic function on B(0, r). Show that in every punctured disc about 0, f takes every value of ℂ̂ infinitely often, with at most two exceptions. *Answer outline:* if f omitted three values on some B*(0, s): if ∞ is one of them, f is holomorphic there and `thm-great-picard` (contrapositive, B1) makes 0 removable or a pole. Otherwise, with c omitted, g = 1/(f − c) is holomorphic on B*(0, s) (zeros of f − c: none; poles of f: removable zeros of g) and omits two finite values, so 0 is removable or a pole of g; g ≢ 0, so f = c + 1/g is meromorphic at 0. Contradiction. "Infinitely often" by shrinking, as in step 1. **Marking scheme.** (10 points) 2: reduction to "omits three values". 2: the case ∞ omitted. 3: g holomorphic on the punctured disc, with the poles of f handled. 2: g has removable singularity or pole, so f meromorphic at 0. 1: "infinitely often" by shrinking. Fatal errors: treating f as holomorphic when poles accumulate at 0 (they would be zeros of g accumulating at 0, forcing g ≡ 0, which must be excluded explicitly).
  - **C2 (faded, last half blank).** Let f be holomorphic on the right half-plane P, omit 0 and 1, with 1/2 ≤ |f(1)| ≤ α. Show that for real x ≥ 1, |f(x)| ≤ exp(π e^{2C₁(α)} x^{24}). - **R. Review** (STYLE §11.5; problem texts never name their source chapter, solutions do with `@ref`).
  - **R1 (previous chapter, CA.8a).** Show that Σ_{n≥0} z^{n!} cannot be continued analytically across any point of the unit circle. *Answer:* at z = tζ with ζ = e^{2πip/q} and 0 < t < 1, every term with n ≥ q equals t^{n!} > 0, so the sum tends to ∞ as t ↑ 1; roots of unity are dense in the circle. The method of CA.8a's natural-boundary proof for Σ z^{2ⁿ}.
  - **R2 (3–4 chapters back, CA.7).** Show that an entire function whose zeros are exactly the points n² (n ≥ 1), all simple, is e^{h(z)} Π(1 − z/n²) for some entire h. *Answer:* Π(1 − z/n²) converges locally uniformly (Σ 1/n² < ∞) with exactly these zeros; the quotient is entire and zero-free, hence e^{h} (CA.7 Weierstrass factorization, CA.4 logarithms).
  - **R3 (spiral, earlier Part: Arzelà–Ascoli, AN2).** The real functions sin(nx) on (0, 1) are uniformly bounded and real-analytic. Do they have a subsequence converging uniformly on [1/4, 3/4]? What does the holomorphic setting supply that is missing here? *Answer:* no. If sin(n_k x) converged uniformly on [1/4, 3/4], then ∫_{1/4}^{3/4}(sin n_k x − sin n_j x)² dx → 0 as j, k → ∞; but for fixed j and k → ∞ the integral tends to 1/4 + ∫ sin² n_j x dx ≥ 1/4 − O(1/n_j), since the cross term ∫ sin n_k x sin n_j x dx = O(1/(n_k − n_j)) by direct integration. Holomorphy supplies equicontinuity through Cauchy's estimates (CA.5's proof of Montel); here sin(nz) is not bounded on any complex neighbourhood of [1/4, 3/4], so that route is closed.
  - **R4 (no hint of method).** Let f be holomorphic on B*(0, 1) with |f(z)| ≤ log(1/|z|). Show 0 is removable. *Answer:* z f(z) → 0, so z f(z) has a removable singularity with value 0, hence f has a removable singularity (Riemann, CA.3). Look-alikes: great Picard and Casorati–Weierstrass need omitted values or a non-dense image, which the hypothesis does not give directly; the growth bound points to Riemann's theorem applied to z f(z).
