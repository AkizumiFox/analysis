**Named moves** (in `index.md`; each has Scratch Work at its first appearance):
- **Telescope a fast subsequence.** From a Cauchy sequence pick \(n_1 < n_2 < \cdots\) with \(\|x_{n_{k+1}} - x_{n_k}\| < 2^{-k}\); the telescoping series is absolutely convergent. (First appearance §01; reused §05; MT.7 reuses it for Riesz–Fischer.)
- **Normalize by homogeneity.** For an inequality that is homogeneous in each variable, reduce to the case where the norms are 1, prove it there, scale back. (First appearance §02, Hölder.)
- **Operator norm by two inequalities.** An estimate gives \(\|T\| \le C\); a test vector, or a sequence of test vectors, gives \(\|T\| \ge C\). (First appearance §03; spiral from AN1.1 "equality of sups by two inequalities".)
- **Extend by density.** Define the extension as a limit along a sequence from the dense set, check independence of the sequence by interleaving, then check linearity and the norm. (First appearance §03.)
- **Test against the equality case of Hölder.** To show a functional's representing sequence lies in \(\ell^{p'}\), feed it the vector that makes Hölder an equality. (First appearance §06.)

## index.md

- **Story.** Up to now each function space came with its own completeness theorem: \(C[a,b]\) in AN1.9/AN2.2, \(L^1\) in MT.2. This chapter finds the common frame: a vector space with a norm in which Cauchy sequences converge, and the linear maps between such spaces that respect the norm.
- **Chapter hook (opened here, paid in §03, `thm-volterra-equation`).** The integral equation
  \[ f(x) - \int_0^x k(x,t) f(t)\, dt = g(x), \qquad 0 \le x \le 1, \]
  with \(k\) and \(g\) continuous. Write \(V_k f(x) = \int_0^x k(x,t) f(t)\,dt\). Formally \(f = (I - V_k)^{-1} g = g + V_k g + V_k^2 g + \cdots\), the geometric series. Two questions make the formal answer honest: *in which sense does \(\sum V_k^n g\) converge*, and *why is the limit a solution*. Show the case \(k \equiv 2\), \(g \equiv 1\): the series is \(\sum (2x)^n / n! = e^{2x}\), and \(e^{2x}\) solves the equation, even though "\(|V_k| = 2 > 1\)" would make a geometric series look hopeless. The answer needs three things this chapter builds: completeness of \(C[0,1]\) under the sup norm, a norm on operators with \(\|V_k^n\| \le M^n / n!\), and the fact that absolutely convergent series converge in a complete space.
- **Second hook (opened in §06, paid partly there, the rest in FA.3, FA.1b, FA.2).** *Which continuous linear functionals does a space have?* §06 answers it for \(\ell^p\) and \(c_0\); \(\ell^\infty\) needs Hahn–Banach (FA.3), \(L^p\) needs Radon–Nikodym (FA.1b), \(C(K)\) needs Radon measures (FA.2).

No measure theory is used; the measure-theoretic examples (\(L^p\)) arrive in MT.7, which treats this chapter as its frame.

## 01-normed-spaces.md — Norms, Completeness, and Series

Budget ≤ 4,000 main-line words.

- **Opening.** Recall from AN2.1 that a norm is a source of metrics, and from AN2.2 which sup-metric spaces are complete. This section makes the norm itself the object.
- **`def-norm`** on a vector space \(X\) over \(\mathbb K\): \(\|x\| \ge 0\) with \(\|x\| = 0 \iff x = 0\); \(\|\lambda x\| = |\lambda| \|x\|\); \(\|x + y\| \le \|x\| + \|y\|\). A **seminorm** drops "\(\|x\| = 0 \Rightarrow x = 0\)" (needed in §05). **`def-normed-space`**, with the metric \(d(x,y) = \|x - y\|\) (AN2.1).
- **`prp-norm-continuous`.** \(\bigl|\|x\| - \|y\|\bigr| \le \|x - y\|\); addition \(X \times X \to X\) and scalar multiplication \(\mathbb K \times X \to X\) are continuous. Proof: reverse triangle; \(\|\lambda x - \mu y\| \le |\lambda - \mu|\|x\| + |\mu|\|x - y\|\).
- **Failure first.** Two Cauchy sequences with no limit in the space:
  - **`exm-c00-incomplete`.** In \(c_{00}\) with \(\|\cdot\|_\infty\), \(x^{(n)} = (1, \tfrac12, \dots, \tfrac1n, 0, 0, \dots)\): \(\|x^{(m)} - x^{(n)}\|_\infty = 1/(n+1)\) for \(m > n\), so Cauchy. Any limit would have to be \((1/k)_k\) coordinatewise (coordinates are 1-Lipschitz), which is not finitely supported.
  - **`exm-l1-norm-c01-incomplete`.** On \(C[0,1]\), \(\|f\|_1 = \int_0^1 |f|\) (Riemann). \(f_n\) is 0 on \([0, \tfrac12]\), linear up to 1 on \([\tfrac12, \tfrac12 + \tfrac1n]\), 1 after. \(\|f_m - f_n\|_1 \le \tfrac1{2n}\) for \(m > n\). If \(f_n \to f\) in \(\|\cdot\|_1\) with \(f\) continuous, then \(\int_0^{1/2} |f| = 0\) and \(\int_{1/2 + \delta}^1 |f - 1| = 0\) for each \(\delta > 0\), so \(f = 0\) on \([0, \tfrac12]\) and \(f = 1\) on \((\tfrac12, 1]\), not continuous. (A continuous non-negative function with zero integral is zero: AN1.8.)
  - Moral, stated plainly: the geometric series of the hook only makes sense if partial sums that ought to converge do converge.
- **`def-banach-space`.** A normed space complete in its metric.
- **`prp-sup-norm-spaces-banach`.** \(B(S)\) (bounded \(\mathbb K\)-valued functions on a set \(S\)) and \(C(K)\) (\(K\) compact metric) with \(\|f\|_\infty\) are Banach; so is \((\mathbb K^n, \|\cdot\|)\) for every norm. Proof: AN2.2 for \(B(S)\) and \(C_b(X) = C(K)\); for \(\mathbb K^n\), equivalence of norms [AN2.3 `thm-norms-equivalent`] and completeness of \(\mathbb K^n\) in the Euclidean norm.
- **`def-series-in-normed-space`.** \(\sum x_n\) converges if the partial sums converge; absolutely convergent if \(\sum \|x_n\| < \infty\).
- **`thm-banach-iff-absolute-series`** (key proof). A normed space \(X\) is Banach if and only if every absolutely convergent series in \(X\) converges in \(X\).
  - **Scratch Work** (first appearance of *telescope a fast subsequence*; set apart before the proof). (⇒) is the Cauchy criterion for partial sums: \(\|s_m - s_n\| \le \sum_{n < k \le m} \|x_k\|\). (⇐) needs a series out of a Cauchy sequence. The obvious one, \(x_1 + \sum (x_{n+1} - x_n)\), telescopes to \(x_n\), but \(\sum \|x_{n+1} - x_n\|\) can diverge (witness in ℝ: the partial sums of \(\sum (-1)^k/k\) are Cauchy, with steps \(1/n\)). Fix: skip ahead so that the steps shrink geometrically. Draw the subsequence as points getting closer by halves.
  - **Proof.** (⇐) Given Cauchy \((x_n)\), choose \(n_1 < n_2 < \cdots\) with \(\|x_m - x_n\| < 2^{-k}\) for \(m, n \ge n_k\). The series \(x_{n_1} + \sum_k (x_{n_{k+1}} - x_{n_k})\) has \(\sum \|\cdot\| \le \|x_{n_1}\| + 1\), so it converges; its partial sums are \(x_{n_{k}}\). A Cauchy sequence with a convergent subsequence converges [AN2.2 `thm-cauchy-subsequence-converges`].
- **Quick check.** In \(c_{00}\) with \(\|\cdot\|_\infty\), which absolutely convergent series fails to converge? (*Answer:* \(\sum e_n / n^2\); its partial sums converge coordinatewise to \((1/n^2)\), not in \(c_{00}\), and coordinates are continuous.)
- **`cor-m-test-banach`.** In a Banach space, \(\|x_n\| \le M_n\) with \(\sum M_n < \infty\) implies \(\sum x_n\) converges and \(\|\sum x_n\| \le \sum M_n\). In \(C(K)\) this is the Weierstrass M-test (AN1.9); say so.
- **Warning.** "Complete" is a property of the norm, not of the vector space: \(C[0,1]\) is Banach under \(\|\cdot\|_\infty\) and not under \(\|\cdot\|_1\).
- **Quick check.** Is \((C^1[0,1], \|f\|_\infty)\) Banach? (*Answer:* no: \(\sqrt{(x - \tfrac12)^2 + 1/n} \to |x - \tfrac12|\) uniformly, a uniform limit of \(C^1\) functions that is not \(C^1\); the sequence is Cauchy without a limit in \(C^1\).)
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1** (key-proof comprehension, 5 parts): (a) state `def-banach-space` in symbols; (b) true/false: a closed subset of a normed space is complete (*false*: \(c_{00}\) is closed in itself); (c) in the proof of `thm-banach-iff-absolute-series`, why not use \(\sum (x_{n+1} - x_n)\) directly? (*it need not converge absolutely*); (d) where is the hypothesis "Cauchy" used? (*to choose the \(n_k\)*); (e) what does the proof give if \(X\) is only a seminormed space? (*a limit, not unique*).
  - **B1.** \(C^1[0,1]\) with \(\|f\|_{C^1} = \|f\|_\infty + \|f'\|_\infty\) is Banach. (*Answer:* Cauchy in this norm ⇒ \(f_n \to f\), \(f_n' \to g\) uniformly; term-by-term differentiation (AN1.9) gives \(f \in C^1\), \(f' = g\); then \(\|f_n - f\|_{C^1} \to 0\).)
  - **B2.** Show \(\|f\|_1 \le \|f\|_\infty\) on \(C[0,1]\) and that no \(C\) gives \(\|f\|_\infty \le C\|f\|_1\). (*Answer:* tent of height 1 and base \(2/n\) has \(\|f\|_1 = 1/n\).)
  - **B3.** In a Banach space, if \(\sum \|x_n\| < \infty\) then every rearrangement of \(\sum x_n\) converges to the same sum. (*Answer:* partial sums of the rearrangement differ from \(s_N\) by terms with indices \(> N\) eventually; tail of \(\sum \|x_n\|\).)
  - **C1.** Let \(X\) be a normed space in which every series with \(\|x_n\| \le 2^{-n}\) converges. Show \(X\) is Banach. (*Answer:* the proof of (⇐) only used such series.)
  - **C2** (find the error, true conclusion). "Proof" that \(B(S)\) is complete: "Let \((f_n)\) be Cauchy. For each \(s\), \((f_n(s))\) is Cauchy in \(\mathbb K\), so \(f_n(s) \to f(s)\). Hence \(f_n \to f\) in \(B(S)\)." The conclusion is true; the argument proves only pointwise convergence and never shows \(f\) bounded or \(\|f_n - f\|_\infty \to 0\). Solution names both missing steps and supplies them (uniform \(N\) from Cauchy, let \(m \to \infty\) in \(|f_n(s) - f_m(s)| < \varepsilon\)).

## 02-sequence-spaces.md — The Sequence Spaces

Budget ≤ 4,000 main-line words.

- **Lead-in.** AN2.1 met \(\ell^1, \ell^2, \ell^\infty\). For \(1 < p < \infty\), \(p \ne 2\), the triangle inequality for \(\|x\|_p = (\sum |x_n|^p)^{1/p}\) is not obvious; it is the content of Minkowski's inequality, and Hölder's inequality is the tool.
- **`def-lp-space`.** For \(1 \le p < \infty\): \(\ell^p = \{x = (x_n)_{n \ge 1} : \sum |x_n|^p < \infty\}\), \(\|x\|_p\); \(\ell^\infty\) with \(\|x\|_\infty = \sup |x_n|\). That these are vector spaces and \(\|\cdot\|_p\) is a norm waits for Minkowski; say so before using it.
- **`def-dual-exponent`.** \(p' \in [1, \infty]\) with \(1/p + 1/p' = 1\). Table: \(p = 1, 4/3, 2, 4, \infty\) ↦ \(\infty, 4, 2, 4/3, 1\). Note \((p-1)p' = p\) for \(1 < p < \infty\).
- **`lem-young`.** For \(a, b \ge 0\) and \(1 < p < \infty\): \(ab \le a^p/p + b^{p'}/p'\), with equality iff \(a^p = b^{p'}\).
  - Proof: trivial if \(ab = 0\). Otherwise \(\log\) is strictly concave on \((0, \infty)\) (\(\log'' < 0\), AN1.10, AN1.7), so \(\log(\tfrac1p a^p + \tfrac1{p'} b^{p'}) \ge \tfrac1p \log a^p + \tfrac1{p'} \log b^{p'} = \log ab\), with equality iff \(a^p = b^{p'}\). Picture: the area under \(y = x^{p-1}\) and to the left of it (Proof Idea paragraph only; no figure required).
- **`thm-holder-lp`.** Let \(1 \le p \le \infty\), \(x \in \ell^p\), \(y \in \ell^{p'}\). Then \(\sum |x_n y_n| \le \|x\|_p \|y\|_{p'}\); in particular \((x_n y_n) \in \ell^1\).
  - **Scratch Work** (first appearance of *normalize by homogeneity*): both sides scale by \(|\lambda||\mu|\) under \(x \mapsto \lambda x\), \(y \mapsto \mu y\). So assume \(\|x\|_p = \|y\|_{p'} = 1\); the goal becomes \(\sum |x_n||y_n| \le 1 = \tfrac1p + \tfrac1{p'}\), which is Young summed over \(n\). Then undo the scaling.
  - Proof: cases \(p = 1\) or \(\infty\) directly; zero vectors trivially; else normalize and sum Young.
  - Equality case (remark, used in §06): for \(1 < p < \infty\) and \(\|x\|_p = \|y\|_{p'} = 1\), equality iff \(|x_n|^p = |y_n|^{p'}\) for all \(n\).
- **`thm-minkowski-lp`.** For \(1 \le p \le \infty\) and \(x, y \in \ell^p\): \(x + y \in \ell^p\) and \(\|x + y\|_p \le \|x\|_p + \|y\|_p\). Hence \(\ell^p\) is a normed space.
  - Proof (\(1 < p < \infty\)): first for finite sums over \(n \le N\): \(\sum |x_n + y_n|^p \le \sum |x_n||x_n + y_n|^{p-1} + \sum |y_n||x_n + y_n|^{p-1}\); apply Hölder (finite version) with exponents \(p, p'\), using \((p-1)p' = p\); divide by \((\sum_{n \le N} |x_n + y_n|^p)^{1/p'}\) (if non-zero). Let \(N \to \infty\): partial sums are bounded, so the series converges.
  - **Warning.** Do not apply Hölder to \(|x + y|^{p-1}\) before knowing \(x + y \in \ell^p\); the truncation to \(n \le N\) is what avoids the circle.
- **`exm-p-less-than-one`.** For \(0 < p < 1\), \(\|x\|_p\) on \(\mathbb K^2\) is not a norm: \(\|e_1 + e_2\|_p = 2^{1/p} > 2 = \|e_1\|_p + \|e_2\|_p\). (The unit ball is not convex: sketch the star shape for \(p = 1/2\).)
- **`thm-lp-complete`.** \(\ell^p\) is a Banach space for \(1 \le p \le \infty\).
  - Proof (\(p < \infty\)): Cauchy \((x^{(k)})\) ⇒ each coordinate Cauchy ⇒ \(x^{(k)}_n \to x_n\). Given \(\varepsilon\), \(N_\varepsilon\) with \(\|x^{(k)} - x^{(j)}\|_p < \varepsilon\) for \(k, j \ge N_\varepsilon\). For fixed \(M\), \(\sum_{n \le M} |x^{(k)}_n - x^{(j)}_n|^p < \varepsilon^p\); let \(j \to \infty\), then \(M \to \infty\): \(\|x^{(k)} - x\|_p \le \varepsilon\). Then \(x = x^{(k)} - (x^{(k)} - x) \in \ell^p\) by Minkowski. \(p = \infty\): AN2.2 (or \(\ell^\infty = B(\mathbb N_{\ge 1})\), `prp-sup-norm-spaces-banach`).
  - Forward pointer (prose): MT.7 proves the same for \(L^p(\mu)\), and \(\ell^p = L^p(\text{counting measure})\).
- **`prp-lp-inclusions`.** For \(1 \le p < q \le \infty\): \(\ell^p \subsetneq \ell^q\) and \(\|x\|_q \le \|x\|_p\).
  - Proof: normalize \(\|x\|_p = 1\) (second use of the move, no Scratch Work); then \(|x_n| \le 1\) so \(|x_n|^q \le |x_n|^p\). Strictness: \(x_n = n^{-1/p}\) is in \(\ell^q\) for \(q > p\), not in \(\ell^p\).
  - Remark: on a finite measure space the inclusions of \(L^p\) go the other way (MT.7); the "small \(p\) = small space" intuition belongs to sequences only.
- **`prp-c0-closed`.** \(c_0\) and \(c\) are closed subspaces of \(\ell^\infty\), hence Banach (closed in complete ⇒ complete, AN2.2). Proof for \(c_0\): if \(x^{(k)} \to x\) uniformly and each \(x^{(k)}\) is null, an \(\varepsilon/2\) argument shows \(x\) is null.
- **`prp-sequence-spaces-separable`.** \(c_0\) and \(\ell^p\) (\(1 \le p < \infty\)) are separable: finitely supported sequences with entries in \(\mathbb Q\) (or \(\mathbb Q + i\mathbb Q\)) are a countable dense set.
- **`exm-linfty-not-separable`.** \(\{1_A : A \subseteq \mathbb N_{\ge 1}\}\) is uncountable (F2.3) with pairwise distances 1; disjoint balls of radius \(\tfrac12\) defeat any countable dense set.
- **Quick check.** For which \(p\) is \((n^{-1/2})_n\) in \(\ell^p\)? In \(c_0\)? (*Answer:* \(p > 2\) and \(p = \infty\); yes.)
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1** (4 parts): (a) compute \(p'\) for \(p = 3\); (b) true/false: \(\ell^1 \subseteq c_0\) (*true*); (c) true/false: \(c_0 \subseteq \ell^p\) for some \(p < \infty\) (*false*: \(1/\log(n+1)\)); (d) which step of Minkowski's proof needs the truncation to \(n \le N\)?
  - **B1.** Compute \(\|x\|_p\) for \(x = (2^{-n})_{n \ge 1}\) and all \(p\). (*Answer:* \((2^p - 1)^{-1/p}\); \(\|x\|_\infty = 1/2\).)
  - **B2.** For \(x \in \ell^{p_0}\) with \(p_0 < \infty\), show \(\|x\|_p \to \|x\|_\infty\) as \(p \to \infty\). (*Answer:* \(\|x\|_\infty \le \|x\|_p \le \|x\|_\infty^{1 - p_0/p} \|x\|_{p_0}^{p_0/p}\).)
  - **B3.** Prove the three-term Hölder: \(\sum |x_n y_n z_n| \le \|x\|_p \|y\|_q \|z\|_r\) when \(1/p + 1/q + 1/r = 1\), \(p, q, r \in (1, \infty)\). (*Answer:* Hölder with exponents \(p\) and \(p'\), then Hölder for \(|y|^{p'}, |z|^{p'}\) with exponents \(q/p', r/p'\).)
  - **C1** (find the error, true conclusion). "Proof" that \(\ell^p\) is complete: "\(\|x^{(k)} - x\|_p^p = \sum_n \lim_j |x^{(k)}_n - x^{(j)}_n|^p = \lim_j \sum_n |x^{(k)}_n - x^{(j)}_n|^p \le \varepsilon^p\)." Conclusion true; the interchange of \(\lim_j\) and \(\sum_n\) is unjustified (show \(\sum_n \lim_j \ne \lim_j \sum_n\) for \(e_j\)); the solution repairs it with the truncation to \(n \le M\), which gives only "\(\le\)" in the right direction.

(*Answer:* `prp-lp-inclusions`; \(\sum_{n \le N} e_n/N\) has \(\|\cdot\|_1 = 1\), \(\|\cdot\|_2 = N^{-1/2}\).)

## 03-bounded-operators.md — Bounded Operators

Budget ≤ 5,000 main-line words (the longest section; split at "The Neumann series" if the draft runs over).

- **Failure first: `exm-differentiation-unbounded`.** \(D f = f'\) from \((C^1[0,1], \|\cdot\|_\infty)\) to \((C[0,1], \|\cdot\|_\infty)\) is linear. \(f_n(x) = x^n/n\): \(\|f_n\|_\infty = 1/n \to 0\) but \(\|Df_n\|_\infty = 1\). So \(D\) is not continuous at 0, and (by translation, `thm-bounded-iff-continuous` below) nowhere. Contrast: with the \(C^1\) norm of §01 B1, \(\|Df\|_\infty \le \|f\|_{C^1}\). Moral: for a linear map, continuity is a growth bound.
- **`def-bounded-operator`.** \(T \colon X \to Y\) linear is bounded if \(\exists C \ge 0\ \forall x\ \|Tx\| \le C\|x\|\). **Warning:** "bounded" means bounded on the unit ball, not bounded on \(X\) (only \(T = 0\) is that).
- **`thm-bounded-iff-continuous`.** For linear \(T\), TFAE: (i) \(T\) is continuous at 0; (ii) \(T\) is continuous; (iii) \(T\) is bounded; (iv) \(T\) is Lipschitz. Proof: (i)⇒(iii) by \(\varepsilon = 1\), \(\delta\), and scaling \(x \mapsto \tfrac{\delta}{2\|x\|} x\); (iii)⇒(iv) by \(\|Tx - Ty\| = \|T(x - y)\|\); the rest are immediate.
- **`def-operator-norm`.** \(\|T\| = \sup\{\|Tx\| : \|x\| \le 1\}\); equal to \(\sup_{\|x\| = 1}\) when \(X \ne \{0\}\), and to the least \(C\) in the definition. \(\mathcal B(X, Y)\) the space of bounded operators.
- **`prp-operator-norm-properties`.** \(\|\cdot\|\) is a norm on \(\mathcal B(X,Y)\); \(\|Tx\| \le \|T\|\|x\|\); \(\|ST\| \le \|S\|\|T\|\). Spiral sentence: AN3.1 did this for matrices.
- **Scratch Work** (first appearance of *operator norm by two inequalities*), on the multiplication operator:
  - **`exm-multiplication-operator`.** For \(a \in \ell^\infty\) and \(1 \le p \le \infty\), \(M_a x = (a_n x_n)\) is in \(\mathcal B(\ell^p)\) with \(\|M_a\| = \|a\|_\infty\). Upper: \(|a_n x_n| \le \|a\|_\infty |x_n|\). Lower: \(\|M_a e_n\|_p = |a_n|\) with \(\|e_n\|_p = 1\), so \(\|M_a\| \ge \sup |a_n|\). Scratch Work explains choosing test vectors where the estimate is nearly tight, and that a sup may need a *sequence* of test vectors (no single one attains it, e.g. \(a_n = 1 - 1/n\)).
- **`exm-shifts`.** \(R(x_1, x_2, \dots) = (0, x_1, x_2, \dots)\), \(L(x_1, x_2, \dots) = (x_2, x_3, \dots)\) on \(\ell^p\). \(\|Rx\|_p = \|x\|_p\) (isometry, not onto: \(e_1 \notin\) image); \(\|L\| = 1\) (test \(e_2\)); \(LR = I\), \(RL \ne I\) (\(RLe_1 = 0\)). In finite dimension a one-sided inverse is two-sided (LA1.2, rank–nullity); here it fails.
- **`exm-integral-operator-norm`** (worked example). \(k\) continuous on \([0,1]^2\), \((Kf)(x) = \int_0^1 k(x,t) f(t)\,dt\). Then \(Kf \in C[0,1]\) (AN2.11, or uniform continuity of \(k\)), and on \((C[0,1], \|\cdot\|_\infty)\): \(\|K\| = \max_x \int_0^1 |k(x,t)|\,dt\).
  - Upper: direct. Lower: let \(x_0\) maximize \(x \mapsto \int |k(x,t)|\,dt\) (continuous, EVT); \(f_\varepsilon(t) = \overline{k(x_0,t)} / (|k(x_0,t)| + \varepsilon)\) is continuous with \(\|f_\varepsilon\|_\infty \le 1\), and \(Kf_\varepsilon(x_0) = \int |k|^2/(|k| + \varepsilon) \ge \int (|k| - \varepsilon)\) since \(|k|^2 \ge |k|^2 - \varepsilon^2\). (The obvious test function \(\overline{\operatorname{sgn} k(x_0, \cdot)}\) is not continuous: that is why a sequence is needed. Say so.)
- **`exm-volterra-powers`.** \(Vf(x) = \int_0^x f\). By induction \(V^n f(x) = \int_0^x \frac{(x-t)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!} f(t)\,dt\) (Fubini for continuous functions on a triangle, AN1.8/AN3 level, or induction with the fundamental theorem), so \(\|V^n\| = 1/n!\) (upper by the formula, lower by \(f \equiv 1\)). Note \(\|V^n\|^{1/n} \to 0\) while \(\|V\| = 1\) (forward pointer: spectral radius, FA.7/FA.8).
- **`thm-bounded-operators-complete`.** If \(Y\) is Banach, \(\mathcal B(X,Y)\) is Banach. Proof: \((T_n)\) Cauchy ⇒ \((T_n x)\) Cauchy for each \(x\) ⇒ \(Tx = \lim T_n x\), linear; for \(n, m \ge N\), \(\|T_n x - T_m x\| \le \varepsilon \|x\|\); let \(m \to \infty\): \(\|T_n x - Tx\| \le \varepsilon\|x\|\), so \(T_n - T\) bounded, \(T\) bounded, \(\|T_n - T\| \le \varepsilon\).
- **`thm-neumann-series`.** Let \(X\) be Banach and \(T \in \mathcal B(X)\) with \(\sum_n \|T^n\| < \infty\) (for instance \(\|T\| < 1\)). Then \(I - T\) is invertible in \(\mathcal B(X)\) and \((I - T)^{-1} = \sum_{n \ge 0} T^n\).
  - Proof: the series converges in \(\mathcal B(X)\) by `thm-banach-iff-absolute-series` and `thm-bounded-operators-complete`; with \(S_N = \sum_{n \le N} T^n\), \((I - T)S_N = S_N(I - T) = I - T^{N+1}\), and \(\|T^{N+1}\| \to 0\) (terms of a convergent series); continuity of multiplication (`prp-operator-norm-properties`) passes to the limit.
  - Remark: \(\|T^n\| \le \|T\|^n\), so \(\|T\| < 1\) suffices; the weaker hypothesis is what the hook needs.
- **`prp-invertibles-open`.** In a Banach space \(X\), if \(T \in \mathcal B(X)\) has a bounded inverse and \(\|S - T\| < 1/\|T^{-1}\|\), then \(S\) has a bounded inverse. Proof: \(S = T(I - T^{-1}(T - S))\) and `thm-neumann-series`. Spiral: AN3.1's \(GL(n)\) open.
- **`thm-volterra-equation`** (**hook paid**). Let \(k\) be continuous on \(\Delta = \{(x,t) : 0 \le t \le x \le 1\}\), \(M = \max_\Delta |k|\), and \((V_k f)(x) = \int_0^x k(x,t) f(t)\,dt\). Then \(V_k \in \mathcal B(C[0,1])\), \(\|V_k^n\| \le M^n / n!\), and for every \(g \in C[0,1]\) the equation \(f - V_k f = g\) has exactly one solution \(f \in C[0,1]\), namely \(f = \sum_{n \ge 0} V_k^n g\) (uniformly convergent).
  - Proof: \(V_k f\) continuous (uniform continuity of \(k\) on the compact \(\Delta\)); induction \(|V_k^n f(x)| \le M^n x^n \|f\|_\infty / n!\); \(\sum M^n/n! = e^M < \infty\); `thm-neumann-series`.
  - Return to the hook's example \(k \equiv 2\): \(\|V_k\| = 2\), yet \(\sum \|V_k^n\| \le e^2\), and the series gives \(e^{2x}\). Spiral sentence: AN2.4 solved \(y' = F(x, y)\) by iterating an integral operator; here the equation is linear and the iteration is a series.
- **`thm-blt-extension`.** Let \(D\) be a dense subspace of a normed space \(X\), \(Y\) Banach, \(T \colon D \to Y\) bounded linear. Then there is exactly one \(\tilde T \in \mathcal B(X, Y)\) with \(\tilde T|_D = T\), and \(\|\tilde T\| = \|T\|\).
  - **Scratch Work** (first appearance of *extend by density*): to define \(\tilde T x\), take \(x_n \in D\), \(x_n \to x\); \((Tx_n)\) is Cauchy since \(\|Tx_n - Tx_m\| \le \|T\|\|x_n - x_m\|\); \(Y\) complete gives a limit; two sequences give the same limit because the interleaved sequence also converges to \(x\). Then linearity and \(\|\tilde Tx\| = \lim \|Tx_n\| \le \|T\|\|x\|\). Uniqueness: two continuous maps agreeing on a dense set agree (AN2.1).
- **`exm-step-integral-extension`** (worked example; ties to MT ch. 1, HA.9). On the step functions \(S[a,b] \subset B[a,b]\) with \(\|\cdot\|_\infty\), \(I(s) = \sum c_i (x_i - x_{i-1})\) is well defined, linear, \(|I(s)| \le (b - a)\|s\|_\infty\). BLT extends \(I\) to the closure \(\overline{S[a,b]}\) (the *regulated* functions; name only), which contains \(C[a,b]\) (uniform continuity). On \(C[a,b]\) the extension is the Riemann integral (both are bounded on \(\overline{S} \cap\) Riemann-integrable functions and agree on \(S\)). **Weird example:** \(f(x) = \sin(1/x)\), \(f(0) = 0\), is Riemann integrable but not within distance \(< 1/2\) of any step function (a step function has a one-sided limit at 0; \(f\) oscillates between \(\pm1\) on every \((0, \delta)\)). So extension by density does not recover all of Riemann's integral; MT.2 recovers more than all of it.
- **Quick checks.** (1) Swap quantifiers: what does "\(\forall x\ \exists C\ \|Tx\| \le C\|x\|\)" say? (*Nothing: every linear map satisfies it.*) (2) Compute \(\|\delta_t\|\) for \(\delta_t f = f(t)\) on \(C[0,1]\). (*1: test \(f \equiv 1\).*)
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1** (5 parts): (a) define \(\|T\|\); (b) true/false: an isometry is bounded with norm 1 (*true, if \(X \ne \{0\}\)*); (c) true/false: \(\|T^2\| = \|T\|^2\) (*false*: \(T(x_1, x_2) = (x_2, 0)\)); (d) name the move in `exm-integral-operator-norm`; (e) which hypothesis of `thm-neumann-series` fails for \(T = I\)?

Reprove `thm-bounded-operators-complete` via `thm-banach-iff-absolute-series`: given \(\sum \|T_n\| < \infty\), define \(Tx = \sum T_n x\) … show \(\|T - \sum_{n \le N} T_n\| \to 0\).   - **B3.** On \(\ell^2\), show the diagonal operator with entries \(1/n\) is bounded but \(I - \lambda M_a\) is not invertible for \(\lambda = n\). Find \(\|(I - \tfrac12 M_a)^{-1}\|\). ## 04-finite-dimension.md — Finite and Infinite Dimension

Budget ≤ 3,500 main-line words.

- **Lead-in.** In finite dimension every norm is equivalent [AN2.3 `thm-norms-equivalent`], closed bounded sets are compact (Heine–Borel), and every linear map is continuous. This section shows each of the three is exactly a finite-dimensional phenomenon.
- **`prp-finite-dim-subspace-closed`.** Every finite-dimensional normed space is Banach; every finite-dimensional subspace of a normed space is closed. Proof: a basis gives a linear bijection with \(\mathbb K^n\), bi-Lipschitz by norm equivalence; completeness transfers; complete subsets are closed.
- **`prp-finite-dim-operators-bounded`.** If \(\dim X < \infty\), every linear \(T \colon X \to Y\) is bounded. Proof: \(\|Tx\| \le \max_i \|Te_i\| \cdot \sum |x_i| \le C\|x\|\) by norm equivalence with \(\|\cdot\|_1\) in coordinates.
- **The three failures in infinite dimension:**
  - **`exm-c00-norms-inequivalent`.** On \(c_{00}\), \(\|\cdot\|_1\) and \(\|\cdot\|_\infty\): \(s_N = e_1 + \cdots + e_N\) has \(\|s_N\|_1 = N\), \(\|s_N\|_\infty = 1\).
  - **`exm-c00-unbounded-functional`.** \(\varphi(x) = \sum n x_n\) on \((c_{00}, \|\cdot\|_\infty)\): linear, \(\varphi(e_n) = n\). No choice principle is involved.
  - **`prp-discontinuous-functional-exists`.** Every infinite-dimensional normed space carries a discontinuous linear functional. Proof: a Hamel basis exists by Zorn (F2.4, cited in prose, `see` edge); normalize, choose distinct \(b_1, b_2, \dots\), set \(\varphi(b_n) = n\) and \(\varphi = 0\) on the rest. Remark: the proof is not constructive, and no explicit discontinuous functional on a Banach space can be written down without a choice principle; state this as a remark without proof.
- **`lem-riesz`.** Let \(Y\) be a closed proper subspace of a normed space \(X\) and \(0 < \theta < 1\). There is \(x \in X\) with \(\|x\| = 1\) and \(\operatorname{dist}(x, Y) \ge \theta\).
  - **Scratch Work** (a new move, set apart): pick \(x_0 \notin Y\); \(d = \operatorname{dist}(x_0, Y) > 0\) because \(Y\) is closed; choose \(y_0 \in Y\) with \(\|x_0 - y_0\| \le d/\theta\) (possible since \(d/\theta > d\)); normalize \(x = (x_0 - y_0)/\|x_0 - y_0\|\). For \(y \in Y\), \(\|x - y\| = \|x_0 - (y_0 + \|x_0 - y_0\| y)\| / \|x_0 - y_0\| \ge d \theta / d\). Draw the picture in \(\mathbb R^2\) with \(Y\) a line: \(\theta = 1\) is attainable there (perpendicular), which is the Hilbert-space situation (FA.6); in general it is not (§06 C2).
- **`thm-ball-compact-iff-finite-dim`** (key proof). For a normed space \(X\), the closed unit ball is compact if and only if \(\dim X < \infty\).
  - Proof: (⇐) Heine–Borel after a bi-Lipschitz identification with \(\mathbb K^n\). (⇒) If \(\dim X = \infty\), build \(x_1, x_2, \dots\) with \(\|x_n\| = 1\) and \(\|x_n - x_m\| \ge \tfrac12\): \(Y_n = \operatorname{span}(x_1, \dots, x_n)\) is closed (`prp-finite-dim-subspace-closed`) and proper, apply `lem-riesz` with \(\theta = \tfrac12\). No convergent subsequence.
  - Corollary sentence: an infinite-dimensional normed space is not locally compact (spiral: AN2.3's \(\ell^2\), AN2.8).
- **Quick check.** In \(\ell^p\), what \(x_n\) does the proof produce, essentially? (*Answer:* \(e_n\), with \(\|e_n - e_m\|_p = 2^{1/p}\).)
- **Warning.** "Closed and bounded ⇒ compact" is a theorem about \(\mathbb K^n\), not about normed spaces.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1** (key-proof comprehension, 5 parts): (a) where does the proof of `thm-ball-compact-iff-finite-dim` use that \(Y_n\) is closed? (b) why \(\theta = \tfrac12\) and not 1? (c) does the proof need \(X\) complete? (*no*) (d) true/false: the closed unit ball of \(C[0,1]\) is compact (*false*; witness \(x^n\) or tents with disjoint supports); (e) negate "every linear map on \(X\) is bounded".
  - **B1.** Give \(f_n \in C[0,1]\), \(\|f_n\|_\infty = 1\), \(\|f_n - f_m\|_\infty = 1\) for \(n \ne m\). (*Answer:* tents of height 1 supported in disjoint intervals \([2^{-n-1}, 2^{-n}]\).)
  - **B2.** A linear map \(T \colon (c_{00}, \|\cdot\|_1) \to Y\) is bounded iff \(\sup_n \|Te_n\| < \infty\), and then \(\|T\| = \sup_n \|Te_n\|\). (*Answer:* \(\|Tx\| \le \sum |x_n|\|Te_n\|\); test vectors \(e_n\).)
  - **C1.** Let \(\|\cdot\|\) be any norm on \(c_{00}\) for which every coordinate functional \(\delta_n(x) = x_n\) is bounded. Show \((c_{00}, \|\cdot\|)\) is not complete. (*Answer:* the series \(\sum_n 2^{-n} e_n/\|e_n\|\) is absolutely convergent; if it converged to \(s \in c_{00}\), continuity of \(\delta_n\) would give \(s_n = 2^{-n}/\|e_n\| \ne 0\) for every \(n\), so \(s\) has infinite support; `thm-banach-iff-absolute-series`. The solution adds that the general statement, "no Banach space has a countable Hamel basis", is proved by Baire in FA.4.)
  - **C2** (optional; cut if the section runs long). Show that in `lem-riesz` the vector \(x\) can be chosen in any subspace \(Z\) with \(Y \subsetneq Z\). (*Answer:* apply the lemma in the normed space \(Z\), where \(Y\) is still closed and proper.)

## 05-quotients-and-completions.md — Subspaces, Quotients, and Completions

Budget ≤ 4,000 main-line words.

- **`prp-closure-subspace`.** The closure of a subspace is a subspace (continuity of the operations).
- **`prp-closed-subspace-banach`.** A subspace of a Banach space is Banach iff it is closed (AN2.2: closed in complete ⇔ complete).
- **`exm-c00-dense`.** \(c_{00}\) is dense in \(c_0\) and in \(\ell^p\) for \(p < \infty\) (tails); its closure in \(\ell^\infty\) is \(c_0\), so \(c_{00}\) is not dense in \(\ell^\infty\) (\(\|(1,1,\dots) - x\|_\infty \ge 1\) for \(x \in c_0\)).
- **Failure-motivated quotient norm.** On \(X/M = \{x + M\}\), the only sensible size of a coset is its distance from 0: \(\|x + M\| = \inf_{m \in M} \|x - m\| = \operatorname{dist}(x, M)\).
  - **`prp-quotient-seminorm`.** This is always a seminorm on \(X/M\); it is a norm iff \(M\) is closed. Weird example: \(c_0/c_{00}\), where every coset has "norm" 0 because \(c_{00}\) is dense.
  - **`def-quotient-norm`** for closed \(M\).
- **`thm-quotient-banach`.** If \(X\) is Banach and \(M\) a closed subspace, then \(X/M\) is Banach.
  - Proof (second use of *telescope a fast subsequence*, through `thm-banach-iff-absolute-series`; Proof Idea, no Scratch Work): given \(\sum \|x_k + M\| < \infty\), choose \(m_k \in M\) with \(\|x_k - m_k\| < \|x_k + M\| + 2^{-k}\); \(\sum (x_k - m_k)\) converges in \(X\) to \(s\); \(\|\sum_{k \le N} x_k + M - (s + M)\| \le \|\sum_{k \le N}(x_k - m_k) - s\| \to 0\).
- **`prp-quotient-map`.** \(\pi \colon X \to X/M\) is linear, \(\|\pi\| \le 1\), and \(\|\pi\| = 1\) if \(M \ne X\) (by `lem-riesz`); \(\pi\) maps the open unit ball of \(X\) onto the open unit ball of \(X/M\) (forward pointer: this is the model case of the open mapping theorem, FA.4).
- **`prp-induced-operator`.** If \(T \in \mathcal B(X, Y)\) and \(M \subseteq \ker T\) is closed, there is a unique \(\tilde T \in \mathcal B(X/M, Y)\) with \(\tilde T \pi = T\), and \(\|\tilde T\| = \|T\|\). Also: \(\ker T\) is closed for every bounded \(T\). Proof: well defined on cosets; \(\|\tilde T(x + M)\| = \|T(x - m)\| \le \|T\|\|x - m\|\), inf over \(m\); \(\ge\) from \(\tilde T\pi = T\), \(\|\pi\| \le 1\).
- **`exm-linfty-mod-c0`** (worked example). In \(\ell^\infty / c_0\), \(\|x + c_0\| = \limsup_n |x_n|\). (≤: subtract the first \(N\) coordinates, an element of \(c_{00} \subseteq c_0\). ≥: for \(m \in c_0\), \(\sup_n |x_n - m_n| \ge \limsup |x_n - m_n| = \limsup |x_n|\).) Second use of *norm by two inequalities*.
- **`prp-product-banach`.** For normed \(X, Y\), \(\|(x,y)\| = \|x\| + \|y\|\) on \(X \times Y\) is a norm inducing the product topology (equivalent to \(\max\)); \(X \times Y\) is Banach iff \(X\) and \(Y\) are. (Coordinatewise Cauchy. Needed for the closed graph theorem, FA.4.)
- **`thm-normed-completion`.** Every normed space \(X\) is (linearly, isometrically) a dense subspace of a Banach space \(\hat X\), unique up to an isometric linear bijection fixing \(X\).
  - Proof: AN2.2's metric completion \(\hat X\); addition and scalar multiplication are uniformly continuous on bounded pieces (`prp-norm-continuous`), so they extend (the *extend by density* move, applied to \(X \times X \to X\)); the norm extends as distance to 0; the identities of a vector space and of a norm pass to limits. Uniqueness: extend the identity map of \(X\) by `thm-blt-extension` both ways.
  - Examples (prose): the completion of \((c_{00}, \|\cdot\|_p)\) is \(\ell^p\) (\(p < \infty\)), of \((c_{00}, \|\cdot\|_\infty)\) is \(c_0\); of \((C[0,1], \|\cdot\|_1)\) is \(L^1[0,1]\) (forward pointer: MT.7 proves \(L^1\) complete and \(C[0,1]\) dense in it).
- **Quick check.** Is \(\{x \in \ell^1 : \sum x_n = 0\}\) closed in \(\ell^1\)? In \((c_{00}, \|\cdot\|_\infty)\) (the same condition)? (*Answer:* yes — kernel of the bounded functional \(\sum x_n\) on \(\ell^1\); no — on \((c_{00}, \|\cdot\|_\infty)\) the functional is unbounded and the kernel is dense: \(e_1 - \tfrac1N(e_2 + \cdots + e_{N+1}) \to e_1\).)

## 06-dual-spaces.md — Dual Spaces

Budget ≤ 5,000 main-line words.

- **Failure-motivated definition: `exm-algebraic-dual-too-big`.** The space of *all* linear functionals on \(c_{00}\) contains \(\varphi(x) = \sum n x_n\) (§04), which carries no information compatible with the norm (its kernel is dense). Keep only continuous ones.
- **`def-dual-space`.** \(X^* = \mathcal B(X, \mathbb K)\) with the operator norm. **`prp-dual-banach`**: \(X^*\) is a Banach space for every normed \(X\) (`thm-bounded-operators-complete` with \(Y = \mathbb K\)); in particular the dual of an incomplete space is complete.
- **`prp-functional-kernel`.** A linear \(\varphi \ne 0\) on \(X\) is bounded iff \(\ker \varphi\) is closed; then \(\operatorname{dist}(x, \ker\varphi) = |\varphi(x)|/\|\varphi\|\) (the formula is B2). Proof of ⇐: choose \(x_0\) with \(\varphi(x_0) = 1\), \(d = \operatorname{dist}(x_0, \ker \varphi) > 0\); for \(\varphi(x) \ne 0\), \(x/\varphi(x) - x_0 \in \ker\varphi\), so \(\|x\|/|\varphi(x)| \ge d\).
- **Second hook (open).** For a given \(X\), what is \(X^*\)? Answer it where the norm is explicit.
- **`thm-dual-lp`** (key proof). Let \(1 \le p < \infty\). The map \(\Phi \colon \ell^{p'} \to (\ell^p)^*\), \(\Phi(y)(x) = \sum_n x_n y_n\), is a linear isometric bijection.
  - **Scratch Work** (first appearance of *test against the equality case of Hölder*). Hölder gives \(\|\Phi(y)\| \le \|y\|_{p'}\). For the other direction, look at when Hölder is an equality: \(|x_n|^p\) proportional to \(|y_n|^{p'}\), with phases aligned, i.e. \(x_n = |y_n|^{p'-1}\,\overline{\operatorname{sgn} y_n}\). Truncate to \(n \le N\) so the vector is in \(\ell^p\) whatever \(y\) is.
  - Proof. Injective and isometric need \(\|\Phi(y)\| \ge \|y\|_{p'}\); surjective: given \(\varphi\), set \(y_n = \varphi(e_n)\). Since \(c_{00}\) is dense in \(\ell^p\) (\(p < \infty\)), \(x = \lim_N \sum_{n \le N} x_n e_n\), so \(\varphi(x) = \sum x_n y_n\) by continuity. Show \(y \in \ell^{p'}\), \(\|y\|_{p'} \le \|\varphi\|\): for \(p > 1\), with \(x^{(N)}_n = |y_n|^{p'-1}\overline{\operatorname{sgn} y_n}\) (\(n \le N\)), \(\varphi(x^{(N)}) = S_N := \sum_{n \le N} |y_n|^{p'}\) and \(\|x^{(N)}\|_p = S_N^{1/p}\) (since \((p'-1)p = p'\)), so \(S_N \le \|\varphi\| S_N^{1/p}\) and \(S_N^{1/p'} \le \|\varphi\|\); let \(N \to \infty\). For \(p = 1\), \(|y_n| = |\varphi(e_n)| \le \|\varphi\|\). The same computation applied to \(\varphi = \Phi(y)\) gives the isometry.
  - **Warning.** The proof uses density of \(c_{00}\); for \(p = \infty\) it is not dense and the statement fails (below).
- **`thm-dual-c0`.** \(\Phi \colon \ell^1 \to c_0^*\), \(\Phi(y)(x) = \sum x_n y_n\), is a linear isometric bijection. Proof: as above with \(x^{(N)}_n = \overline{\operatorname{sgn} y_n}\) for \(n \le N\) (second use of the move; Proof Idea only).
- **`exm-norm-not-attained`.** \(\varphi = \Phi(y)\) on \(c_0\) with \(y_n = 2^{-n}\): \(\|\varphi\| = \|y\|_1 = 1\), but \(|\varphi(x)| < 1\) whenever \(\|x\|_\infty \le 1\) (equality would force \(|x_n| = 1\) for all \(n\)). The sup in `def-operator-norm` is not a max.
- **What fails at \(p = \infty\)** (remark, forward pointer). \(\Phi \colon \ell^1 \to (\ell^\infty)^*\) is still a linear isometry (same proof, test vectors in \(c_{00}\)), but not onto: on \(c\), \(\lim\) is a bounded functional vanishing on \(c_0\); *if* it extends to \(\psi \in (\ell^\infty)^*\) and \(\psi = \Phi(y)\), then \(y_n = \psi(e_n) = 0\), so \(\psi = 0\), contradicting \(\psi(1,1,\dots) = 1\). The extension exists by Hahn–Banach (FA.3); the hook "is \((\ell^\infty)^* = \ell^1\)?" is paid there.
- **`exm-functionals-on-ck`** (worked examples; the dual of \(C(K)\) itself is FA.2). On \(C[a,b]\) or \(C(K)\):
  - \(\delta_t\), \(\|\delta_t\| = 1\); \(\varphi = \sum_{k \le n} c_k \delta_{t_k}\) for distinct \(t_k\), \(\|\varphi\| = \sum |c_k|\) (test with a continuous \(f\), \(\|f\|_\infty \le 1\), \(f(t_k) = \overline{\operatorname{sgn} c_k}\): piecewise linear on \([a,b]\); on a metric \(K\), \(f = \sum \overline{\operatorname{sgn} c_k}\,\max(0, 1 - \operatorname{dist}(\cdot, t_k)/r)\) with \(2r < \min_{j \ne k} d(t_j, t_k)\)).
  - \(\varphi_g(f) = \int_a^b f g\) for \(g \in C[a,b]\): \(\|\varphi_g\| = \int_a^b |g|\) (third use of the \(f_\varepsilon = \bar g/(|g| + \varepsilon)\) trick from §03).
  - \(f \mapsto \int_a^b f\,d\alpha\) for \(\alpha\) of bounded variation (AN2.9, AN2.10): \(\|\cdot\| \le V_a^b(\alpha)\). Prose: every element of \(C[a,b]^*\) has this form (F. Riesz's theorem; proved in FA.2 in the general form for \(C(K)\)). No proof here.
- **`def-canonical-embedding`.** \(J \colon X \to X^{**}\), \((Jx)(\varphi) = \varphi(x)\); linear, \(\|Jx\| \le \|x\|\). That \(J\) is always an isometry needs Hahn–Banach (FA.3, forward pointer); for the spaces below it is checked by hand.
- **`def-reflexive`.** \(X\) is reflexive if \(J\) is onto.
- **`prp-lp-reflexive`.** For \(1 < p < \infty\), \(J\) on \(\ell^p\) is an isometric bijection onto \((\ell^p)^{**}\). Proof: \(\|Jx\| = \sup_{\|y\|_{p'} \le 1} |\sum x_n y_n| = \|x\|_p\) by `thm-dual-lp` for \(p'\). Onto: given \(\Psi \in (\ell^p)^{**}\), \(\Psi \circ \Phi_p \in (\ell^{p'})^*\) equals \(\Phi_{p'}(x)\) for some \(x \in \ell^p\) (`thm-dual-lp` for \(p'\), allowed since \(p' < \infty\)); unwind: \(\Psi(\Phi_p(y)) = \sum y_n x_n = (Jx)(\Phi_p(y))\) for all \(y\), and \(\Phi_p\) is onto, so \(\Psi = Jx\).
- **`prp-c0-not-reflexive`.** Under \(c_0^* \cong \ell^1\) and \((\ell^1)^* \cong \ell^\infty\), \(J \colon c_0 \to c_0^{**}\) becomes the inclusion \(c_0 \hookrightarrow \ell^\infty\), which is isometric and not onto. (Old exit check; kept.) Prose: \(\ell^1\) is not reflexive either, but that needs \((\ell^\infty)^*\) (FA.3).
- **`def-banach-adjoint`**, **`prp-banach-adjoint-norm`.** For \(T \in \mathcal B(X,Y)\), \(T^* \colon Y^* \to X^*\), \(T^*\psi = \psi \circ T\); \(T^* \in \mathcal B(Y^*, X^*)\) with \(\|T^*\| \le \|T\|\) (equality needs Hahn–Banach, FA.3). **`exm-shift-adjoints`**: under \((\ell^p)^* \cong \ell^{p'}\) (\(1 \le p < \infty\)), \(R^* = L\) and \(L^* = R\) on \(\ell^{p'}\); \(M_a^* = M_a\) (no conjugate: the pairing is bilinear; contrast FA.9's Hilbert adjoint, where \(M_a^* = M_{\bar a}\)).
- **Quick checks.** (1) What is \(\|\Phi(y)\|\) for \(y = (1, -1, 0, 0, \dots)\) on \(\ell^2\)? On \(\ell^1\)? (*\(\sqrt2\); 1.*) (2) Is \(c_0^*\) separable? (*Yes, it is \(\ell^1\).*) Is \(\ell^1\) the dual of a separable space? (*Yes, of \(c_0\).*)
