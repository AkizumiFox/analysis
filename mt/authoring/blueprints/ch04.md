**Chapter hook and its payment.**

## index.md

- **Story.**
  - MT.1 built Lebesgue measure by covering sets with cubes; MT.2 built the integral from it. Look back at those proofs: after m* was defined, cubes were never used again. Only countable additivity, monotonicity and the closure of measurable sets under countable operations did any work.
  - That observation makes measure theory portable. A measure is any countably additive set function on a σ-algebra. Counting measure, point masses, probability on coin tosses, and "length weighted by an increasing F" are all measures, and every theorem of MT.2 holds for all of them.
  - The question is how to build one. The chapter's answer is Carathéodory's: start from a rough size on simple sets, take the induced outer measure, and keep exactly the sets that split every other set additively.
  - The test case is the Cantor function F (hook above), whose "dF" puts all its mass on a set of length zero.
- **What you need:** MT.1 and MT.2 throughout; AN2.1 (metric spaces, distance to a closed set) for §01 and §03; AN1.4 (open sets of ℝ, Heine–Borel) for §01 and §05; F2.3 (countability, choice) for §01, §02, §05; AN2.10 (Riemann–Stieltjes) for §05–§06.

## 01-sigma-algebras.md — σ-Algebras and Measurable Maps

Budget: ≈ 4,200 words. Scratch Work: the good-sets principle (first appearance in the volume).

- **Opening frame.** MT.1 proved that Lebesgue-measurable sets survive complements and countable unions, and MT.2 used nothing else about them. Name that structure.
- **Failure-motivated definition.**
  - Try "finite unions only": the **algebra** of finite disjoint unions of intervals (a, b] in (0, 1] (`def-algebra-of-sets`: contains X, closed under complements and finite unions).
  - The functions fₙ = 𝟙 of (1/2, 1] ∪ (1/4, 1/3] ∪ ⋯ ∪ (1/(2n), 1/(2n−1)] are indicators of sets in the algebra and increase pointwise to the indicator of an infinite union of intervals that is not a finite union of intervals. So pointwise limits leave "finite unions". The integral's limit theorems need **countable** unions.
  - `def-sigma-algebra`: 𝓜 ⊆ P(X) with X ∈ 𝓜, closed under complements and countable unions. Consequences in one display: ∅, countable intersections, differences, lim sup and lim inf of sequences of sets.
  - `def-measurable-space` (X, 𝓜).
- **Examples:** P(X); {∅, X}; the σ-algebra of a countable partition (unions of blocks); `exm-countable-cocountable`: on any set X, the sets that are countable or have countable complement (proof uses F2.3: a countable union of countable sets is countable; if one of the sets is cocountable, so is the union). The Lebesgue σ-algebra 𝓛(ℝᵈ) of MT.1.
- **Non-example by minimal change:** the countable sets alone, on uncountable X (not closed under complements).
- **`prp-intersection-of-sigma-algebras`** (any family, proof one line) and **`def-generated-sigma-algebra`** σ(𝓔): the intersection of all σ-algebras containing 𝓔.
  - **Warning.** σ(𝓔) has no "formula": it is *not* in general obtained by one round of countable unions and complements of members of 𝓔. Say so in one sentence; do not discuss transfinite hierarchies.
- **`def-borel-sigma-algebra`:** for a metric space (X, d), 𝓑(X) = σ(open sets). Equivalently σ(closed sets).
- **`thm-borel-generators-line`.** 𝓑(ℝ) is generated by each of: the open intervals; the rays (a, ∞); the rays (a, ∞) with a ∈ ℚ; the half-open intervals (a, b]. For ℝᵈ: generated by the half-open boxes ∏(aᵢ, bᵢ], and also by those with dyadic rational endpoints.
  - **Scratch Work** (first appearance of the good-sets principle), set apart from the proof: to show σ(rays) ⊇ open sets, it suffices that each open set is built from rays by countable operations; (a, b) = (a, ∞) ∖ ⋂ₙ (b − 1/n, ∞); then AN1.4 writes each open set as a countable union of open intervals. The other inclusion is free because rays are open. Then state the principle in general: "σ(𝓔) ⊆ 𝓜 as soon as 𝓔 ⊆ 𝓜 and 𝓜 is a σ-algebra."
  - Proof for ℝᵈ: every open set is a countable union of dyadic half-open cubes (each point lies in a dyadic cube inside the set; there are countably many dyadic cubes). Cite MT.1 if it proved this decomposition; otherwise prove in three lines.
- **Measurable maps.**
  - `def-measurable-map`: f : (X, 𝓜) → (Y, 𝓝) with f⁻¹(N) ∈ 𝓜 for every N ∈ 𝓝. `def-measurable-function`: f : X → [−∞, ∞] with {f > a} ∈ 𝓜 for all a ∈ ℝ (equivalently, measurable into 𝓑([−∞, ∞]); state, prove as B exercise). "Borel measurable" when 𝓜 = 𝓑(X); "Lebesgue measurable" when 𝓜 = 𝓛.
  - `lem-measurable-on-generators`: if 𝓝 = σ(𝓔), it suffices that f⁻¹(E) ∈ 𝓜 for E ∈ 𝓔. Proof: {N : f⁻¹(N) ∈ 𝓜} is a σ-algebra (preimages commute with complements and unions): good-sets principle, second use, written without Scratch Work.
  - `cor-continuous-borel`: continuous maps between metric spaces are Borel measurable; compositions of measurable maps are measurable.
- **`thm-measurable-closure`.** For measurable f, g, fₙ : X → [−∞, ∞] on (X, 𝓜): sup fₙ, inf fₙ, lim sup fₙ, lim inf fₙ, and lim fₙ where it exists, are measurable; f + g (where defined, i.e. no ∞ − ∞) and fg (with 0 · ∞ = 0) are measurable; f⁺, f⁻, |f| are measurable; {x : lim fₙ(x) exists} ∈ 𝓜.
  - Proof route: {sup fₙ > a} = ⋃ {fₙ > a}; lim sup = inf over k of sup over n ≥ k; {f + g > a} = ⋃_{q ∈ ℚ} {f > q} ∩ {g > a − q} for real-valued f, g, then the extended case by splitting on {f = ±∞}; fg via (f + g)² − (f − g)² for real-valued, then cases. Say in one sentence that these are MT.1's proofs with 𝓛 replaced by 𝓜, and that the transfer works because MT.1's proofs used only σ-algebra operations.
- **`thm-simple-approximation-abstract`.** For measurable f : X → [0, ∞] there are simple measurable φ₁ ≤ φ₂ ≤ ⋯ with φₙ → f pointwise, and uniformly on any set where f is bounded. Explicit φₙ = min(n, 2⁻ⁿ⌊2ⁿ f⌋). Proof: the MT.1 proof verbatim (cite its label), with one display checking φₙ ≤ φₙ₊₁.
- **Warnings.**
  - A function can have |g| Borel without g being even Lebesgue measurable: g = 𝟙_V − 𝟙_{ℝ∖V} with V the Vitali set of MT.1.
  - **Composition** g ∘ h of a Lebesgue-measurable g with a continuous h need not be Lebesgue measurable. The reason: g⁻¹(Borel) is only a Lebesgue set, and a continuous h can pull a Lebesgue set back to a non-Lebesgue set. (Composition is safe when the outer function is Borel.) The witness is built in §05 (`exm-lebesgue-not-borel`); state the promise.
- **Quick check.** List σ({{1}, {2}}) on X = {1, 2, 3, 4}. Answer (folded): the 8 unions of the blocks {1}, {2}, {3, 4}.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Which are σ-algebras on ℕ? (i) the finite-or-cofinite sets; (ii) {E : E ∩ {0, 1} is ∅ or {0, 1}}; (iii) {E : 0 ∈ E}; (iv) {∅, evens, odds, ℕ}. Answers: (i) an algebra but not a σ-algebra (the evens are a countable union of singletons); (ii) yes; (iii) no (not closed under complements, and ∅ is missing); (iv) yes.
  - **A2.** Show f : X → ℝ is measurable iff {f > q} ∈ 𝓜 for all q ∈ ℚ. Answer: {f > a} = ⋃_{q > a} {f > q}.
  - **B1.** If X is countable and 𝓜 contains every singleton then 𝓜 = P(X). Answer: each E is a countable union of singletons.
  - **B2.** 𝓑(ℝ) = σ(compact sets). Answer: compact sets are closed; every closed F = ⋃ (F ∩ [−n, n]).
  - **B3.** f : X → [−∞, ∞] is measurable iff f⁻¹(B) ∈ 𝓜 for every Borel B ⊆ [−∞, ∞] (with 𝓑([−∞, ∞]) generated by the rays (a, ∞]). Answer: `lem-measurable-on-generators`.
  - **B4.** For f : X → Y and a σ-algebra 𝓝 on Y, {f⁻¹(N) : N ∈ 𝓝} is a σ-algebra on X, the smallest making f measurable.
  - **C1.** No σ-algebra is countably infinite. Answer outline: an infinite σ-algebra contains a sequence of nonempty pairwise disjoint sets (build by splitting: some atom-like set has infinitely many members of 𝓜 below it, or use the finer partition by the sets ⋂ Eᵢ^{±}); the unions over subsets of ℕ are distinct, so there are at least 2^ℵ₀ sets.
  - **C2.** On ℝ, σ(singletons) is the countable–cocountable σ-algebra; deduce that it is strictly smaller than 𝓑(ℝ) ((0, 1) is neither countable nor cocountable, using F2.3).

## 02-measures.md — Measures, Null Sets and Completion

Budget: ≈ 4,000 words. No new Scratch Work (techniques already met in MT.1).

- **Opening frame.** Lebesgue measure on 𝓛 is countably additive. Make that the definition.
- **`def-measure`:** μ : 𝓜 → [0, ∞] with μ(∅) = 0 and μ(⋃Eⱼ) = Σ μ(Eⱼ) for pairwise disjoint Eⱼ ∈ 𝓜. **`def-measure-space`.** Why μ(∅) = 0 is stated: the function ≡ ∞ is countably additive otherwise.
- **`exm-counting-dirac`.** Counting measure # on P(X); δₓ; weighted point masses Σ w(x)δₓ on P(X) for w : X → [0, ∞] (sum of nonnegative terms over an arbitrary index set, defined as a sup of finite sums, one line); Lebesgue m on 𝓛(ℝᵈ) and on 𝓑(ℝᵈ); restriction μ|_E(A) = μ(A ∩ E); sums and nonnegative multiples of measures.
- **`def-pushforward`:** for measurable T : (X, 𝓜) → (Y, 𝓝), T_*μ(N) = μ(T⁻¹N). It is a measure (B exercise). Example: m on [0, 1) pushed forward by x ↦ 2x mod 1 is m on [0, 1) again (check on intervals; uniqueness from §04 finishes it — forward pointer, phrased as a later exercise).
- **`exm-cocountable-measure`.** On uncountable X with the countable–cocountable σ-algebra, μ(E) = 0 if E is countable, 1 if E is cocountable. Countable additivity: among disjoint sets at most one is cocountable. Every point is null, yet μ takes only the values 0 and 1. (Enters the weird-examples table.)
- **`thm-measure-properties`.** Monotone; countably subadditive; μ(B ∖ A) = μ(B) − μ(A) if A ⊆ B and μ(A) < ∞; continuity from below; continuity from above **when μ(E₁) < ∞**.
  - Proof: MT.1's proofs with m replaced by μ (disjointification Bⱼ = Eⱼ ∖ ⋃_{i<j} Eᵢ). Write the continuity from above in full: it is where finiteness enters.
  - **Warning with witness:** Eₙ = {n, n+1, …} under counting measure decreases to ∅ with every μ(Eₙ) = ∞. Lebesgue: (n, ∞).
- **`thm-borel-cantelli`.** If Σ μ(Eₙ) < ∞ then μ(lim sup Eₙ) = 0. Proof: lim sup Eₙ ⊆ ⋃_{n ≥ k} Eₙ for every k, and the tail of the series → 0. (First Borel–Cantelli lemma; MT.8 and PR.1 cite it.)
  - Worked example: for a.e. x ∈ [0, 1], |x − p/q| < q⁻³ has only finitely many solutions p/q. Eq = ⋃ₚ (p/q − q⁻³, p/q + q⁻³) ∩ [0, 1] has measure ≤ (q + 1) · 2q⁻³, summable.
- **`def-sigma-finite`, finite, probability measure.** Examples: m is σ-finite; # on ℕ is σ-finite; # on ℝ is not (a countable union of finite sets is countable, F2.3). **Quick check**: is # on ℝ complete? Is it σ-finite? (Yes; no.)
- **`def-null-set`** and "μ-almost everywhere". Warning: "μ-a.e." depends on μ: under δ₀, "f = g a.e." means only f(0) = g(0).
- **Failure-motivated `def-complete-measure`.** MT.1's Lebesgue measure has the property that every subset of a null set is measurable (and null). Its restriction to 𝓑(ℝ) does not: there is a subset of the Cantor set that is not Borel. **Section hook:** the witness needs the Cantor function and is built in §05 (`exm-lebesgue-not-borel`). Definition: every subset of a null set belongs to 𝓜.
- **`thm-completion`.** Let (X, 𝓜, μ) be a measure space, 𝒩 its null sets. Then 𝓜̄ = {E ∪ F : E ∈ 𝓜, F ⊆ N for some N ∈ 𝒩} is a σ-algebra, μ̄(E ∪ F) = μ(E) is well defined, and μ̄ is the unique measure on 𝓜̄ extending μ; it is complete. Every complete measure extending μ extends μ̄.
  - Proof sketch: closure under countable unions is immediate (a countable union of null sets is null). Complements: replace F by F ∖ E and N by N ∖ E, so E ∩ N = ∅; then (E ∪ F)ᶜ = (E ∪ N)ᶜ ∪ (N ∖ F), of the required form. Well defined: if E₁ ∪ F₁ = E₂ ∪ F₂ then E₁ ⊆ E₂ ∪ N₂, so μ(E₁) ≤ μ(E₂); symmetric. Countable additivity and uniqueness are routine.
- **`lem-completion-measurable-function`.** If f : X → [−∞, ∞] is 𝓜̄-measurable, there is an 𝓜-measurable g with f = g μ̄-a.e. Proof: the ladder on sets: for f = 𝟙_{E ∪ F} take g = 𝟙_E; for simple f by linearity; for f ≥ 0 take the simple φₙ ↑ f, choose 𝓜-measurable ψₙ = φₙ off a null Nₙ ∈ 𝓜, and set g = lim ψₙ off N = ⋃ Nₙ, g = 0 on N; general f by f⁺ − f⁻. (MT.4 cites it for Fubini on completed products.)
- **`thm-lebesgue-completes-borel`.** 𝓛(ℝᵈ) is the completion of 𝓑(ℝᵈ) under m. Proof: ⊇ because m on 𝓛 is complete (MT.1) and 𝓑 ⊆ 𝓛. ⊆: by MT.1's characterization, E ∈ 𝓛 equals H ∪ Z with H an F_σ ⊆ E and m*(Z) = 0; Z lies in a null G_δ (MT.1), so E = H ∪ Z is of the form in `thm-completion`.

## 03-caratheodory.md — Outer Measures and Carathéodory's Theorem

Budget: ≈ 4,800 words (the chapter's key section). Scratch Work twice: split the test set (Carathéodory), peel off by distance (metric outer measures).

- **Opening frame (failure).** Why not define a measure on all subsets? MT.1's Vitali argument: the rational translates V + q (q ∈ ℚ ∩ [−1, 1]) are disjoint, lie in [−1, 2], and all have the same outer measure; countable additivity of m* on them would give 3 ≥ Σ m*(V + q) ∈ {0, ∞} with m*(V) > 0 forced by [0, 1] ⊆ ⋃ (V + q). So m* is only subadditive on P(ℝ). We need a test, phrased without geometry, that picks the sets on which m* is additive.
- **`def-outer-measure`:** μ* : P(X) → [0, ∞] with μ*(∅) = 0, monotone, countably subadditive. Examples: m* of MT.1; any measure on P(X); μ*(A) = 0 if A is countable, 1 otherwise (X uncountable); μ*(A) = 1 for every nonempty A.
- **`prp-outer-measure-from-covers`.** Let 𝓔 ⊆ P(X) contain ∅ and let ρ : 𝓔 → [0, ∞] with ρ(∅) = 0. Then μ*(A) = inf{Σⱼ ρ(Eⱼ) : Eⱼ ∈ 𝓔, A ⊆ ⋃ Eⱼ} (inf ∅ = ∞) is an outer measure. Proof: the ε2⁻ʲ argument of MT.1 (cite its label for m*; no Scratch Work, the move is not new). Instances: m* (cubes, volume); μ_F* (§05: intervals (a, b], F(b) − F(a)); Hausdorff (`exm-hausdorff-outer-measure` below).
- **Failure-motivated `def-caratheodory-measurable`.** We want additivity μ*(A ∪ B) = μ*(A) + μ*(B) for disjoint "good" sets. Requiring it only for pairs of good sets is circular; instead demand that a good set A **cut every set T cleanly**: μ*(T) = μ*(T ∩ A) + μ*(T ∖ A) for all T ⊆ X. Remarks: ≤ is free by subadditivity, so only ≥ is checked, and only for μ*(T) < ∞. The definition mentions no topology.
- **`exm-two-point-outer-measure`.** X = {a, b}, μ*(∅) = 0, μ* = 1 on every nonempty set. It is an outer measure; {a} is not measurable (test T = X: 1 < 1 + 1). Only ∅, X are measurable. (Weird-examples table.)
- **`thm-caratheodory`.** If μ* is an outer measure on X, the μ*-measurable sets form a σ-algebra 𝓜, and μ*|𝓜 is a complete measure.
  - **Scratch Work** (first appearance of *split the test set*), apart from the proof: find what to test. For two disjoint measurable A₁, A₂ and any T, test A₂ against T ∩ (A₁ ∪ A₂): this gives μ*(T ∩ (A₁ ∪ A₂)) = μ*(T ∩ A₁) + μ*(T ∩ A₂). That identity, iterated, is the whole engine; the rest is passing to the limit and using subadditivity once.
  - **Proof (typeset in full).** (1) Null sets: μ*(Z) = 0 ⇒ μ*(T ∩ Z) + μ*(T ∖ Z) ≤ 0 + μ*(T); so 𝓜 contains every null set (completeness at the end). (2) Complements: the definition is symmetric in A and Aᶜ. (3) Finite unions: for A, B ∈ 𝓜, μ*(T) = μ*(T ∩ A) + μ*(T ∖ A) = μ*(T ∩ A) + μ*((T ∖ A) ∩ B) + μ*(T ∖ (A ∪ B)) ≥ μ*(T ∩ (A ∪ B)) + μ*(T ∖ (A ∪ B)), since T ∩ (A ∪ B) = (T ∩ A) ∪ ((T ∖ A) ∩ B). (4) For disjoint A₁, …, Aₙ ∈ 𝓜 and Bₙ = A₁ ∪ ⋯ ∪ Aₙ: μ*(T ∩ Bₙ) = Σ_{j ≤ n} μ*(T ∩ Aⱼ), by induction with the Scratch Work identity. (5) Countable disjoint unions: let B = ⋃ Aⱼ. Since Bₙ ∈ 𝓜 and T ∖ Bₙ ⊇ T ∖ B, μ*(T) = μ*(T ∩ Bₙ) + μ*(T ∖ Bₙ) ≥ Σ_{j ≤ n} μ*(T ∩ Aⱼ) + μ*(T ∖ B). Let n → ∞, then use countable subadditivity: μ*(T) ≥ Σⱼ μ*(T ∩ Aⱼ) + μ*(T ∖ B) ≥ μ*(T ∩ B) + μ*(T ∖ B). So B ∈ 𝓜. Taking T = B gives countable additivity. (6) A general countable union is a disjoint union of sets in 𝓜 by (2)–(3). (7) Completeness: by (1) and monotonicity.
- **`thm-lebesgue-caratheodory`.** For Lebesgue outer measure m* on ℝᵈ, a set is m*-measurable in Carathéodory's sense iff it is Lebesgue measurable in MT.1's sense (for every ε > 0 there is an open U ⊇ E with m*(U ∖ E) < ε).
  - **Coordination:** the old MT.1 stub listed "equivalence with Carathéodory's criterion" in its §3. If the final MT.1 blueprint keeps it, this item becomes a **recall** with a citation, and the paragraph pays the volume-level point instead ("so MT.1's measure is the Carathéodory measure of m*"). If MT.1 drops it, prove it here:
  - Proof route. (⇒) Let E be Lebesgue measurable and T arbitrary with m*(T) < ∞. MT.1 gives a G_δ set G ⊇ T with m(G) = m*(T). Then m*(T ∩ E) + m*(T ∖ E) ≤ m(G ∩ E) + m(G ∖ E) = m(G) = m*(T), using monotonicity of m* and additivity of m on 𝓛. (⇐) First for m*(E) < ∞: choose open U ⊇ E with m(U) < m*(E) + ε (MT.1's outer regularity of m*); test E against T = U: m(U) = m*(E) + m*(U ∖ E), so m*(U ∖ E) < ε. General E: the sets E ∩ B(0, k) are Carathéodory measurable (balls are Lebesgue measurable, hence Carathéodory measurable by (⇒), and 𝓜 is closed under intersections), have finite outer measure, and get open Uₖ with m*(Uₖ ∖ (E ∩ B(0, k))) < ε2⁻ᵏ; U = ⋃ Uₖ works.
  - Pays the opening frame: cubes entered only through m*.
- **Metric outer measures.**
  - Motivation: Carathéodory's theorem gives *some* σ-algebra, possibly {∅, X} (`exm-two-point-outer-measure`). For outer measures on metric spaces we want at least the Borel sets.
  - `def-metric-outer-measure`: μ*(A ∪ B) = μ*(A) + μ*(B) whenever d(A, B) > 0.
  - **`thm-metric-outer-borel`.** If μ* is a metric outer measure on a metric space X, every Borel set is μ*-measurable.
  - **Scratch Work** (first appearance of *peel off by distance*): by the good-sets principle it suffices to test a closed F. Points of T ∖ F have positive distance to F, so T ∖ F is the increasing union of Tₙ = {x ∈ T : d(x, F) ≥ 1/n}. Each Tₙ is at positive distance from T ∩ F, so metric additivity gives μ*(T ∩ F) + μ*(Tₙ) ≤ μ*(T). What is missing is μ*(Tₙ) → μ*(T ∖ F): outer measures need not be continuous from below on arbitrary sets. Rings Rₖ = Tₖ₊₁ ∖ Tₖ repair this: rings two or more apart are at positive distance, so the even rings and the odd rings each add up, and the tail of the ring series is what separates μ*(Tₙ) from μ*(T ∖ F).
  - Proof (typeset): assume μ*(T) < ∞. For each N, metric additivity and induction give Σ_{k ≤ N} μ*(R₂ₖ) = μ*(⋃_{k ≤ N} R₂ₖ) ≤ μ*(T); same for odd k. So Σₖ μ*(Rₖ) < ∞. Then T ∖ F = Tₙ ∪ ⋃_{k ≥ n} Rₖ, so μ*(T ∖ F) ≤ μ*(Tₙ) + Σ_{k ≥ n} μ*(Rₖ), and the tail → 0. Hence μ*(T ∩ F) + μ*(T ∖ F) ≤ μ*(T).
    - Distances (one display): Tₙ = {x ∈ T : d(x, F) ≥ 1/n} increases with n, and Rₖ = Tₖ₊₁ ∖ Tₖ = {x ∈ T : 1/(k+1) ≤ d(x, F) < 1/k}. For x ∈ Rₖ and y ∈ Rⱼ with j ≥ k + 2, d(y, F) < 1/j ≤ 1/(k+2), so d(x, y) ≥ d(x, F) − d(y, F) > 1/(k+1) − 1/(k+2) > 0, by the 1-Lipschitz property of d(·, F) (AN2.1).
  - Corollary in prose: m* is a metric outer measure (a cover of A ∪ B by small cubes splits into covers of A and of B once the cubes have diameter < d(A, B); this needs MT.1's fact that m* can be computed with cubes of diameter < δ — cite or prove in two lines by subdividing). Hence 𝓑(ℝᵈ) ⊆ 𝓛(ℝᵈ), recovering MT.1.
  - **`exm-hausdorff-outer-measure`** (statement and definition only; verification is C2). For s ≥ 0 and δ > 0, H^s_δ(A) = inf{Σ (diam Eⱼ)^s : A ⊆ ⋃ Eⱼ, diam Eⱼ < δ}, H^s = lim_{δ→0} H^s_δ (increasing as δ ↓). H^s is a metric outer measure, so Borel sets are H^s-measurable. H⁰ is counting measure. Say in one sentence, without proof and marked as not used later, that H^d on ℝᵈ is a constant multiple of m and that the Cantor set has positive finite H^{log 2/log 3}.
- **Warning.** Measurability is relative to μ*: the same set can be measurable for one outer measure and not for another (`exm-two-point-outer-measure` versus counting measure on {a, b}).
- **Quick check.** For μ*(A) = 0 on countable A and 1 otherwise (X uncountable), which sets are μ*-measurable? Answer: exactly the countable and the cocountable sets; the measure obtained is `exm-cocountable-measure`. (Proof of "only these": if A and Aᶜ are both uncountable, test with T = X: 1 < 1 + 1.)

## 04-extension-and-uniqueness.md — Premeasures, Extension and Uniqueness

Budget: ≈ 4,600 words. Contains the chapter's find-the-error item and the π-λ theorem (the main tool for uniqueness). The monotone class theorem, which MIRA and Folland use, is **not** proved here: MT.4 (ch05) creates `thm-monotone-class` for sections of product sets, and this section only forward-points to it.

- **Opening frame.** Carathéodory gives a measure from an outer measure. In practice we know a "size" only on a small family (intervals, boxes, cylinder sets) and want (a) a measure on the generated σ-algebra that extends it, and (b) to know it is the only one.
- **Failure-motivated `def-premeasure`.** On an algebra 𝓐, finite additivity is not enough: on ℕ with the algebra of finite-or-cofinite sets, μ₀(A) = 0 for finite A and 1 for cofinite A is finitely additive, but ℕ = ⋃ {n} gives 1 ≠ Σ 0. Such a μ₀ can never extend to a measure. Definition: μ₀ : 𝓐 → [0, ∞], μ₀(∅) = 0, and μ₀(⋃Aⱼ) = Σ μ₀(Aⱼ) whenever the Aⱼ ∈ 𝓐 are disjoint **and** ⋃ Aⱼ ∈ 𝓐. σ-finite premeasure: X = ⋃ Xₙ with Xₙ ∈ 𝓐, μ₀(Xₙ) < ∞.
- **`thm-caratheodory-extension`** (Carathéodory–Hahn). Let μ₀ be a premeasure on an algebra 𝓐 and μ* the outer measure induced by covers from 𝓐 (`prp-outer-measure-from-covers`). Then (a) μ* = μ₀ on 𝓐; (b) every A ∈ 𝓐 is μ*-measurable; hence (c) μ = μ*|σ(𝓐) is a measure extending μ₀. (d) If ν is any measure on σ(𝓐) extending μ₀, then ν(E) ≤ μ(E) for all E, with equality when μ(E) < ∞. (e) If μ₀ is σ-finite, μ is the unique extension.
  - Proof. (a) μ* ≤ μ₀ by the one-set cover. If A ⊆ ⋃ Aⱼ, put Bⱼ = A ∩ Aⱼ ∖ ⋃_{i<j} Aᵢ ∈ 𝓐: disjoint with union A ∈ 𝓐, so μ₀(A) = Σ μ₀(Bⱼ) ≤ Σ μ₀(Aⱼ) by the premeasure property and finite-additive monotonicity. (b) Given T and ε, take a cover Aⱼ with Σ μ₀(Aⱼ) ≤ μ*(T) + ε; split μ₀(Aⱼ) = μ₀(Aⱼ ∩ A) + μ₀(Aⱼ ∖ A); the two families cover T ∩ A and T ∖ A. (c) `thm-caratheodory`. (d) For E ∈ σ(𝓐) and any cover E ⊆ ⋃ Aⱼ, ν(E) ≤ Σ ν(Aⱼ) = Σ μ₀(Aⱼ), so ν ≤ μ. If μ(E) < ∞, choose a cover with μ(A ∖ E) < ε where A = ⋃ Aⱼ (possible by the definition of μ* and (a)); ν(A) = μ(A) by continuity from below on finite unions of the Aⱼ; then μ(E) ≤ μ(A) = ν(A) = ν(E) + ν(A ∖ E) ≤ ν(E) + μ(A ∖ E) < ν(E) + ε. (e) Write X = ⋃ Xₙ disjoint with finite μ₀(Xₙ); apply (d) to E ∩ Xₙ and sum. (Alternatively (e) follows from `thm-uniqueness-pi` below; say so in one sentence.)
- **`exm-nonunique-extension`.** X = ℚ, 𝓐 = finite disjoint unions of sets (a, b] ∩ ℚ (a < b real, with unbounded versions), μ₀ = ∞ on every nonempty set, 0 on ∅. σ(𝓐) = P(ℚ) (each singleton is a countable intersection of members of 𝓐). Counting measure and 2·counting measure both extend μ₀ and differ on {0}. μ₀ is not σ-finite. (Weird-examples table.)
- **π- and λ-systems.**
  - Failure motivation for uniqueness: "two measures agreeing on a generating class agree" is false. `exm-generators-not-enough`: X = {1, 2, 3, 4}, 𝓔 = {{1, 2}, {2, 3}}, μ = δ₁ + δ₃, ν = δ₂ + δ₄. They agree on 𝓔 and on X (total 2), and σ(𝓔) = P(X), yet μ({2}) = 0 ≠ 1 = ν({2}). What fails: 𝓔 is not closed under intersection ({2} = {1,2} ∩ {2,3}).
  - `def-pi-system`: closed under finite intersections. `def-lambda-system`: X ∈ 𝓛, B ∖ A ∈ 𝓛 whenever A ⊆ B are in 𝓛, and increasing unions stay in 𝓛. (Name it "λ-system" in text; say Dynkin system is a synonym.) Why λ: the sets where two finite measures agree form one (differences use finiteness; increasing unions use continuity from below), but generally not a σ-algebra (unions of non-nested sets are not controlled).
  - **`thm-pi-lambda`.** If 𝒫 is a π-system, 𝓛 a λ-system, and 𝒫 ⊆ 𝓛, then σ(𝒫) ⊆ 𝓛.
  - Proof route (typeset): (i) a family that is both a π-system and a λ-system is a σ-algebra (complements from X ∖ A; finite unions from complements and intersections; countable unions as increasing unions). (ii) Let ℓ(𝒫) be the smallest λ-system containing 𝒫. For A ∈ 𝒫, 𝓛_A = {B : A ∩ B ∈ ℓ(𝒫)} is a λ-system containing 𝒫, so ⊇ ℓ(𝒫). (iii) Now for A ∈ ℓ(𝒫), 𝓛_A again is a λ-system and by (ii) contains 𝒫, so ⊇ ℓ(𝒫). So ℓ(𝒫) is a π-system, hence a σ-algebra by (i), hence ⊇ σ(𝒫).
  - The move **bootstrap with a π-system** is named here. No Scratch Work (T2 taper; spec/08 C5 says Proof Idea only by AN3); give a three-sentence Idea before the proof explaining why the bootstrap runs twice.
- **`thm-uniqueness-pi`.** Let μ, ν be measures on σ(𝒫), 𝒫 a π-system, with μ = ν on 𝒫, and suppose X = ⋃ₙ Xₙ for some Xₙ ∈ 𝒫 with μ(Xₙ) < ∞ (not necessarily nested or disjoint). Then μ = ν on σ(𝒫).
  - Proof: fix n; {E ∈ σ(𝒫) : μ(E ∩ Xₙ) = ν(E ∩ Xₙ)} is a λ-system (finiteness of μ(Xₙ) = ν(Xₙ) for differences; continuity from below for increasing unions) containing 𝒫 (π-property: E ∩ Xₙ ∈ 𝒫); `thm-pi-lambda` gives μ(E ∩ Xₙ) = ν(E ∩ Xₙ) for **all** E ∈ σ(𝒫). Disjointify: for E ∈ σ(𝒫) apply this to Eₙ = E ∖ ⋃_{i<n} Xᵢ ∈ σ(𝒫), so μ and ν agree on each piece E ∩ (Xₙ ∖ ⋃_{i<n} Xᵢ), and countable additivity sums the pieces.
  - Hypothesis check (for S2a): without "Xₙ ∈ 𝒫 with finite measure", `exm-nonunique-extension` is a counterexample (half-open rational intervals form a π-system).
- **`thm-translation-invariant-unique`.** Let μ be a Borel measure on ℝᵈ, finite on bounded Borel sets, with μ(E + x) = μ(E) for all Borel E and x ∈ ℝᵈ. Then μ = c·m on 𝓑(ℝᵈ), with c = μ((0, 1]ᵈ).
  - Proof: 𝒫 = {∅} ∪ {half-open dyadic cubes ∏(kᵢ2⁻ⁿ, (kᵢ + 1)2⁻ⁿ], n ≥ 0, kᵢ ∈ ℤ} is a π-system (two dyadic cubes are nested or disjoint) generating 𝓑(ℝᵈ) (`thm-borel-generators-line`). (0, 1]ᵈ is a disjoint union of 2^{nd} translates of a dyadic cube Q of side 2⁻ⁿ, so μ(Q) = c·2^{−nd} = c·m(Q). The countably many cubes of side 1 cover ℝᵈ and have finite measure, so `thm-uniqueness-pi` (countable-cover form) gives μ = c·m. MT.5 cites this for m(T E) = |det T| m(E).
- **Monotone classes (forward pointer only).** One paragraph: a second bootstrap tool, the monotone class theorem for algebras, does the same job as π-λ and is proved in MT.4 where products need it; say that either tool would serve there.
- **Find-the-error item** (`STYLE.md` per-chapter requirement; placed as a worked "Spot the flaw" box before the exercises). Flawed proof: "μ and ν agree on 𝓔 and on X. The sets where they agree form a σ-algebra (complements: μ(Eᶜ) = μ(X) − μ(E) = ν(Eᶜ); countable unions: by countable additivity). So they agree on σ(𝓔)." Error: the union step needs disjoint sets; for overlapping sets, agreement on A and B does not give agreement on A ∪ B. Witness: `exm-generators-not-enough`.
- **Quick check.** On X = {1, 2, 3}, is {∅, {1}, {2}, X} a π-system? a λ-system? Answer: π-system yes ({1} ∩ {2} = ∅); not a λ-system (X ∖ {1} = {2, 3} is missing).
- **Exercises.**

  - **B2.** Two finite Borel measures μ, ν on ℝ with μ((−∞, x]) = ν((−∞, x]) for all x are equal. Answer: rays (−∞, x] and ∅ form a π-system generating 𝓑(ℝ); μ(ℝ) = lim μ((−∞, n]).
  - **B3.** Prove part (d) of `thm-caratheodory-extension` in the case μ(E) < ∞.
  - **C1 (exit check).** Prove `thm-uniqueness-pi` from `thm-pi-lambda`, and show it fails without the finiteness hypothesis. Marking scheme: (3) λ-system verification including where finiteness is used; (2) π-system contains generators; (2) the limit; (3) a counterexample with explanation.
  - **C2.** Let 𝓐 be an algebra and μ, ν finite measures on σ(𝓐) that agree on 𝓐. Show μ = ν using only `thm-pi-lambda` (an algebra is a π-system), and then show by `exm-nonunique-extension` that finiteness cannot be dropped.

## 05-lebesgue-stieltjes.md — Lebesgue–Stieltjes Measures on the Line

Budget: ≈ 4,800 words. Scratch Work: none new (the compactness-plus-ε2⁻ʲ argument appeared in MT.1 for m*(Q) = |Q|); the failure that motivates right-continuity is shown as a worked example instead.

- **Opening frame = the chapter hook** (Cantor function, AN2.10). Goal: for increasing F, a Borel measure μ_F with μ_F((a, b]) = F(b) − F(a).
- **Failure-motivated right-continuity.** F = 𝟙_{(0, ∞)} (so F(0) = 0, F(0+) = 1). Then (0, 1] = ⋃ₙ (1/(n+1), 1/n], disjoint, but F(1) − F(0) = 1 while every F(1/n) − F(1/(n+1)) = 0. No measure can satisfy the formula. Only right-continuity is consistent with half-open intervals (a, b]: continuity from above forces μ((a, a + 1/n]) → 0.
- **Standing hypothesis:** F : ℝ → ℝ increasing and right-continuous; F(±∞) = lim_{x→±∞} F(x) ∈ [−∞, ∞].
- **`lem-half-open-algebra`.** Finite disjoint unions of h-intervals (a, b], (a, ∞), (−∞, b], ℝ, ∅ form an algebra 𝓐 on ℝ, and σ(𝓐) = 𝓑(ℝ). Define μ₀ on h-intervals by F(b) − F(a) (with F(±∞)), extended additively. Well definedness by common refinement (one display).
- **`thm-stieltjes-premeasure`.** μ₀ is a σ-finite premeasure on 𝓐.
  - Proof route (typeset; the key proof of the section): reduce to one interval I = (a, b] = ⊔ (aⱼ, bⱼ]. ≥: finite additivity and monotonicity for each finite subfamily. ≤ (the content): given ε, right-continuity at a gives δ with F(a + δ) − F(a) < ε, and at each bⱼ gives δⱼ with F(bⱼ + δⱼ) − F(bⱼ) < ε2⁻ʲ. The open intervals (aⱼ, bⱼ + δⱼ) cover the compact [a + δ, b] (Heine–Borel, AN1.4); a finite subcover and telescoping give F(b) − F(a + δ) ≤ Σ (F(bⱼ + δⱼ) − F(aⱼ)) ≤ Σ μ₀((aⱼ, bⱼ]) + ε. Unbounded intervals: apply to (a, b] ∩ (−n, n] and let n → ∞. σ-finite: (−n, n].
  - Remark in one sentence: this is where right-continuity is used, and it is used at **two** places: at a and at each bⱼ.
- **`thm-lebesgue-stieltjes`.** (a) For each increasing right-continuous F there is a unique Borel measure μ_F with μ_F((a, b]) = F(b) − F(a) for all a < b. (b) μ_F = μ_G iff F − G is constant. (c) Conversely, if μ is a Borel measure on ℝ finite on bounded sets, then F(x) = μ((0, x]) for x ≥ 0 and F(x) = −μ((x, 0]) for x < 0 is increasing, right-continuous, and μ = μ_F.
  - Proof: (a) existence by `thm-caratheodory-extension` and `thm-stieltjes-premeasure`; uniqueness by `thm-uniqueness-pi` (h-intervals form a π-system; Xₙ = (−n, n]). (b) compare increments. (c) monotone by monotonicity of μ; right-continuous by continuity from above (finite measure of (x, x + 1]); μ = μ_F by uniqueness.
  - Also: the Carathéodory σ-algebra 𝓜_F ⊇ 𝓑(ℝ), and (ℝ, 𝓜_F, μ_F) is the completion of (ℝ, 𝓑, μ_F) (one line via `thm-completion` uniqueness and `thm-caratheodory-extension` (d); the drafter may state this without proof as a remark if the argument runs long, and must then mark it as not used later).
- **`prp-stieltjes-atoms`.** μ_F({x}) = F(x) − F(x−); μ_F([a, b]) = F(b) − F(a−), μ_F((a, b)) = F(b−) − F(a), μ_F([a, b)) = F(b−) − F(a−). μ_F has no atoms iff F is continuous. **Warning:** μ_F((a, b]) and μ_F([a, b]) differ exactly by the jump at a.
- **Examples.**
  - F(x) = x: μ_F = m on 𝓑(ℝ) (uniqueness), consistent with MT.1.
  - F = 𝟙_{[0, ∞)}: μ_F = δ₀. F(x) = ⌊x⌋: counting measure on ℤ. F(x) = x + 𝟙_{[0,∞)}(x): m + δ₀ (Quick check below).
  - `exm-rational-atoms`: enumerate ℚ = {q₁, q₂, …}; F(x) = Σ_{qₙ ≤ x} 2⁻ⁿ is increasing, right-continuous, jumps at every rational, and is continuous at every irrational. μ_F = Σ 2⁻ⁿ δ_{qₙ}: a probability measure carried by ℚ, whose F is strictly increasing. (Weird-examples table.)
- **`thm-stieltjes-regularity`.** For E ∈ 𝓜_F: μ_F(E) = inf{μ_F(U) : U ⊇ E open} = sup{μ_F(K) : K ⊆ E compact}.
  - Proof route: outer: from the definition of μ* by covers (aⱼ, bⱼ], enlarge to (aⱼ, bⱼ + δⱼ) with μ_F((bⱼ, bⱼ + δⱼ)) < ε2⁻ʲ (right-continuity). Inner: first E bounded, E ⊆ [−n, n]: apply outer regularity to [−n, n] ∖ E to get open U, then K = [−n, n] ∖ U ⊆ E is compact with μ_F(E ∖ K) ≤ μ_F(U ∖ ([−n,n] ∖ E)) < ε; general E by continuity from below on E ∩ [−n, n]. FA.2 cites this as the model case of a Radon measure.
- **The Cantor function (`lem-cantor-function`).** Define F on [0, 1] by: for x = Σ cᵢ3⁻ⁱ ∈ C with cᵢ ∈ {0, 2}, F(x) = Σ (cᵢ/2)2⁻ⁱ; extend to [0, 1] as constant on each removed interval (value at its endpoints), F = 0 on (−∞, 0), 1 on (1, ∞). Statement: F is well defined, increasing, continuous, constant on each component of [0, 1] ∖ C, and F(C) = [0, 1]. Proof sketch (≈ 300 words): well defined at the two endpoints of a removed interval (the two ternary expansions map to the two binary expansions of one dyadic rational); increasing by comparing first differing digits; continuous because |x − y| < 3⁻ⁿ with x, y ∈ C forces agreement of the first n digits, hence |F(x) − F(y)| ≤ 2⁻ⁿ, and an increasing function whose range is an interval has no jumps. If MT.1 (or AN2.9) already defined the Cantor function with these properties, cite it instead and keep only the statement; the coordinator-notes list this.
- **`exm-cantor-measure`** (pays the chapter hook). μ = μ_F for the Cantor function: μ(ℝ) = 1; μ({x}) = 0 for every x (F continuous); each removed interval J has μ(J) = 0 (F constant on its closure); so μ([0, 1] ∖ C) = 0 (countable union), μ(C) = 1, while m(C) = 0 (MT.1). The mass sits on a Lebesgue-null set, and no single point carries any. Forward pointers (prose only): MT.11 (ch16) calls such a measure singular continuous; MT.10 (ch14) shows this F is not absolutely continuous.
  - Self-similarity (stated, proved in exercise C3): μ(E/3) = μ(E)/2 and μ(E/3 + 2/3) = μ(E)/2 for Borel E ⊆ [0, 1].
- **Paying the §02 and §01 hooks.**
  - **`lem-nonmeasurable-inside`.** Every Lebesgue-measurable E ⊆ ℝ with m(E) > 0 contains a non-measurable subset. Proof route: let V be a Vitali set (one representative of each class of ℝ/ℚ, choice as in F2.3; MT.1 built V in [0, 1], here take representatives for all of ℝ, or translate MT.1's argument). E = ⋃_{q ∈ ℚ} E ∩ (V + q). If each E ∩ (V + q) were measurable, each would have measure 0: a measurable A ⊆ V + q, bounded by first intersecting with [−n, n], has disjoint rational translates A + r (r ∈ ℚ ∩ [0, 1]) inside a bounded set, so m(A) = 0. Then m(E) = 0, a contradiction. (If MT.1 proved this, cite it.)
  - **`exm-lebesgue-not-borel`.** φ(x) = x + F(x) on [0, 1] (F the Cantor function) is a strictly increasing continuous bijection [0, 1] → [0, 2], so a homeomorphism (AN1/AN2.1: a continuous bijection of a compact space onto a metric space). φ maps each removed interval J onto an interval of length m(J) (F is constant on J), so m(φ([0, 1] ∖ C)) = 1 and m(φ(C)) = 1. By `lem-nonmeasurable-inside`, φ(C) contains a non-measurable A. Let B = φ⁻¹(A) ⊆ C. Then m*(B) = 0, so B ∈ 𝓛. If B were Borel, A = (φ⁻¹)⁻¹(B) would be Borel (φ⁻¹ continuous, `cor-continuous-borel`), hence Lebesgue measurable: contradiction. So B ∈ 𝓛 ∖ 𝓑, and 𝓑 is not complete under m (pays §02). Moreover 𝟙_B is Lebesgue measurable, ψ = φ⁻¹ is continuous, and 𝟙_B ∘ ψ = 𝟙_A is not Lebesgue measurable (pays §01's warning).

## 06-abstract-integration.md — Integration on a Measure Space

Budget: ≈ 4,800 words. Last section: R group, weird-examples table, List of Results. No Scratch Work (all proofs follow MT.2's techniques; T2 taper).

- **Opening frame.** Read MT.2's proofs of MCT, Fatou and DCT again. The facts about m that they used are: countable additivity, continuity from below, and the simple-function approximation. All hold on any measure space (§01–§02). So the integral transfers. To make this independent of the route MT.2 took (S&S's route starts with bounded convergence via Egorov on finite measure), this section re-proves MCT directly and derives the rest.
- **`def-integral-simple-abstract`:** for simple φ = Σ aᵢ𝟙_{Aᵢ} ≥ 0 (canonical form), ∫ φ dμ = Σ aᵢ μ(Aᵢ) with 0 · ∞ = 0; independence of the representation (MT.2's proof). **`def-integral-nonnegative-abstract`:** ∫ f dμ = sup{∫ φ dμ : 0 ≤ φ ≤ f simple}.
- **Lemma inside the MCT proof** (named move *integrals of simple functions are measures*): for simple φ ≥ 0, E ↦ ∫_E φ dμ is a measure.
- **`thm-mct-abstract`.** If 0 ≤ f₁ ≤ f₂ ≤ ⋯ are measurable and fₙ → f pointwise, then ∫ fₙ dμ → ∫ f dμ. Proof (typeset in full, the section's key proof): ≤ is monotonicity. For ≥: fix simple 0 ≤ φ ≤ f and t ∈ (0, 1); Eₙ = {fₙ ≥ tφ} increases to X (where φ(x) = 0 trivially; where φ(x) > 0, tφ(x) < f(x)); ∫ fₙ ≥ t∫_{Eₙ} φ → t∫ φ by continuity from below of the measure above; let t → 1 and take sup over φ.
- **`thm-additivity-abstract`.** ∫(f + g) = ∫ f + ∫ g for measurable f, g ≥ 0, and ∫ Σ fₙ = Σ ∫ fₙ. Proof: simple approximants (`thm-simple-approximation-abstract`) plus MCT — first instance of the **indicator–simple–MCT ladder**.
- **`thm-fatou-abstract`.** ∫ lim inf fₙ ≤ lim inf ∫ fₙ for measurable fₙ ≥ 0. Proof: MCT on gₖ = inf_{n ≥ k} fₙ.
- **`def-integrable-abstract`**, ∫ f = ∫ f⁺ − ∫ f⁻; complex-valued by real and imaginary parts; L¹(μ) as classes mod a.e. equality, ‖f‖₁. Linearity and |∫ f| ≤ ∫ |f| (complex case by the rotation trick, as in MT.2; cite).
- **`thm-dct-abstract`.** If fₙ → f a.e., |fₙ| ≤ g a.e., g ∈ L¹(μ), then f ∈ L¹(μ) and ∫ |fₙ − f| dμ → 0. Proof: Fatou on 2g − |fₙ − f|. (Measurability of the a.e. limit: if μ is not complete, f must be assumed measurable or modified on a null set; state the hypothesis "f measurable" explicitly, and point out that on a complete space it is automatic. **S2a should check this hypothesis is stated.**)
- **`thm-l1-complete-abstract`.** L¹(μ) is complete, and an L¹-convergent sequence has an a.e.-convergent subsequence. Proof: MT.2's proof verbatim (fast Cauchy subsequence, Σ |f_{n_{k+1}} − f_{n_k}| ∈ L¹ by MCT); cite the MT.2 label and list the two lines that used m. MT.7 generalizes to Lᵖ(μ).
- **Counting measure and series.**
  - **`thm-counting-integral`.** On (ℕ, P(ℕ), #): ∫ f d# = Σ f(n) for f ≥ 0; L¹(#) = ℓ¹ and the integral is the sum.
  - **`cor-tonelli-series`.** For a_{nk} ≥ 0, Σₙ Σₖ a_{nk} = Σₖ Σₙ a_{nk} (in [0, ∞]). Proof: `thm-additivity-abstract` (series form) on fₙ(k) = a_{nk}. And for absolutely summable families the same with signs, by DCT. Worked counterexample with signs: a_{nn} = 1, a_{n,n+1} = −1, else 0: the two iterated sums are 0 and 1 — DCT's dominating function fails. (MT.4 revisits this as counting × counting.)
  - Tannery's theorem as a DCT corollary (B3), with a prose pointer to AN1.9/AN2.12 where series limits were handled by hand.
- **`thm-pushforward-integral`.** For measurable T : X → Y and g : Y → [0, ∞] measurable, ∫_Y g d(T_*μ) = ∫_X g ∘ T dμ; the same for g ∈ L¹(T_*μ). Proof: the indicator–simple–MCT ladder, written out as the model. Examples: ∫ f dδₓ = f(x); for μ = Σ w(x)δₓ, ∫ f dμ = Σ w(x)f(x). PR.1 (side branch) reads this as "expectation of g(X) computed with the distribution of X".
- **`thm-density-measure`.** For f ≥ 0 measurable, ν(E) = ∫_E f dμ is a measure, μ(E) = 0 ⇒ ν(E) = 0, and ∫ g dν = ∫ g f dμ for g ≥ 0 measurable. Proof: countable additivity is `thm-additivity-abstract` (series form); the formula by the ladder. MT.11 (ch16) asks for the converse (Radon–Nikodym). - **`thm-stieltjes-agrees`** (pays the chapter hook). Let F be increasing and right-continuous and f continuous on [a, b]. Then the Riemann–Stieltjes integral of AN2.10 satisfies ∫ₐᵇ f dF = ∫_{(a, b]} f dμ_F.
