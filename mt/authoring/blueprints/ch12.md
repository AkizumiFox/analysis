## Chapter hook (opened in the chapter opening; paid in §3)

> **Hook.** On [0, 1], suppose f_n → f almost everywhere and ∫ |f_n|² ≤ 1 for every n. Must ∫ |f_n − f| → 0?

The opening shows why the tools of MT.2 do not answer it. The sequence

  h_n = √(n(n+1)) · 1_{(1/(n+1), 1/n]}

satisfies ∫ h_n² = 1, h_n → 0 at every point, and its smallest possible dominating function sup_n h_n equals √(n(n+1)) on (1/(n+1), 1/n], whose integral Σ 1/√(n(n+1)) diverges (each term exceeds 1/(n+1)). So the dominated convergence theorem cannot be the reason, even though here ∫ h_n = 1/√(n(n+1)) → 0. The hook asks for a theorem that replaces "one integrable dominator" by a condition that an L²-bound supplies. **Paid by** `cor-lp-bounded-vitali` in §3 (yes, and in Lq for every q < 2).

Second hook (opened at the end of §6, paid in MT.9): the Hardy–Littlewood maximal function of a nonzero f ∈ L¹(ℝᵈ) is never integrable, yet it obeys exactly the Markov-type bound t · m({Mf > t}) ≤ C‖f‖₁ that defines weak L¹. Stated as a pointer "in Chapter 13", with no `@ref`. STATUS entry: promise opened `sec-weak-lp` → MT.9 weak (1,1) inequality.

## §1 Five Ways to Converge — `sec-modes-of-convergence`

**Opening.** The hook (above). Then: MT.2 and MT.7 used three notions (pointwise a.e., uniform, Lᵖ). This section adds two and fixes the vocabulary on an arbitrary measure space (X, 𝓜, μ). Throughout, functions are measurable and real- or complex-valued (finite everywhere after changing them on a null set).

**Definitions.**
- `def-convergence-almost-uniform`: f_n → f almost uniformly if for every δ > 0 there is E ∈ 𝓜 with μ(E) < δ and f_n → f uniformly on X \ E.
- `def-convergence-in-measure` (**failure-motivated**). Motivation paragraph: the typewriter (defined here in one line, studied in §2) converges to 0 in every Lᵖ, p < ∞, and at no point. So "Lᵖ ⇒ a.e." is false, and a.e. convergence cannot be the common weakening of Lᵖ convergence and a.e. convergence. What both control is the *size of the set where f_n is far from f*. Definition: f_n → f in measure if for every ε > 0, μ({x : \|f_n(x) − f(x)\| > ε}) → 0.
- `def-cauchy-in-measure`: for every ε > 0, μ({\|f_n − f_m\| > ε}) → 0 as m, n → ∞.

**Statements and proof sketches.**
- `thm-markov-inequality` (Markov's inequality). (X, 𝓜, μ) any measure space, f measurable, 0 < p < ∞, t > 0: μ({\|f\| ≥ t}) ≤ t^{−p} ∫ \|f\|ᵖ dμ. *Proof:* tᵖ 1_{\{|f| ≥ t\}} ≤ \|f\|ᵖ pointwise; integrate (monotonicity of the integral, MT.2). The case p = 2 applied to f − c is Chebyshev's inequality (named in a remark; PR.1 uses it).
  **Scratch Work** (first appearance of the move *convert an integral bound into a bound on a level set*): draw the graph of \|f\|ᵖ and the rectangle of height tᵖ over {\|f\| ≥ t}; the rectangle lies under the graph. The proof is that picture in one line.
- `prp-lp-implies-in-measure`. 1 ≤ p < ∞, f_n, f ∈ Lᵖ(μ), ‖f_n − f‖_p → 0 ⇒ f_n → f in measure. *Proof:* Markov with exponent p applied to f_n − f: μ({\|f_n − f\| > ε}) ≤ ε^{−p}‖f_n − f‖_pᵖ. Also: ‖f_n − f‖_∞ → 0 ⇒ f_n → f uniformly off a null set, hence almost uniformly.
- `prp-almost-uniform-implies-ae-and-measure`. Almost uniform ⇒ a.e. and in measure, on any measure space. *Proof:* (in measure) given ε, δ take E; for n large {\|f_n − f\| > ε} ⊂ E. (a.e.) take E_k with μ(E_k) < 1/k; convergence holds off ⋂_k E_k, a null set.
- `thm-egorov-measure-space` (Egorov's theorem on a measure space). μ(X) < ∞, f_n → f a.e. ⇒ f_n → f almost uniformly. *Proof:* recall MT.1's Egorov on E ⊂ ℝᵈ, m(E) < ∞; its proof used only that m restricted to E is a finite measure: E_{k,N} = ⋃_{n ≥ N} {\|f_n − f\| ≥ 1/k} decreases in N to a null set, so continuity from above (finite measure, MT.3) gives N_k with μ(E_{k,N_k}) < δ 2^{−k}; E = ⋃_k E_{k,N_k}. Written out in full (about 12 lines), with a sentence saying which line needs μ(X) < ∞.
- `cor-ae-implies-in-measure-finite`. μ(X) < ∞, f_n → f a.e. ⇒ f_n → f in measure. *Proof:* `thm-egorov-measure-space` + `prp-almost-uniform-implies-ae-and-measure`. Forward note: the drift (§2) shows μ(X) < ∞ cannot be dropped, and this is the gap MT.1's Egorov exercise ("fails on ℝ with 1_{[n,n+1]}") already showed.

**Worked example** `exm-five-modes-one-sequence`: f_n = 1_{[0, 1/n]} + 1/n on [0, 1] tested against all five modes (answers as in A1 below, worked in the text with a different sequence: g_n(x) = x^n on [0, 1]: a.e. ✓ (x < 1), almost uniformly ✓ (uniform on [0, 1 − δ]), in measure ✓, Lᵖ ✓ for p < ∞ (‖g_n‖_pᵖ = 1/(np + 1)), L^∞ ✗ (‖g_n‖_∞ = 1)).

**Quick check** `.check`: "If f_n → f in L³(μ), give an upper bound for μ({\|f_n − f\| > 1/10}) in terms of ‖f_n − f‖₃." Answer: 1000 ‖f_n − f‖₃³.

**Exercises.**
- **A1** `exr-modes-of-convergence-a1`. In which of the five modes does f_n = 1_{[0, 1/n]} + 1/n → 0 on [0, 1]? *Answer:* a.e. (every x > 0); almost uniformly (on [δ, 1], f_n = 1/n once n > 1/δ); in measure; in Lᵖ for 1 ≤ p < ∞ (‖f_n‖_p ≤ n^{−1/p} + 1/n by Minkowski); not in L^∞ (‖f_n‖_∞ = 1 + 1/n).
- **A2** `exr-modes-of-convergence-a2`. ‖f‖₂ = 3. Bound μ({\|f\| ≥ 6}). *Answer:* ≤ 9/36 = 1/4.
- **B1** `exr-modes-of-convergence-b1`. Sums: f_n → f and g_n → g in measure ⇒ f_n + g_n → f + g in measure. *Answer:* {\|f_n + g_n − f − g\| > ε} ⊂ {\|f_n − f\| > ε/2} ∪ {\|g_n − g\| > ε/2}.
- **B2** `exr-modes-of-convergence-b2`. On ℝ let f_n(x) = x and g_n = 1/n. Show f_n → x and g_n → 0 in measure (indeed uniformly) but f_n g_n does not converge to 0 in measure. *Answer:* {\|x\|/n > 1} has infinite measure for every n.
- **C1** `exr-modes-of-convergence-c1`. μ(X) < ∞, f_n → f and g_n → g in measure ⇒ f_n g_n → fg in measure. *Answer:* f_n g_n − fg = (f_n − f)(g_n − g) + f(g_n − g) + g(f_n − f). Since f is finite everywhere and μ(X) < ∞, continuity from above gives K with μ({\|f\| > K}) < η; then μ({\|f\|\|g_n − g\| > ε}) ≤ η + μ({\|g_n − g\| > ε/K}). The same for g; and {\|(f_n − f)(g_n − g)\| > ε} ⊂ {\|f_n − f\| > √ε} ∪ {\|g_n − g\| > √ε}. Marks where μ(X) < ∞ is used (B2 shows it is needed).
- **C2** `exr-modes-of-convergence-c2`. μ(X) < ∞. Show f_n → f in measure iff ∫ min(1, \|f_n − f\|) dμ → 0. *Answer:* (⇐) for 0 < ε < 1, Markov applied to min(1, \|f_n − f\|) gives μ({\|f_n − f\| > ε}) ≤ ε^{−1} ∫ min(1, \|f_n − f\|). (⇒) ∫ min(1, \|f_n − f\|) ≤ ε μ(X) + μ({\|f_n − f\| > ε}). Remark in the solution: d(f, g) = ∫ min(1, \|f − g\|) is a metric on classes, so convergence in measure is metric convergence on a finite measure space (a.e. convergence is not; not proved here).

## §2 Four Test Sequences and the Implication Diagram — `sec-four-test-sequences`

**Opening.** Each non-implication between the modes is witnessed by one of four sequences, each failing in its own way: mass that piles up on a shrinking set (spike), mass that moves off to infinity (drift), mass that thins out over a growing set (spread), and mass that sweeps the same set again and again (typewriter). The section builds the table above (first four rows) and the diagram.

**Examples** (each with a three-line verification in the text):
- `exm-spike`: s_n = n · 1_{(0, 1/n)} on [0, 1]. → 0 everywhere; uniformly on [δ, 1] for n > 1/δ, so almost uniformly; in measure; ∫ s_n = 1, so not in L¹.
- `exm-drift`: d_n = 1_{[n, n+1]} on ℝ. → 0 everywhere; m({d_n > 1/2}) = 1, so not in measure, not in L¹, not almost uniformly (contrapositive of `prp-almost-uniform-implies-ae-and-measure`).
- `exm-spread`: w_n = n^{−1/p} · 1_{[0, n]} on ℝ, 1 ≤ p < ∞ fixed. → 0 uniformly; ‖w_n‖_p = 1.
- `exm-typewriter`: for n = 2ᵏ + j with 0 ≤ j < 2ᵏ, t_n = 1_{[j2^{−k}, (j+1)2^{−k}]} on [0, 1]. ‖t_n‖_pᵖ = 2^{−k} → 0, so Lᵖ (p < ∞) and in measure. For each x ∈ [0, 1] and each k some j has x ∈ I_{k,j} and some j′ has x ∉ I_{k,j′}, so t_n(x) takes both values 0 and 1 for infinitely many n: no point of convergence. But t_{2ᵏ} = 1_{[0, 2^{−k}]} → 0 at every x ≠ 0.

The last observation motivates:

- `lem-borel-cantelli` (the first Borel–Cantelli lemma). E_k ∈ 𝓜, Σ μ(E_k) < ∞ ⇒ μ(⋂_m ⋃_{k ≥ m} E_k) = 0, i.e. almost every x lies in only finitely many E_k. *Proof:* μ(⋂_m ⋃_{k≥m} E_k) ≤ Σ_{k ≥ m} μ(E_k) → 0. (Coordinator: if MT.3's re-blueprint already proves it, replace by a recall; PR.1 cites it from whichever chapter owns it.)
- `thm-riesz-subsequence` (Riesz's theorem). **Key proof 1.** Any measure space. f_n → f in measure ⇒ some subsequence f_{n_k} → f almost uniformly, hence a.e. *Proof:* choose n_1 < n_2 < … with μ(E_k) < 2^{−k}, where E_k = {\|f_{n_k} − f\| > 2^{−k}}; F_m = ⋃_{k ≥ m} E_k has μ(F_m) < 2^{1−m}; on X \ F_m, \|f_{n_k} − f\| ≤ 2^{−k} for all k ≥ m: uniform convergence. A.e. from `prp-almost-uniform-implies-ae-and-measure` (or `lem-borel-cantelli` directly; the text notes both routes).
  **Scratch Work** (first appearance of the move *fast subsequence*): we cannot control all n, but we may pick n_k so late that the bad sets shrink summably; the number 2^{−k} is used twice (for ε and for the measure) only for economy; any ε_k → 0 and Σ δ_k < ∞ would do. A margin sketch shows the bad sets E_k for the typewriter with n_k = 2^{k+1}.
- `cor-lp-subsequence-recovered`. Recovers MT.7's "Lᵖ-convergent ⇒ a.e.-convergent subsequence" in one line: `prp-lp-implies-in-measure` + `thm-riesz-subsequence`. (Remark only; MT.7's own proof stands.)
- `thm-cauchy-in-measure-complete`. (f_n) Cauchy in measure ⇒ there is a measurable f with f_n → f in measure, unique a.e. *Proof:* as in Riesz, pick n_k with μ({\|f_{n_{k+1}} − f_{n_k}\| > 2^{−k}}) < 2^{−k}; off F_m the series Σ (f_{n_{k+1}} − f_{n_k}) converges uniformly, defining f (set f = 0 on the null set ⋂ F_m); then f_{n_k} → f almost uniformly hence in measure, and {\|f_n − f\| > ε} ⊂ {\|f_n − f_{n_k}\| > ε/2} ∪ {\|f_{n_k} − f\| > ε/2}.
- `prp-limits-agree`. If f_n → f in one of the five modes and f_n → g in another (or the same), then f = g a.e. *Proof:* each mode gives a subsequence converging a.e. (L^∞ and almost uniform directly; Lᵖ and in measure by `prp-lp-implies-in-measure` and `thm-riesz-subsequence`); pass to a common sub-subsequence; a.e. limits are a.e. unique.
- `lem-subsubsequence` (subsequence principle for real sequences). a_n → a iff every subsequence of (a_n) has a further subsequence converging to a. *Proof:* (⇐) if not, some ε and a subsequence stay ε away from a; no further subsequence converges to a. (Coordinator: if AN1's sequences chapter already has this, recall it instead.)
- `cor-subsequence-criterion-in-measure`. μ(X) < ∞: f_n → f in measure iff every subsequence has a further subsequence converging to f a.e. *Proof:* (⇒) Riesz on the subsequence. (⇐) fix ε, put a_n = μ({\|f_n − f\| > ε}); every subsequence of (a_n) has a further subsequence along which f_n → f a.e., hence in measure (`cor-ae-implies-in-measure-finite`), so a_n → 0 along it; apply `lem-subsubsequence`.
- `cor-dct-in-measure` (dominated convergence in measure). Any measure space. \|f_n\| ≤ g ∈ L¹(μ), f_n → f in measure ⇒ f ∈ L¹ and ‖f_n − f‖₁ → 0. *Proof:* a_n = ‖f_n − f‖₁; any subsequence has (Riesz) a further subsequence converging a.e., along which \|f\| ≤ g a.e. and a_n → 0 by DCT (MT.2); `lem-subsubsequence`.

**The implication diagram** (`fig-modes-diagram`, a TikZ figure; text version as a table). Arrows valid on every measure space: L^∞ ⇒ almost uniform ⇒ a.e.; almost uniform ⇒ in measure; Lᵖ ⇒ in measure; in measure ⇒ (subsequence) almost uniform. Arrows needing μ(X) < ∞: a.e. ⇒ almost uniform (Egorov) ⇒ in measure; L^q ⇒ Lᵖ for p < q (MT.7). Arrows needing domination or UI: in measure ⇒ L¹ (§3). Each missing arrow is labelled with its witness: a.e. ⇏ in measure (drift); in measure ⇏ a.e. and L¹ ⇏ a.e. (typewriter); a.e. ⇏ L¹, in measure ⇏ L¹ (spike; spread on ℝ); uniform ⇏ Lᵖ on ℝ (spread).

**Quick check**: "Which of the four test sequences converges in measure but not a.e.? Which converges a.e. but not in measure?" Answer: typewriter; drift.

**Exercises.**
- **A1** `exr-four-test-sequences-a1` (comprehension, key proof 1, §11.2). (a) Where does the proof of `thm-riesz-subsequence` use μ(X) < ∞? (b) Why must n_1 < n_2 < … be chosen increasing? (c) Run the proof on the typewriter: give n_k satisfying μ(E_k) < 2^{−k} and say where the subsequence converges. *Answers:* (a) nowhere; (b) otherwise (f_{n_k}) is not a subsequence, and the conclusion would be about a rearranged sequence; (c) n_k = 2^{k+1}: t_{n_k} = 1_{[0, 2^{−k−1}]}, μ(E_k) = 2^{−k−1} < 2^{−k}; converges to 0 on (0, 1].
- **A2** `exr-four-test-sequences-a2`. Which arrows of the diagram need μ(X) < ∞, and which test sequence shows it? *Answer:* a.e. ⇒ in measure and a.e. ⇒ almost uniform (drift); L^q ⇒ Lᵖ for p < q (the spread v_n = n^{−1/p} 1_{[0,n]} on ℝ: ‖v_n‖_q = n^{1/q − 1/p} → 0 while ‖v_n‖_p = 1).
- **B1** `exr-four-test-sequences-b1` (**proof ordering**, §11.4; the one ordering item of the chapter). Seven numbered lines of the Riesz proof, shuffled, plus one distractor: "(viii) For each x choose n_k(x) with \|f_{n_k(x)}(x) − f(x)\| < 2^{−k}." *Answer:* an admissible order (choose n_k; define E_k; bound μ(E_k); define F_m; bound μ(F_m); uniform convergence off F_m; conclude a.e.); the bounds on μ(E_k) and the definition of F_m may be swapped; (viii) fails because the subsequence must not depend on x, and in measure gives no pointwise information to choose from.
- **B2** `exr-four-test-sequences-b2`. Show f_n → f in measure ⇒ \|f_n\| → \|f\| in measure. Then on ℝ let f_n(x) = x + 1/n: show f_n → x uniformly but f_n² does not converge to x² in measure. *Answer:* \|\|f_n\| − \|f\|\| ≤ \|f_n − f\|. f_n² − x² = 2x/n + 1/n², and {\|2x/n + 1/n²\| > 1} has infinite measure.
- **B3** `exr-four-test-sequences-b3`. μ(X) < ∞, f_n → f in measure, φ: ℂ → ℂ continuous ⇒ φ ∘ f_n → φ ∘ f in measure. *Answer:* by `cor-subsequence-criterion-in-measure` (⇒) every subsequence has a further subsequence with f_n → f a.e., hence φ ∘ f_n → φ ∘ f a.e. along it; then (⇐) of the same corollary.
- **C1** `exr-four-test-sequences-c1`. Construct f_n on [0, 1] with f_n → 0 in measure and lim sup f_n(x) = ∞ for every x. *Answer:* scaled typewriter f_n = k · t_n for 2ᵏ ≤ n < 2^{k+1}: m({f_n > ε}) ≤ 2^{−k}; each x lies in some I_{k,j} for every k, so f_n(x) = k for some n in the k-th block.
- **C2** `exr-four-test-sequences-c2` (**exit check**). Draw the implication diagram for the five modes on (i) a general measure space and (ii) a finite one; give a witness for every missing arrow and a proof or citation for every arrow present. *Answer:* `fig-modes-diagram`. **Marking scheme** (10): 3 for the correct arrow set in (i), 2 for the additional arrows in (ii), 4 for one valid witness per missing arrow (drift, spike, spread, typewriter, each checked), 1 for citing Riesz as "subsequence only". Fatal: an arrow in measure ⇒ a.e. without "subsequence"; drift used on a finite measure space.

## §3 Uniform Integrability and the Vitali Convergence Theorem — `sec-uniform-integrability`

**Opening.** The hook again, with h_n: no dominator, yet convergence. Then the spike: what goes wrong is that for every M the part of s_n above height M carries all the mass, ∫_{\{s_n > M\}} s_n = 1 for n > M. Domination forbids this, but so does much less.

**Definitions.**
- `def-uniformly-integrable` (**failure-motivated**, from the spike). A family 𝓕 ⊂ L¹(μ) is uniformly integrable (UI) if for every ε > 0 there is M with sup_{f ∈ 𝓕} ∫_{\{|f| > M\}} \|f\| dμ < ε.
- `def-tight` (**failure-motivated**, from the drift and the spread: both UI, both → 0 pointwise, ∫ stays 1). 𝓕 is tight if for every ε > 0 there is E ∈ 𝓜 with μ(E) < ∞ and sup_{f ∈ 𝓕} ∫_{X \ E} \|f\| < ε. Every family is tight when μ(X) < ∞.
- `def-uniformly-absolutely-continuous`: for every ε > 0 there is δ > 0 with sup_{f ∈ 𝓕} ∫_A \|f\| < ε whenever μ(A) < δ.

**Statements.**
- `exm-ui-families`: (a) a single f ∈ L¹ (and any finite family) is UI: ∫_{\{|f| > M\}} \|f\| → 0 by DCT (MT.2); (b) a dominated family \|f\| ≤ g ∈ L¹ is UI: ∫_{\{|f| > M\}} \|f\| ≤ ∫_{\{g > M\}} g; (c) the spike is not UI; (d) drift and spread are UI (M = 1 gives 0) and not tight.
- `prp-ui-equivalent`. (i) On any measure space: if 𝓕 is bounded in L¹ and uniformly absolutely continuous, then 𝓕 is UI. (ii) If 𝓕 is UI, then 𝓕 is uniformly absolutely continuous; if moreover μ(X) < ∞ (or 𝓕 is tight), 𝓕 is bounded in L¹. *Proof:* (i) Markov: μ({\|f\| > M}) ≤ C/M < δ for M > C/δ. (ii) ∫_A \|f\| ≤ M μ(A) + ∫_{\{|f| > M\}} \|f\|; for boundedness, ∫ \|f\| ≤ ∫_{X\E} \|f\| + M μ(E) + ∫_{\{|f|>M\}} \|f\| with E = X when μ(X) < ∞. Remark: without finiteness or tightness, {1_{[0,n]}} is UI and unbounded in L¹(ℝ).
- `prp-lp-bounded-ui`. Any measure space, 1 < p < ∞, sup_{f ∈ 𝓕} ‖f‖_p ≤ C ⇒ 𝓕 UI, with ∫_{\{|f| > M\}} \|f\| ≤ Cᵖ M^{1−p}. *Proof:* on {\|f\| > M}, \|f\| = \|f\|ᵖ \|f\|^{1−p} ≤ \|f\|ᵖ M^{1−p}.
- `prp-de-la-vallee-poussin-sufficient` (de la Vallée-Poussin criterion, sufficiency). Any measure space. If Φ: [0, ∞) → [0, ∞) satisfies Φ(t)/t → ∞ as t → ∞ and K = sup_{f ∈ 𝓕} ∫ Φ(\|f\|) < ∞, then 𝓕 is UI. *Proof:* given ε choose M with Φ(t) ≥ (K/ε) t for t > M (K > 0 wlog); then ∫_{\{|f|>M\}} \|f\| ≤ (ε/K) ∫ Φ(\|f\|) ≤ ε. Examples: Φ(t) = tᵖ (recovers the previous proposition without the constant), Φ(t) = t log(1 + t). The converse (for every UI family such Φ exists, even convex) is exercise C1; nothing depends on it.
- `thm-vitali-convergence-finite` (Vitali convergence theorem, finite measure). **Key proof 2.** μ(X) < ∞; f_n ∈ L¹(μ); (f_n) UI; f_n → f in measure (in particular, a.e., by `cor-ae-implies-in-measure-finite`). Then f ∈ L¹(μ) and ‖f_n − f‖₁ → 0. *Proof:* (1) L¹-bound C from `prp-ui-equivalent`(ii). (2) Riesz gives f_{n_k} → f a.e.; Fatou gives ∫ \|f\| ≤ C. (3) The family {f_n} ∪ {f} is UI (`exm-ui-families` (a) and a union), hence uniformly absolutely continuous; take δ for ε. (4) Let A_n = {\|f_n − f\| > ε}; for n ≥ N, μ(A_n) < δ. (5) ∫ \|f_n − f\| ≤ ∫_{A_n} (\|f_n\| + \|f\|) + ε μ(X) < 2ε + ε μ(X).
  **Scratch Work** (first appearance of the move *split X into a small set and a set where f_n is close to f*): before the proof, a three-region picture: where \|f_n − f\| is small (cost ≤ ε μ(X): finiteness pays here), where it is large (small measure: uniform absolute continuity pays here), and the reminder that both bounds must be uniform in n, which is exactly what "uniformly" in UI buys. The text says which of the five lines fails for the spike (line 5: ∫_{A_n} \|s_n\| = 1).
- `thm-vitali-convergence-general` (Vitali convergence theorem). Any measure space; f_n ∈ L¹(μ); (f_n) UI and tight; f_n → f in measure. Then f ∈ L¹ and ‖f_n − f‖₁ → 0. *Proof:* L¹-bound by `prp-ui-equivalent`(ii) (tight case); f ∈ L¹ by Riesz + Fatou; choose E with μ(E) < ∞ and ∫_{X\E} \|f_n\| < ε for all n and ∫_{X\E} \|f\| < ε (tightness of {f_n} ∪ {f}: take the union of the two sets E); apply `thm-vitali-convergence-finite` to the restrictions to E (μ restricted to E is a finite measure; UI and convergence in measure restrict).
- `thm-vitali-convergence-converse`. Any measure space. f_n, f ∈ L¹(μ), ‖f_n − f‖₁ → 0 ⇒ (f_n) is UI and tight, and f_n → f in measure. *Proof:* in measure by `prp-lp-implies-in-measure`. L¹-bounded. For n ≥ N with ‖f_n − f‖₁ < ε: ∫_A \|f_n\| ≤ ∫_A \|f\| + ε, ∫_{X\E} \|f_n\| ≤ ∫_{X\E} \|f\| + ε; the finitely many f_1, …, f_N and f are each absolutely continuous and tight (for f ∈ L¹: E = {\|f\| > 1/j} has finite measure by Markov, and ∫_{\{|f| ≤ 1/j\}} \|f\| → 0 by DCT); then `prp-ui-equivalent`(i).
- `cor-vitali-convergence-iff`. f_n, f ∈ L¹(μ): ‖f_n − f‖₁ → 0 ⇔ f_n → f in measure and (f_n) is UI and tight. On μ(X) < ∞ drop "tight".
- `cor-lp-bounded-vitali` (**pays the hook**). μ(X) < ∞, 1 < p < ∞, sup_n ‖f_n‖_p < ∞, f_n → f a.e. or in measure ⇒ f ∈ Lᵖ (Fatou along an a.e. subsequence) and ‖f_n − f‖₁ → 0. For the hook: p = 2, so ∫ \|h_n − 0\| → 0 and, in general, ∫ \|f_n − f\| → 0. Closing sentence of the hook: the dominator was never the point; the uniform control of the tall parts was.

**Quick check**: "Is {n · 1_{[0, 1/n²]}} UI on [0, 1]?" Answer: yes: for n > M the tall part has integral 1/n < 1/M, and for n ≤ M it is empty.

**Exercises.**
- **A1** `exr-uniform-integrability-a1` (comprehension, key proof 2). (a) In which lines of the proof of `thm-vitali-convergence-finite` is μ(X) < ∞ used? (b) Run the proof on the drift restricted to [0, 1]: what happens? (c) Which line fails for the spike, and how? (d) The proof's idea in two sentences. *Answers:* (a) line 1 (L¹ bound via `prp-ui-equivalent`) and line 5 (the term ε μ(X)); (b) on [0, 1] the drift is eventually 0, the proof runs trivially; on ℝ line 5 has ε · ∞; (c) line 3: the spike is not uniformly absolutely continuous (∫_{(0,1/n)} s_n = 1 with measure 1/n), so line 5's first term stays near 1; (d) where f_n is close to f the error is at most ε μ(X); the set where it is far has small measure, and uniform integrability makes the integrals over small sets uniformly small.
- **A2** `exr-uniform-integrability-a2`. UI or not on [0, 1]: (i) sin(nx); (ii) n · 1_{[0, 1/n²]}; (iii) n · 1_{[0, 1/n]}; (iv) √n · 1_{[0, 1/n]}. *Answers:* (i) yes (dominated by 1); (ii) yes (Quick check); (iii) no (spike); (iv) yes (bounded in L², `prp-lp-bounded-ui`, or ∫_{tall part} = n^{−1/2} for √n > M).
- **B1** `exr-uniform-integrability-b1`. Show: if 𝓕 is UI and each g ∈ 𝓖 satisfies \|g\| ≤ \|f\| for some f ∈ 𝓕, then 𝓖 is UI; a finite union of UI families is UI. *Answer:* ∫_{\{|g| > M\}} \|g\| ≤ ∫_{\{|f| > M\}} \|f\|; take the largest M.
- **B2** `exr-uniform-integrability-b2`. μ(X) < ∞, 1 < p < ∞, sup ‖f_n‖_p < ∞, f_n → f in measure. Show ‖f_n − f‖_q → 0 for 1 ≤ q < p. *Answer:* f ∈ Lᵖ (Fatou along an a.e. subsequence). \|f_n − f\|^q ≤ 2^{q−1}(\|f_n\|^q + \|f\|^q), and (\|f_n\|^q) is bounded in L^{p/q} with p/q > 1, so UI; by B1 (\|f_n − f\|^q) is UI; \|f_n − f\|^q → 0 in measure; Vitali.
- **B3** `exr-uniform-integrability-b3` (**find the error, true conclusion**, §11.3). Claim: if f_n → f in measure on [0, 1] and \|f_n\| ≤ 1, then ∫ f_n → ∫ f. "Proof: By Riesz's theorem, f_n → f a.e. Since \|f_n\| ≤ 1, dominated convergence gives ∫ f_n → ∫ f." *Answer:* the conclusion is true (`cor-dct-in-measure`). The first line fails: Riesz gives a.e. convergence of a *subsequence* only; counterexample to the step: the typewriter converges in measure and at no point. Repair: `lem-subsubsequence`.
- **B4** `exr-uniform-integrability-b4` (find the error, false conclusion). Claim: on ℝ, if (f_n) is UI and f_n → 0 a.e., then ∫ f_n → 0. "Proof: by `thm-vitali-convergence-finite`." *Answer:* the conclusion is false (drift, spread: ∫ = 1); the cited theorem needs μ(X) < ∞; the general theorem needs tightness and in-measure convergence, and the drift has neither.
- **C1** `exr-uniform-integrability-c1` (converse of de la Vallée-Poussin). If 𝓕 is UI (any measure space), there is a convex increasing Φ with Φ(t)/t → ∞ and sup_{f} ∫ Φ(\|f\|) ≤ 1. *Answer:* choose M_1 < M_2 < … with M_k ≥ 1 and sup_f ∫_{\{|f| > M_k\}} \|f\| ≤ 2^{−k}; put Φ(t) = Σ_k (t − M_k)⁺ (finite: only finitely many terms are nonzero at each t; each term convex, increasing). ∫ (\|f\| − M_k)⁺ ≤ ∫_{\{|f| > M_k\}} \|f\| ≤ 2^{−k}, so ∫ Φ(\|f\|) ≤ 1 (MCT). For t ≥ 2M_k, Φ(t) ≥ Σ_{j ≤ k} (t − M_j) ≥ k t/2, so Φ(t)/t → ∞.

Fatal: DCT applied without a dominator; Fatou applied to the full sequence when only convergence in measure is given. Second route (via `prp-ui-equivalent` and truncation at M) maps step by step.

## §4 Choosing a Limit Theorem — `sec-choosing-a-limit-theorem`

**Opening.** The reader now has six ways to move a limit through an integral. This section adds the one that uses only the integrals themselves (Scheffé), states the Lᵖ form of Vitali, and assembles the checklist that the book uses from here on (final form of the limit-exchange checklist begun in MT.2).

**Statements.**
- `thm-scheffe` (Scheffé's lemma). Any measure space. f_n, f ∈ L¹(μ), f_n → f a.e., ∫ \|f_n\| → ∫ \|f\|. Then ‖f_n − f‖₁ → 0. *Proof:* g_n = \|f_n\| + \|f\| − \|f_n − f\| ≥ 0 (triangle inequality), g_n → 2\|f\| a.e.; Fatou: 2∫\|f\| ≤ lim inf ∫ g_n = 2∫\|f\| − lim sup ‖f_n − f‖₁; subtract the finite number 2∫\|f\|. Also holds with "in measure" in place of "a.e." (`lem-subsubsequence`, remark).
  **Scratch Work** (first appearance of the move *Fatou on the slack*: apply Fatou not to the sequence but to a nonnegative quantity that measures how far a known inequality is from equality). This is the move faded in B2 and C2.
- `thm-vitali-convergence-lp`. μ(X) < ∞, 1 ≤ p < ∞, f_n ∈ Lᵖ. Then f_n → f in Lᵖ iff f_n → f in measure and (\|f_n\|ᵖ) is UI. *Proof:* (⇐) f ∈ Lᵖ by Fatou along a subsequence; \|f_n − f\|ᵖ ≤ 2^{p−1}(\|f_n\|ᵖ + \|f\|ᵖ) is UI (B1 of §3 is not cited: the one-line domination argument is repeated); `thm-vitali-convergence-finite` applied to \|f_n − f\|ᵖ → 0 in measure. (⇒) `prp-lp-implies-in-measure`; \|f_n\|ᵖ ≤ 2^{p−1}(\|f_n − f\|ᵖ + \|f\|ᵖ), and (\|f_n − f\|ᵖ) converges in L¹ hence is UI by `thm-vitali-convergence-converse`.
- `alg-limit-exchange-checklist` (`.algorithm` block). To show ∫ f_n → ∫ f (or ‖f_n − f‖ → 0):
  1. f_n ≥ 0 increasing → MCT.
  2. one integrable g with \|f_n\| ≤ g → DCT (a.e.) or `cor-dct-in-measure`.
  3. you know ∫ \|f_n\| → ∫ \|f\| → `thm-scheffe`.
  4. μ(X) < ∞ and sup ‖f_n‖_p < ∞ for some p > 1 → `cor-lp-bounded-vitali`.
  5. otherwise test UI directly → `thm-vitali-convergence-finite`; on an infinite measure space test tightness too → `thm-vitali-convergence-general`.
  6. you only need an inequality → Fatou.
  7. none applies → look for mass escaping: compute ∫_{\{|f_n|>M\}} \|f_n\| and ∫_{X\E} \|f_n\|; one of the test sequences is usually hiding (then the limit typically fails).

**Worked examples.**
- `exm-checklist-dct`: lim ∫_0^∞ (1 + x/n)^{−n} dx = 1. For n ≥ 2, (1 + x/n)^{−n} ≤ (1 + x/2)^{−2} (since (1 + x/n)ⁿ increases in n; the monotonicity is recalled from AN1/AN2 as a fact about e) and ∫_0^∞ (1 + x/2)^{−2} = 2; DCT. Check: the integral equals n/(n−1) exactly for n ≥ 2.
- `exm-peak-at-one`: f_n(x) = n x^{n−1} on [0, 1]: → 0 a.e., ∫ f_n = 1. Checklist: no dominator (sup_n f_n(x) ≥ c/(1 − x) near 1), not UI (∫_{\{f_n > M\}} f_n = 1 − (M/n)^{n/(n−1)} → 1 as n → ∞, for each M). Conclusion: no theorem applies and the exchange fails. Added to the table.
- `exm-checklist-vitali`: f_n(x) = √n xⁿ on [0, 1]: ∫ f_n² = n/(2n + 1) ≤ 1/2, f_n → 0 a.e.; step 4 gives ∫ f_n → 0 (check: ∫ f_n = √n/(n + 1)). A dominator does exist here (the text asks the reader to find one in a Quick check), showing that step 4 is often quicker, not only stronger.
- `exm-checklist-scheffe`: Gaussian densities φ_σ(x) = (2πσ²)^{−1/2} e^{−x²/(2σ²)} with σ_n → 1: φ_{σ_n} → φ_1 pointwise, all integrals 1; Scheffé gives L¹ convergence and hence sup_A \|∫_A φ_{σ_n} − ∫_A φ_1\| → 0 (pointer to PR.1: convergence of probability densities).
- `exm-checklist-tightness`: the spread w_n = n^{−1} 1_{[0,n]} on ℝ: steps 1–4 fail, UI holds, tightness fails; ∫ w_n = 1 ↛ 0.

**Quick check**: "Find a dominator for √n xⁿ on [0, 1]." Answer: maximize √t · x^t over t > 0 (critical point t = 1/(2 log(1/x))): √n xⁿ ≤ (2e log(1/x))^{−1/2} on (0, 1), integrable since ∫_0^1 (log(1/x))^{−1/2} dx = Γ(1/2) = √π. The point of the example stands: step 4 needed none of this.

**Exercises.**
- **A1** `exr-choosing-a-limit-theorem-a1`. Name the tool and the limit: (a) lim ∫_0^1 eˣ xⁿ (n + 1) dx; (b) lim ∫_0^∞ e^{−x} cos(x/n) dx; (c) lim ∫_0^1 n^{1/3} xⁿ dx. *Answers:* (a) e: mass of (n+1)xⁿ concentrates at 1 (not UI; the checklist fails; compute by the substitution/integration by parts: (n+1)∫ eˣ xⁿ = e − ∫ eˣ x^{n+1} → e); (b) 1 by DCT with dominator e^{−x}; (c) 0: ∫ (n^{1/3} xⁿ)² = n^{2/3}/(2n + 1) bounded, step 4 (direct: n^{1/3}/(n+1) → 0).
- **A2** `exr-choosing-a-limit-theorem-a2` (comprehension of Scheffé). (a) Why is g_n ≥ 0? (b) Where is ∫ \|f\| < ∞ used? (c) Which step fails for the spike, and why does the spike not contradict Scheffé? *Answers:* (a) triangle inequality; (b) to subtract 2∫\|f\| from both sides; (c) ∫ \|s_n\| = 1 ↛ 0 = ∫ 0: the hypothesis fails, not a step.
- **B1** `exr-choosing-a-limit-theorem-b1`. f_n ≥ 0, ∫ f_n = 1, f_n → f a.e., ∫ f = 1. Show sup_{A ∈ 𝓜} \|∫_A f_n − ∫_A f\| → 0. *Answer:* bounded by ‖f_n − f‖₁, Scheffé.
- **B2** `exr-choosing-a-limit-theorem-b2` (**faded, step 2 of 3**: last step blank). Generalized DCT: \|f_n\| ≤ g_n, g_n, g ∈ L¹, g_n → g and f_n → f a.e., ∫ g_n → ∫ g. Show ‖f_n − f‖₁ → 0. Given: "Put G_n = g_n + g − \|f_n − f\| ≥ 0; G_n → 2g a.e. By Fatou, … ." *Name the move, then complete it.* *Answer:* Fatou on the slack: 2∫ g ≤ lim inf ∫ G_n = 2∫ g − lim sup ‖f_n − f‖₁.
- **B3** `exr-choosing-a-limit-theorem-b3`. μ(X) < ∞, 1 ≤ p < ∞, f_n → f in measure, \|f_n\| ≤ g ∈ Lᵖ ⇒ f_n → f in Lᵖ. *Answer:* \|f_n\|ᵖ ≤ gᵖ ∈ L¹, UI; `thm-vitali-convergence-lp`. (Remark: true on any measure space via `cor-dct-in-measure` applied to \|f_n − f\|ᵖ ≤ 2ᵖ gᵖ.)
- **C1** `exr-choosing-a-limit-theorem-c1`. Show that tightness cannot be dropped in `thm-vitali-convergence-general` even if sup \|f_n\| ≤ 1, ∫ \|f_n\| = 1 and f_n → 0 uniformly. *Answer:* the spread n^{−1} 1_{[0,n]}.
- **C2** `exr-choosing-a-limit-theorem-c2` (**faded, step 3 of 3**: last half blank). 1 ≤ p < ∞, f_n, f ∈ Lᵖ, f_n → f a.e., ‖f_n‖_p → ‖f‖_p ⇒ ‖f_n − f‖_p → 0. Given only: "Consider 2^{p−1}(\|f_n\|ᵖ + \|f\|ᵖ) − \|f_n − f\|ᵖ." *Name the move, then complete it.* *Answer:* it is ≥ 0 by convexity of t ↦ tᵖ, → 2ᵖ\|f\|ᵖ a.e.; Fatou: 2ᵖ‖f‖_pᵖ ≤ 2ᵖ‖f‖_pᵖ − lim sup ‖f_n − f‖_pᵖ.

## §5 The Distribution Function and the Layer-Cake Formula — `sec-layer-cake`

**Opening (failure-motivated).** Markov's inequality, used in every section so far, only looks at one level set at a time. The Lᵖ norm forgets *where* f is large but remembers *how much of X* lies at each height: x and 1 − x on [0, 1] are different functions with the same norms in every Lᵖ. The object that records exactly this is the distribution function.

**Definitions and statements.**
- `def-distribution-function`. d_f(t) = μ({x : \|f(x)\| > t}) for t ≥ 0 (values in [0, ∞]). f and g are equimeasurable if d_f = d_g. (NOTATION addition: d_f; Lebesgue measure stays m.)
- `prp-distribution-function-properties`. (a) d_f is decreasing and right-continuous; (b) \|f\| ≤ \|g\| a.e. ⇒ d_f ≤ d_g; (c) d_{f+g}(s + t) ≤ d_f(s) + d_g(t); (d) d_{cf}(t) = d_f(t/\|c\|) for c ≠ 0; (e) if \|f_n\| ↑ \|f\| a.e. then d_{f_n} ↑ d_f; (f) Markov: d_f(t) ≤ t^{−p}‖f‖_pᵖ. *Proof:* (a) {\|f\| > t} = ⋃_k {\|f\| > t_k} for t_k ↓ t, continuity from below (no finiteness needed); (c) inclusion of level sets; (e) continuity from below.
- `thm-layer-cake` (layer-cake formula). **Key proof 3.** Any measure space, f measurable, 0 < p < ∞: ∫ \|f\|ᵖ dμ = p ∫_0^∞ t^{p−1} d_f(t) dt (both sides possibly ∞). More generally, if φ: [0, ∞) → [0, ∞) is C¹ with φ(0) = 0 and φ′ ≥ 0, and {f ≠ 0} is σ-finite, then ∫ φ(\|f\|) dμ = ∫_0^∞ φ′(t) d_f(t) dt. *Proof:* (σ-finite case) φ(\|f(x)\|) = ∫_0^{|f(x)|} φ′(t) dt = ∫_0^∞ φ′(t) 1_{\{(x,t) : |f(x)| > t\}} dt by the fundamental theorem of calculus; the set {(x, t) : \|f(x)\| > t} is measurable for 𝓜 ⊗ 𝓑 (it is {F > 0} for F(x, t) = \|f(x)\| − t, a difference of product-measurable functions); Tonelli on {f ≠ 0} × (0, ∞) (MT.4) swaps the integrals. (Power case without σ-finiteness) if d_f(t_0) = ∞ for some t_0 > 0, then d_f = ∞ on [0, t_0) by monotonicity, so the right side is ∞, and ∫ \|f\|ᵖ ≥ t_0ᵖ d_f(t_0) = ∞; otherwise {f ≠ 0} = ⋃_k {\|f\| > 1/k} is σ-finite and the first case applies with φ(t) = tᵖ. Remark: replacing > by ≥ in d_f changes it at most at countably many t (a monotone function has countably many jumps), so the integral is unchanged.
  **Scratch Work** (first appearance of the move *write a function as the integral of its level sets and swap*): the picture of the region under the graph of \|f\| sliced horizontally (layers of height dt and area d_f(t)) versus vertically; the formula is the statement that both slicings give the same area; the factor p t^{p−1} comes from slicing at heights tᵖ. Shorter than in §1–§3 (fading).
- `cor-lp-by-tails`. f ∈ Lᵖ iff ∫_0^∞ t^{p−1} d_f(t) dt < ∞; for f ≥ 0 on any measure space, ∫ f = ∫_0^∞ d_f(t) dt; for a random variable, E\|X\| = ∫_0^∞ P(\|X\| > t) dt (pointer to PR.1).
- `cor-tall-part-formula`. ∫_{\{|f| > M\}} \|f\| = M d_f(M) + ∫_M^∞ d_f(t) dt. *Proof:* `cor-lp-by-tails` (p = 1) applied to \|f\| 1_{\{|f|>M\}}, whose distribution function is d_f(M) for t < M and d_f(t) for t ≥ M. (Used in §6 for weak-Lᵖ-bounded families.)

**Worked example** `exm-power-on-ball`: f(x) = \|x\|^{−a} on B = B(0, 1) ⊂ ℝᵈ, a > 0. d_f(t) = v_d min(1, t^{−d/a}) (from m(B(0, r)) = v_d rᵈ, MT.5). Layer cake: ∫_B \|f\|ᵖ = p v_d ∫_0^1 t^{p−1} dt + p v_d ∫_1^∞ t^{p−1−d/a} dt, finite iff p < d/a, and then equal to v_d · d/(d − ap). Check against polar coordinates (MT.5): d v_d ∫_0^1 r^{d−1−ap} dr = d v_d/(d − ap). The text says which computation needed polar coordinates and which only dilation.

**Quick check**: "Compute d_f for f(x) = x on [0, 2] and verify ∫ x² dx by the layer cake." Answer: d_f(t) = 2 − t for 0 ≤ t < 2, 0 after; 2∫_0^2 t(2 − t) dt = 8/3.

## §6 Weak Lᵖ — `sec-weak-lp`

**Opening (failure-motivated).** Many arguments use an Lᵖ bound only through Markov: d_f(t) ≤ t^{−p}‖f‖_pᵖ. Some natural functions satisfy that bound without being in Lᵖ: 1/x on (0, 1) has d(t) = min(1, 1/t), so t d(t) ≤ 1, while ∫ 1/x = ∞. The definition keeps Markov's conclusion and drops its hypothesis. Throughout §6, 1 ≤ p < ∞.

**Definitions and statements.**
- `def-weak-lp`. ‖f‖_{p,∞} = sup_{t > 0} t d_f(t)^{1/p}; L^{p,∞}(μ) is the set of (classes of) measurable f with ‖f‖_{p,∞} < ∞; L^{∞,∞} := L^∞. `.warning`: ‖·‖_{p,∞} is not a norm for p = 1 (below), which is why it is written with the subscript p,∞ and called a quasi-norm. (NOTATION addition.)
- `prp-lp-in-weak-lp`. ‖f‖_{p,∞} ≤ ‖f‖_p; so Lᵖ ⊂ L^{p,∞}. *Proof:* Markov.
- `prp-weak-lp-quasinorm`. ‖cf‖_{p,∞} = \|c\| ‖f‖_{p,∞}; ‖f‖_{p,∞} = 0 iff f = 0 a.e.; ‖f + g‖_{p,∞} ≤ 2(‖f‖_{p,∞} + ‖g‖_{p,∞}). *Proof:* d_{f+g}(t) ≤ d_f(t/2) + d_g(t/2) ≤ 2ᵖ t^{−p}(‖f‖ᵖ + ‖g‖ᵖ), and (aᵖ + bᵖ)^{1/p} ≤ a + b for p ≥ 1.
- `exm-weak-l1-triangle-fails`: on (0, 1), u(x) = 1/x and v(x) = 1/(1 − x) have ‖u‖_{1,∞} = ‖v‖_{1,∞} = 1, while s = u + v = 1/(x(1 − x)) ≥ 4 has d_s(t) = 1 for t < 4 and 1 − √(1 − 4/t) for t ≥ 4, so ‖s‖_{1,∞} = sup_t t d_s(t) = 4 (attained at t = 4; t d_s(t) decreases from 4 to 2 on [4, ∞)). Hence 4 > 1 + 1 (not a norm) and 4 = 2(1 + 1) (the constant 2 is attained). Remark, not proved, nothing depends on it: for p > 1 an equivalent norm exists; for p = 1 none does.
- `exm-power-weak-lp`: f(x) = \|x\|^{−d/p} on ℝᵈ: d_f(t) = m(B(0, t^{−p/d})) = v_d t^{−p}, so ‖f‖_{p,∞} = v_d^{1/p}, while ∫ \|f\|ᵖ = ∫ \|x\|^{−d} = p v_d ∫_0^∞ t^{−1} dt = ∞ (layer cake). Second table of the chapter (1/x, \|x\|^{−d/p}, 1/x + 1/(1 − x)).
- `prp-weak-convergence-implies-measure`. ‖f_n − f‖_{p,∞} → 0 ⇒ f_n → f in measure. *Proof:* d_{f_n − f}(ε) ≤ ε^{−p}‖f_n − f‖_{p,∞}ᵖ. Diagram addition: Lᵖ ⇒ L^{p,∞} ⇒ in measure.
- `prp-weak-bounded-ui`. 1 < p < ∞: a family bounded in L^{p,∞}, sup ‖f‖_{p,∞} ≤ C, is UI, with ∫_{\{|f| > M\}} \|f\| ≤ Cᵖ (p/(p−1)) M^{1−p}. *Proof:* `cor-tall-part-formula` with d_f(t) ≤ Cᵖ t^{−p}. Improves `prp-lp-bounded-ui` (weak bound suffices). Fails for p = 1: the spike has ‖s_n‖_{1,∞} = 1.
- `thm-weak-lp-sandwich`. 1 ≤ p_0 < p < p_1 ≤ ∞; f ∈ L^{p_0,∞} ∩ L^{p_1,∞} ⇒ f ∈ Lᵖ, with, for every T > 0,
  ‖f‖_pᵖ ≤ (p/(p − p_0)) A^{p_0} T^{p − p_0} + (p/(p_1 − p)) B^{p_1} T^{p − p_1} (p_1 < ∞),
  ‖f‖_pᵖ ≤ (p/(p − p_0)) A^{p_0} ‖f‖_∞^{p − p_0} (p_1 = ∞),
  where A = ‖f‖_{p_0,∞}, B = ‖f‖_{p_1,∞}. *Proof Idea only* (fading: the reader writes the three lines; the text gives the split): layer cake, split ∫_0^∞ at T, use d_f(t) ≤ A^{p_0} t^{−p_0} below T and d_f(t) ≤ B^{p_1} t^{−p_1} above; for p_1 = ∞ take T = ‖f‖_∞ and note d_f = 0 beyond it. Move named *split the level integral at a height*; MT.9 applies it to Tf with f itself split at height T (Marcinkiewicz), stated as "in Chapter 13" without `@ref`.
- `exm-sandwich-sharp`: f(x) = min(1, 1/x) on (0, ∞): ‖f‖_{1,∞} = 1, ‖f‖_∞ = 1, so the bound gives ‖f‖₂² ≤ 2, and ‖f‖₂² = 1 + ∫_1^∞ x^{−2} dx = 2: equality.

**Closing paragraph (opens the MT.9 hook).** The maximal function Mf of Chapter 13 is never in L¹(ℝᵈ) for f ≠ 0, but satisfies t · m({Mf > t}) ≤ C_d ‖f‖₁: it lies in L^{1,∞}. That is the reason for this section.

**Quick check**: "Is (1 + x)^{−1} · 1_{[0,∞)} in L^{1,∞}(ℝ)? In L¹?" Answer: d(t) = 1/t − 1 for 0 < t < 1, so t d(t) = 1 − t < 1 and ‖·‖_{1,∞} = 1; not in L¹.

**Exercises.**

## Named moves (chapter end, STYLE §2.6)

1. **Integral to level set** (Markov): a bound on ∫ \|f\|ᵖ bounds μ({\|f\| ≥ t}) by t^{−p}∫\|f\|ᵖ.
2. **Fast subsequence**: choose n_k so that the bad sets have summable measure; then Borel–Cantelli or the union bound.
3. **Subsequence principle**: to prove a_n → a, show every subsequence has a further subsequence converging to a; combines with Riesz to turn "in measure" into "a.e." for limit theorems.
4. **Small set / close set split**: where f_n is close to f pay with ε μ(X); where it is far pay with uniform absolute continuity.
5. **Fatou on the slack**: apply Fatou to the nonnegative defect of an inequality (Scheffé, generalized DCT, Lᵖ-Scheffé).
6. **Slice by level sets and swap** (layer cake via Tonelli).
7. **Split the level integral at a height** (weak-Lᵖ sandwich; Marcinkiewicz in Chapter 13).
