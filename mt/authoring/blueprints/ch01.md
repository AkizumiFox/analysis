**Named moves** (in `index.md`):
- **Find a gap.** A closed nowhere dense set F leaves an open gap inside every interval. So any function that vanishes "almost everywhere on the gaps" has infimum 0 on every partition interval. First used in `thm-riemann-l1-incomplete`.
- **Oscillation on a fat set.** If h has oscillation ≥ η at every point of a set E, and every finite cover of E by intervals has total length ≥ c > 0, then U(h,P) − L(h,P) ≥ ηc − (small) for every partition. First used in `prp-fat-cantor-not-integrable`, then in `thm-volterra`.
- **Splice a local model.** Put a scaled copy of one function with a known singularity into every gap of a closed set. Cut it off at a critical point so the pieces join in C¹. First used in `def-volterra-function`.
- **Slice the range.** Group points by the value of f rather than by position: Σ yₖ · length{yₖ ≤ f < yₖ₊₁}. The idea is introduced in §06 and becomes the definition in MT.2.

## index.md

- **Story.** The Riemann integral is a finite procedure: cut the interval into finitely many pieces and sum. Every theorem that exchanges it with a limit needed an extra hypothesis, and each of AN2.10, AN3.4 and AN1.9 ended by noting one.
  - This chapter shows the hypotheses cannot be weakened inside the Riemann theory. Bounded monotone limits escape it (§02). Its natural distance has holes (§03). A bounded derivative can fail to be integrable (§04). It cannot measure an open set (§05).

## 01-the-riemann-integral-reviewed.md — The Riemann integral and its fine print (≈ 3,600 words)

- **Opening frame.** Measure theory exists because of specific failures. This section recalls the Riemann integral in the form the chapter uses, then lists every place an extra hypothesis was needed.
- **Recall, compactly** (no new labels; each item recalled in a clause with its AN1.8 pointer):
  - partitions, U(f,P) and L(f,P), refinement, upper and lower integrals;
  - the criterion "∀ε ∃P: U − L < ε";
  - continuous and monotone functions are integrable, and so is φ∘f;
  - FTC I and II;
  - improper integrals.
  - The oscillation osc_f(x) = lim_{δ→0} (sup − inf of f on (x−δ, x+δ)), and the elementary fact that for a partition interval J containing x in its interior, sup_J f − inf_J f ≥ osc_f(x). Prove the fact in two lines; it is used in §02 and §04.
  - Lebesgue's criterion, *stated* (AN1.8; proved in AN2.10): a bounded f on [a,b] is Riemann integrable iff its set of discontinuities has measure zero. Say that this chapter never uses it as a proof step. Every non-integrability claim below is proved from the Darboux sums, so that the failures stand on the definition alone.
- **Worked example (review):** Thomae's function. Integrable with integral 0, discontinuous exactly on ℚ ∩ [0,1]. Recalled, not reproved. It shows "many discontinuities" is not the issue; their *size* is.
- **The inventory** (a table, the section's centrepiece). Columns: the theorem; the hypothesis it needed; is the hypothesis removable in the Riemann theory?; the witness in this chapter; the MT chapter that removes it.
  1. ∫ lim fₙ = lim ∫ fₙ (AN1.9): uniform convergence. Not removable; witness §02. MT.2 (monotone and dominated convergence).
  2. d/dt ∫ f(x,t) dx = ∫ ∂ₜf (AN2.11): ∂ₜf continuous on a rectangle. Not removable in general. MT.2 (dominated convergence corollary).
  3. Iterated integrals (AN3.4): f continuous, or integrable with all inner integrals existing. MT.4.
  4. FTC II (AN1.8): F′ integrable. Not removable; witness §04. MT.2 and MT.10.
  5. Completeness of a space of integrable functions: none is complete under d₁. Witness §03. MT.7.
  6. Unbounded integrands: improper integrals at finitely many points only. Witness below. MT.2.
  7. Length of a set: only for Jordan-measurable sets. Witness §05. MT.1.
  - Under the table, one sentence: rows 2 and 3 cite AN2.11 and AN3.4 for context; no proof in this chapter depends on them.
- **Unbounded functions.** The Riemann integral needs f bounded (U(f,P) = +∞ otherwise). Improper integrals handle finitely many singular points, one at a time.
  - **`exm-dyadic-singular-function`** (original). Let φ(t) = |t|^{−1/2} for t ≠ 0, with φ(0) = +∞. Define
    f(x) = Σ_{k ≥ 1} Σ_{j odd, 0 < j < 2^k} 4^{−k} φ(x − j/2^k) on [0,1], with values in [0, +∞].
    - f is unbounded on every nondegenerate subinterval, because every subinterval contains a dyadic j/2^k where one term is +∞. So no restriction of f to any [c,d] is Riemann integrable, and no improper integral with finitely many exceptional points applies.
    - Each partial sum f_N (k ≤ N) has finitely many singularities and is improper-integrable. Its integral satisfies ∫₀¹ f_N ≤ Σ_{k≤N} 2^{k−1} · 4^{−k} · 2√2 < √2. Use ∫₀¹ |x − c|^{−1/2} dx = 2(√c + √(1−c)) ≤ 2√2: verify by FTC on each side of c, and maximise √c + √(1−c) at c = 1/2.
    - So ∫ f_N increases to a limit ≤ √2. The integral that f "obviously" has cannot be written down. MT.2 (monotone convergence) gives ∫ f ≤ √2, and also that f < ∞ outside a null set.
    - Sidecar: `verify/mt-ch01/dyadic_singular.py` checks the bound on ∫f_N numerically and the closed form of ∫|x − c|^{−1/2}.
  - **`exm-improper-not-absolute`:** ∫₁^∞ (sin x)/x converges and ∫₁^∞ |sin x|/x diverges (recalled from AN1.8). **Honest remark:** the Lebesgue integral will not include this improper integral either, because Lebesgue integrability is absolute. Conditionally convergent improper integrals remain a limit of integrals (MT.2 exercise). The gauge integral (AN2.14, optional; see) includes them. This keeps the list in §06 honest.
- **Quick check.** Is x ↦ x^{−1/2} (with value 0 at 0) Riemann integrable on [0,1]? Is it improper-integrable on (0,1]? *Answer:* no (unbounded, so U(f,P) = +∞ for every P); yes, with value 2.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** For each inventory row, name the hypothesis and one function that violates it. *Answers:* e.g. row 1: fₙ = 𝟏_{{q₁,…,qₙ}}; row 4: V′ from §04 (forward pointer allowed in an answer).
  - **A2.** Compute osc_f(0) for f = 𝟏_{(0,1]}, for sin(1/x) (f(0) = 0) and for Thomae's function at 1/2. *Answers:* 1; 2; 1/2.
  - **B1.** Prove that a bounded f with osc_f(x) < ε at every x ∈ [a,b] has a partition with U − L < ε(b − a). *Answer sketch:* Heine–Borel on the neighbourhoods where sup − inf < ε, then a Lebesgue number (AN1.4).
  - **B2.** Show that the f of `exm-dyadic-singular-function` restricted to [1/4, 3/4] is still unbounded on every subinterval, and that f(x) < ∞ at x = 1/3. *Answer:* 1/3 has no finite binary expansion. The distance from 1/3 to j/2^k is at least 1/(3·2^k), so the k-th group of terms is ≤ 2^{k−1} · 4^{−k} · √3 · 2^{k/2}, and the series converges.
  - **C1.** Construct a bounded function on [0,1], continuous except on a countable set, that is Riemann integrable but has no antiderivative (no F with F′ = f everywhere). *Answer:* 𝟏_{[1/2,1]}. A derivative has the intermediate value property (AN1.7, Darboux), and this function lacks it. *Hint ladder:* (1) What property do all derivatives share? (2) Find a jump.

## 02-limits-and-the-riemann-integral.md — Bounded limits that escape (≈ 3,900 words)

- **Opening frame.** AN1.9 exchanged ∫ and lim under uniform convergence and asked whether pointwise convergence ever suffices. This section shows that pointwise convergence fails even with every safeguard short of uniformity. The safeguards are monotonicity, a uniform bound and continuous terms. The failure happens because the limit leaves ℛ, not because the integrals misbehave.
- **`thm-monotone-limit-not-riemann`.** There are functions 0 ≤ f₁ ≤ f₂ ≤ ⋯ ≤ 1 on [0,1], each Riemann integrable with ∫fₙ = 0, whose pointwise limit is not Riemann integrable.
  - *Proof.* Enumerate ℚ ∩ [0,1] = {q₁, q₂, …} (F2.3, prose pointer). Let fₙ = 𝟏_{{q₁,…,qₙ}}, which differs from 0 at finitely many points. The limit is 𝟏_{ℚ∩[0,1]}; every partition gives U = 1 and L = 0 (density of ℚ and of the irrationals).
  - *Remark:* the limit is not a pointwise limit of *continuous* functions (C2 below), so one might hope continuity of the fₙ rescues the theorem. It does not. That is the next result.
- **The fat Cantor set** (`def-fat-cantor-set`; AN1.4 built one, and this fixes the parameters used throughout MT.1–MT.2).
  - K₀ = [0,1]. K_n is obtained from K_{n−1}, a disjoint union of 2^{n−1} closed intervals, by removing from the middle of each an open interval of length 4^{−n}. K = ⋂ₙ K_n.
  - Facts:
    - |K_n| (sum of lengths) = 1/2 + 2^{−n−1};
    - each interval of K_n has length (1/2 + 2^{−n−1})/2^n > 4^{−(n+1)}, so the next removal is possible;
    - K is compact and nonempty (AN1.4) and nowhere dense (the intervals of K_n have length → 0);
    - every point of K is a limit of endpoints of removed intervals.
  - Sidecar `verify/mt-ch01/fat_cantor.py` checks the lengths.
- **`lem-fat-cantor-cover`** (key proof). If I₁, …, I_m are intervals with K ⊆ I₁ ∪ ⋯ ∪ I_m, then Σ|I_j| ≥ 1/2.
  - **Scratch Work** (first appearance of the "compactness turns a cover of the limit into a cover of a stage" step):
    - Enlarge each I_j to an open interval of length |I_j| + δ/m.
    - The sets K_n \ (∪ open I_j) are compact and decreasing, with empty intersection, so one is empty (AN1.4). Hence some K_n is covered.
    - The finitely many disjoint intervals of K_n have total length ≤ the total length of a finite cover. This is the finite additivity of length, done honestly via ∫𝟏 of step functions (AN1.8 linearity and monotonicity).
    - Hence Σ|I_j| + δ ≥ |K_n| > 1/2. Let δ → 0.
  - *Proof:* forward, in that order, about 12 lines.
- **`lem-oscillation-fat-set`.** Let h be bounded on [0,1], and suppose osc_h(x) ≥ η for every x ∈ K. Then U(h,P) − L(h,P) ≥ η/2 for every partition P. Hence h ∉ ℛ[0,1].
  - **Scratch Work:**
    - Split the intervals of P into those whose interior meets K ("bad") and the rest.
    - Each bad J has sup_J h − inf_J h ≥ η (the §01 oscillation fact).
    - K is covered by the bad intervals together with the finitely many partition points. Cover each partition point by an interval of length δ/(#P) and apply `lem-fat-cantor-cover`: the total length of the bad intervals is ≥ 1/2 − δ.
  - *Proof:* forward.
- **`prp-fat-cantor-not-integrable`.** 𝟏_K ∉ ℛ[0,1]. More precisely, its upper integral is 1/2 and its lower integral is 0.
  - *Proof Idea:* osc = 1 on K (every neighbourhood of x ∈ K meets a gap), so `lem-oscillation-fat-set` applies.
  - Upper integral ≤ |K_n| (K_n is a union of partition intervals) and ≥ 1/2 (`lem-fat-cantor-cover`).
  - Lower integral 0: every nondegenerate interval meets a gap, so inf = 0 on every partition interval. This is the first, mild appearance of "find a gap"; name it in §03.
- **`prp-continuous-decreasing-to-fat-cantor`** (pays AN1.9 §8). Let gₙ(x) = max(0, 1 − n·dist(x, K)) on [0,1].
  - Then each gₙ is continuous, 1 ≥ g₁ ≥ g₂ ≥ ⋯ ≥ 0, and gₙ → 𝟏_K pointwise, while 𝟏_K ∉ ℛ[0,1].
  - Moreover ∫₀¹ gₙ ↓ 1/2.
  - *Proof:*
    - gₙ ≥ 𝟏_K gives ∫gₙ ≥ upper integral of 𝟏_K = 1/2.
    - gₙ ≤ 𝟏 on the (1/n)-neighbourhood of K. That neighbourhood lies in the (1/n)-neighbourhood of K_m, which has total length ≤ |K_m| + 2^{m+1}/n.
    - Choose m, then n.
- **`exm-fat-cantor-limits`** (worked example). Tabulate ∫gₙ for n = 1, 2, 4, 8, … next to |K_m|, so the reader sees two sequences closing in on 1/2 that the Riemann integral of the limit cannot reach. The numbers come from the sidecar; the text gives the by-hand bounds.
- **`thm-arzela-bounded-convergence`** (statement only). If fₙ ∈ ℛ[a,b], |fₙ| ≤ M, fₙ → f pointwise, **and f ∈ ℛ[a,b]**, then ∫fₙ → ∫f.
  - Say plainly: the proof is given in MT.2 as a corollary of the bounded convergence theorem. The text does not use it.
  - The theorem is the best one can say in the Riemann world, and its last hypothesis is exactly the one `prp-continuous-decreasing-to-fat-cantor` shows cannot be dropped.
  - Contrast with AN1.9's theorem, where uniform convergence *delivers* f ∈ ℛ.
- **Quick check.** fₙ = 𝟏_{[0,1/n]} → 𝟏_{{0}} pointwise. Is the limit integrable, and does ∫fₙ → ∫ lim? *Answer:* yes and yes (0). Pointwise convergence fails only sometimes; the point is that ℛ cannot tell when.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1** (comprehension, key proof `lem-fat-cantor-cover`):
    - (a) where compactness of K_n is used;
    - (b) why the intervals were enlarged to open ones;
    - (c) run the proof for the cover I₁ = [0, 0.4], I₂ = [0.6, 1] and say which K_n it covers.
    - *Answers:* (a) the decreasing compact sets K_n minus the open cover; (b) the complement of an open set in K_n is compact; (c) the first gap is (3/8, 5/8), so I₁ ∪ I₂ covers K₁ = [0, 3/8] ∪ [5/8, 1] and hence K; Σ = 0.8 ≥ 0.75 = |K₁|. The sidecar checks it.
  - **A2.** Compute ∫₀¹ g₁ exactly. *Answer:* for n = 1, g₁(x) = 1 − dist(x, K). It equals 1 except on the gaps. On a gap of length ℓ ≤ 1/4 the deficit is a tent of area ℓ²/4. Total ∫g₁ = 1 − Σₙ 2^{n−1} · 4^{−2n}/4 = 1 − (1/8) · Σ 2^n 16^{−n} = 1 − (1/8) · (1/7) = 55/56. The sidecar checks this.
  - **B1.** Let hₙ be continuous with hₙ ↑ h pointwise on [0,1], and h continuous. Prove ∫hₙ → ∫h. Then say what is missing when h is only assumed Riemann integrable. *Answer:* Dini (AN1.9) gives uniform convergence, hence the integrals converge. Without continuity of h, Dini does not apply; `thm-arzela-bounded-convergence` covers the case, but its proof is in MT.2, so it cannot serve as a proof step here.

## 03-incompleteness.md — The Riemann integrable functions have holes (≈ 4,000 words)

- **Opening frame.** The sup distance on C[0,1] is complete (AN1.9), but it is the wrong ruler for integration. Two functions that differ by 1 on a set of tiny length are far apart in sup and should be close for anything an integral measures: averages, least squares, energy.
- **Failure-motivated definition `def-riemann-distances`.**
  - Hook: 𝟏_{[0,1/n]} and 0 have sup distance 1 for every n, while ∫|difference| = 1/n → 0. The integral sees them merging; the sup metric does not.
  - d₁(f,g) = ∫ₐᵇ |f − g| and d₂(f,g) = (∫ₐᵇ |f − g|²)^{1/2} on ℛ[a,b]. They are well defined by AN1.8 (|·| and squares of integrable functions are integrable).
  - Symmetric, triangle inequality (d₂ needs `lem-riemann-cauchy-schwarz`).
  - **Warning:** d₁(f,g) = 0 does not imply f = g (change f at one point). d₁ is a pseudometric. Identify functions at distance 0. This is the first appearance of "equal except on a small set", and MT.1 will name it "almost everywhere".
- **`lem-riemann-cauchy-schwarz`.** For f, g ∈ ℛ[a,b], |∫fg| ≤ (∫f²)^{1/2}(∫g²)^{1/2}.
  - *Proof Idea:* ∫(|f| − t|g|)² ≥ 0 for every t, which is a quadratic in t with nonpositive discriminant.
  - Consequences: the triangle inequality for d₂, and d₁ ≤ (b − a)^{1/2} d₂.
- **`thm-riemann-l1-incomplete`** (key proof). The step functions fₙ = 𝟏_{K_n} form a d₁-Cauchy sequence in ℛ[0,1], and there is no g ∈ ℛ[0,1] with d₁(fₙ, g) → 0. Hence (ℛ[0,1], d₁) is not complete, even after identifying functions at distance 0.
  - *Try it first* (preamble).
  - **Scratch Work** (first appearance of **find a gap**):
    - The candidate limit is 𝟏_K, which is not in ℛ (§02). But a limit g need only be close to 1_K in d₁, so it could differ from 1_K a lot on a set of small length. Pointwise information about g is useless; integral information is all we have.
    - Ask what g must do on a gap G (an open interval removed at stage m). For n ≥ m, fₙ = 0 on G, so ∫_G |g| = lim ∫_G |fₙ − g| = 0. So g is "zero in integral" on every gap.
    - An integrable function with ∫_G |g| = 0 has inf_G |g| = 0: otherwise ∫_G |g| ≥ |G| · inf > 0.
    - Every partition interval J contains a whole gap (K is nowhere dense), so inf_J |g| = 0. Then L(|g|, P) = 0 for every P, so ∫|g| = 0.
    - Compare: ∫|g| ≥ ∫fₙ − d₁(fₙ, g) → 1/2. Contradiction.
  - *Proof:*
    - Cauchy: d₁(fₙ, f_m) = |K_n| − |K_m| = 2^{−n−1} − 2^{−m−1} for n < m.
    - Then the forward argument above in five numbered steps, the third and fourth citing AN1.8 (integrability of |g|; monotonicity).
  - Mark where nowhere density is used (step 4) and where |K| > 0 is used (step 5). For the middle-thirds Cantor set step 5 fails, and indeed 𝟏_C ∈ ℛ is the d₁-limit there.
- **`cor-riemann-l2-incomplete`.** The same sequence is d₂-Cauchy with no d₂-limit in ℛ[0,1].
  - *Proof:* (𝟏_{K_n} − 𝟏_{K_m})² = |𝟏_{K_n} − 𝟏_{K_m}|, so d₂² = d₁ and the sequence is Cauchy. A d₂-limit would be a d₁-limit by `lem-riemann-cauchy-schwarz`.
- **What the completion would be.**
  - Every metric space has a completion (AN2.2, prose pointer). So abstractly there is a complete space containing ℛ[0,1] densely. Its new points are equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences, not functions.
  - The question MT.7 answers: can those points be taken to be functions, with d₁ given by an integral? Yes: L¹[0,1] (Riesz–Fischer). Opened here, paid there.
- **Remark (see HA.3, side branch).** A reader who has done Fourier series: Parseval sends ℛ(𝕋) to ℓ²(ℤ), but not onto. The safe statement is the only one made: ℓ² is complete and ℛ(𝕋) is not (the argument of `cor-riemann-l2-incomplete` transfers to 𝕋), so the isometry f ↦ (f̂(n)) cannot be onto ℓ². HA.10 builds the right space. No claim in this remark needs HA to verify, and no specific square-summable sequence is asserted to be missed.
- **Quick check.** Is (C[0,1], d₁) complete? *Answer:* no. The continuous gₙ ↓ 𝟏_K of §02 are d₁-Cauchy, since they are monotone with convergent integrals. A continuous d₁-limit g would satisfy ∫_G |g| = lim ∫_G gₙ = 0 on each gap G (the tents over G shrink), so g = 0 on G by continuity. The rest of the theorem's argument runs unchanged.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1** (comprehension, key proof):
    - (a) which step uses that K is nowhere dense;
    - (b) which uses |K| > 0;
    - (c) the proof's idea in two sentences;
    - (d) run the argument with the middle-thirds Cantor set C and its stages C_n, and say which step fails and what the d₁-limit is.
    - *Answers:* (a) step 4; (b) step 5; (c) a limit must vanish in integral on every gap, and every interval contains a gap, so its integral is 0, while the fₙ keep integral ≥ 1/2; (d) step 5 fails since |C_n| → 0, and the limit is 0 (equivalently 𝟏_C).
  - **A2.** Show d₁ is not a metric on ℛ[0,1] but d₁(f,g) = 0 for continuous f, g forces f = g. *Answer:* a positive continuous |f − g| at a point stays ≥ c on an interval.
  - **B1.** Prove d₂ satisfies the triangle inequality, using `lem-riemann-cauchy-schwarz`.
  - **B2** (faded, last step blank). Prove the lower integral of 𝟏_K is 0: "Let P be a partition and J one of its intervals. Since K is nowhere dense, … *Name the move, then complete it.*" *Answer:* find a gap: J contains a gap, where 𝟏_K = 0, so inf_J = 0.
  - **C1.** Show (ℛ[0,1], sup metric) *is* complete, and explain why that does not contradict the theorem. *Answer:* uniform limits of integrable functions are integrable (AN1.9 proof pattern). The sup metric is stronger: 𝟏_{K_n} is not sup-Cauchy, since the distance is 1 at points of the gaps.

    - 2: contradiction with ∫fₙ ≥ 1/2.

## 04-volterra.md — A bounded derivative with no integral (≈ 4,000 words)

- **Opening frame.** FTC II: if F is differentiable on [a,b] and F′ ∈ ℛ[a,b], then ∫F′ = F(b) − F(a). The chapter hook asks whether the second hypothesis is ever needed when F′ is bounded. Recall that x² sin(1/x) already has a discontinuous bounded derivative, yet is integrable: one bad point is harmless. The plan is to make the bad set fat.
- **`lem-x2sin-model`.** Let g(x) = x² sin(1/x) for x > 0, with g(0) = 0. Then:
  - (i) g is differentiable on [0, ∞) with g′(0) = 0 and g′(x) = 2x sin(1/x) − cos(1/x) for x > 0;
  - (ii) |g(x)| ≤ x² and |g′(x)| ≤ 1 + 2x;
  - (iii) g′(1/(2kπ)) = −1 and g′(1/((2k+1)π)) = +1 for k ≥ 1;
  - (iv) for every ℓ > 0 the set {x ∈ (0, ℓ/2] : g′(x) = 0} is nonempty and has a largest element c(ℓ).
  - *Proof Idea:* (i)–(iii) by direct computation. (iv): g′ changes sign between consecutive points of (iii) inside (0, ℓ/2], so the zero set Z is nonempty. For z₀ ∈ Z, the set Z ∩ [z₀, ℓ/2] is closed and bounded (g′ is continuous on (0, ∞)), so it has a maximum.
- **`def-volterra-function`** (**splice a local model**).
  - For each gap (a, b) of K, with ℓ = b − a and c = c(ℓ), set:
    - V(x) = g(x − a) on (a, a + c];
    - V(x) = g(c) on [a + c, b − c];
    - V(x) = g(b − x) on [b − c, b).
  - Set V = 0 on K.
  - **Scratch Work** (first appearance of splicing):
    - Why x² sin(1/x): its derivative oscillates between −1 and 1 at the endpoint, while |g| ≤ x² makes it flat to first order.
    - Why stop at a zero of g′: the constant middle piece has derivative 0, so the pieces join with matching derivatives and V is C¹ inside every gap.
    - Why mirror at b: the same behaviour at both endpoints, so every endpoint of every gap is a bad point.
    - Why c ≤ ℓ/2: the two copies must not overlap.
- **`thm-volterra`** (key proof). V is differentiable at every point of [0,1], and |V′| ≤ 2. V′ is discontinuous at every point of K. And V′ ∉ ℛ[0,1].
  - *Proof route:*
    1. Inside each gap V is C¹: on each piece by `lem-x2sin-model`, and at the junctions a + c, b − c because g′(c) = 0.
    2. At x ∈ K: if y lies in a gap (a, b), then |V(y)| ≤ min(y − a, b − y)² ≤ |y − x|². On the plateau |g(c)| ≤ c² ≤ min(y − a, b − y)² because y is at distance ≥ c from both ends. If y ∈ K, then V(y) = 0. So |V(y) − V(x)| ≤ |y − x|² and V′(x) = 0. One-sided at 0 and 1.
    3. |V′| ≤ 1 + 2c ≤ 1 + 2 · (1/8) ≤ 2 inside gaps (every gap has ℓ ≤ 1/4, so c ≤ 1/8), and 0 on K.
    4. Oscillation: near each endpoint a, `lem-x2sin-model` (iii) gives points a + 1/(2kπ) and a + 1/((2k+1)π) in (a, a + c) with V′ = −1 and +1, for all large k. Every x ∈ K is a limit of endpoints (`def-fat-cantor-set` facts). So every neighbourhood of x contains points with V′ = ±1, and osc_{V′}(x) ≥ 2 ≥ 1.
    5. `lem-oscillation-fat-set` with η = 1 gives V′ ∉ ℛ[0,1].
  - Mark: step 5 is the second use of "oscillation on a fat set". Alternatively, Lebesgue's criterion (AN1.8, stated) gives step 5 at once. The text says so in one "Alternatively," sentence and keeps the self-contained route as the proof.
- **What survives.**
  - V′ is a derivative, so it has the intermediate value property (AN1.7, prose).
  - The continuity points of V′ include every point outside K. Hence they form a dense open set, consistent with C2 of §02 (a derivative is a pointwise limit of continuous difference quotients).
  - The pathology is not topological. It is the size of K.
- **Payoff, forward.** MT.2 shows ∫₀¹ V′ = V(1) − V(0) = 0 for the Lebesgue integral. The difference quotients n(V(x + 1/n) − V(x)) are bounded by 2 (mean value theorem) and converge to V′, so dominated convergence applies. The chapter hook is closed there. Prose pointer only.
- **Quick check.** Why not put the untruncated g(x − a) on the left half of each gap and g(b − x) on the right half? *Answer:* at the midpoint the two pieces meet with derivatives g′(ℓ/2) and −g′(ℓ/2). These differ unless g′(ℓ/2) = 0, so V would fail to be differentiable there.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1** (comprehension, key proof `thm-volterra`):
    - (a) where g′(c) = 0 is used;
    - (b) why |V(y)| ≤ |y − x|² gives V′(x) = 0;
    - (c) which step fails if K is replaced by the middle-thirds Cantor set, and what V′ then is;
    - (d) the proof's idea in two sentences.
    - *Answers:* (a) step 1, junctions; (b) |V(y) − V(x)|/|y − x| ≤ |y − x|; (c) step 5, since the cover lemma fails (content 0), and V′ is then Riemann integrable; (d) splice a function whose derivative oscillates at its endpoint into every gap of a fat nowhere dense set; the derivative is bounded but oscillates on a set of positive content.
  - **A2.** Compute c(ℓ) numerically for ℓ = 1/4 (largest zero of g′ in (0, 1/8]). *Answer:* from the sidecar, to 4 decimals. The text gives the bracketing points of (iii) as a by-hand check.
  - **B1.** Let h ∈ ℛ[0,1]. Show that the set {x : h(x) ≠ V′(x)} cannot have content zero. *Answer:* changing a bounded function on a set of content zero keeps it in ℛ, with the same integral; the proof covers the set by intervals of total length < ε and adjusts the Darboux sums. So h = V′ off a content-zero set would put V′ in ℛ, contradicting `thm-volterra`.
  - **B2.** Show V′ has a primitive (namely V), so "has an antiderivative" and "is Riemann integrable" are independent properties, using §01 C1 for the other direction.
  - **C1** (exit check 2). Prove `thm-volterra` steps 2 and 4 in full. Marking scheme:
    - 3: the estimate |V(y)| ≤ |y − x|², including the plateau case;
    - 2: V′ = 0 on K;
    - 3: points with V′ = ±1 near every endpoint;
    - 2: density of endpoints in K.
    - Fatal error: forgetting the plateau case.
  - **C2.** Replace x² by |x|^{3/2} in the model. Which conclusions survive? *Answer:* V is still differentiable on K (|V(y)| ≤ |y − x|^{3/2}), but V′ is unbounded near endpoints. So V′ is not Riemann integrable for the trivial reason. The bounded case is the interesting one.

## 05-length-of-sets.md — Jordan content, measure zero, and what finite covers cannot see (≈ 4,100 words)

- **Opening frame.** Every failure so far came back to one question: how long is K, or a gap set, or ℚ ∩ [0,1]? The Riemann integral answers that question for some sets. This section finds exactly which sets, and why that is not enough.
- **Failure-motivated definition `def-jordan-content-line`.**
  - Hook: "length" is clear for intervals and finite unions. The natural extension is to approximate from outside and inside by finite unions of intervals.
  - For bounded E ⊆ ℝ:
    - c*(E) = inf{Σ|I_j| : finitely many intervals covering E};
    - c_*(E) = sup{Σ|I_j| : finitely many disjoint intervals contained in E}.
  - **`def-jordan-measurable-line`:** E is Jordan measurable if c*(E) = c_*(E), and then c(E) is the common value.
  - Say that AN3.4 did this in ℝⁿ (see). Here only ℝ is needed.
- **`prp-content-darboux`.** For bounded E ⊆ [a,b], c*(E) is the upper integral of 𝟏_E and c_*(E) is the lower integral. So E is Jordan measurable iff 𝟏_E ∈ ℛ[a,b], and then c(E) = ∫𝟏_E.
  - *Proof Idea:* a partition's upper sum counts the intervals meeting E, which form a cover. Conversely, a finite cover refines to a partition whose E-meeting intervals have total length ≤ the cover's total + ε (endpoints).
- **`prp-content-finitely-additive`.** For disjoint Jordan-measurable E, F: E ∪ F is Jordan measurable and c(E ∪ F) = c(E) + c(F).
  - *Proof:* 𝟏_{E∪F} = 𝟏_E + 𝟏_F, then linearity (AN1.8).
- **`prp-countable-additivity-fails`.**
  - (a) ℚ ∩ [0,1] is a countable union of singletons, each of content 0, but c*(ℚ ∩ [0,1]) = 1 and c_* = 0.
  - (b) (**`exm-gap-set`**) U = (0,1) \ K is a disjoint union of countably many open intervals with total length 1/2, but c_*(U) = 1/2 and c*(U) = 1. So U is an open set that is not Jordan measurable.
  - *Try it first* on (b) (preamble).
  - *Proof of (b):*
    - c_*(U) ≥ the total length of the first N stages of gaps, which tends to 1/2.
    - c_*(U) ≤ 1 − c*(K) = 1/2, because a finite disjoint family inside U leaves a finite union of intervals covering K (`lem-fat-cantor-cover`).
    - c*(U) = 1, because U is dense, so any finite cover of U covers [0,1] up to finitely many points.
  - Lesson in one display: **the only "length" of U that respects countable unions is 1/2, and Jordan content cannot say it.**
- **Measure zero versus content zero.**
  - Recall measure zero (AN1.8): for every ε there is a *countable* cover by intervals of total length < ε.
  - ℚ ∩ [0,1] has measure zero but c* = 1.
  - **Preview (prose, no label):** replacing "finitely many" by "countably many" in c* gives Lebesgue outer measure m*. With it, m*(ℚ ∩ [0,1]) = 0, m*(U) = 1/2 and m*(K) = 1/2. MT.1 defines m* on ℝᵈ with cubes and proves these; this chapter proves none of them.
- **Two notions of small** (`prp-null-comeager-split`). There is a set G ⊆ [0,1] that is comeager and of measure zero. So [0,1] = G ∪ ([0,1] \ G) splits into a null set and a meager set.
  - *Proof:*
    - Enumerate ℚ ∩ [0,1] and let U_n be the union of open intervals of length 2^{−k}/n around the k-th rational (intersected with [0,1]).
    - G = ⋂ U_n is a countable intersection of dense open sets, hence comeager. It is uncountable by AN2.6 (a comeager set in a complete metric space without isolated points).
    - G has measure zero because the U_n cover it with total length ≤ 2/n.
  - K is the opposite: nowhere dense, hence meager, with c*(K) = 1/2.
  - Moral: Baire category (AN2.6) and length measure different things. Riemann integrability cares about length (Lebesgue's criterion, stated); continuity points of pointwise limits care about category (§02 C2).
- **ℝᵈ remark (prose).** K × [0,1] is a compact subset of the plane without Jordan area (AN3.4, see). MT.1 works in ℝᵈ from the start with cubes, following S&S III.
- **The weird-examples table** (the chapter's; it points back to F4.7's table and to AN2.15 as prose). Columns: object; what it does; why the Riemann theory cannot handle it; where MT fixes it.
  - 𝟏_{ℚ∩[0,1]}: increasing limit of integrable functions, not integrable; MT.2.
  - 𝟏_K: decreasing limit of continuous functions, not integrable, upper integral 1/2; MT.1–MT.2.
  - Thomae's function: integrable despite a dense set of discontinuities; no fix needed (contrast).
  - 𝟏_C (middle-thirds): integrable despite uncountably many discontinuities (contrast).
  - V′: bounded derivative, not integrable; MT.2, MT.10.
  - Dyadic singular f: unbounded on every interval, partial integrals bounded by √2; MT.2.
  - 𝟏_{K_n}: d₁-Cauchy without a limit in ℛ; MT.7.
  - U = (0,1) \ K: open, not Jordan measurable; MT.1.
  - G: comeager and null; MT.1 (null sets) together with AN2.6.
- **Quick check.** Is K Jordan measurable? *Answer:* no. c_*(K) = 0 (no interval inside K) and c*(K) = 1/2.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Compute c* and c_* of [0,1] \ {1/n : n ≥ 1}, of the middle-thirds Cantor set, and of ℚ ∩ [0, 1/2]. *Answers:* 1, 1; 0, 0; 1/2, 0.
  - **A2.** Is every open subset of [0,1] Jordan measurable? Every closed one? *Answer:* no to both: U and K.
  - **B1.** Prove `prp-content-darboux` in full (both inequalities, with the ε for endpoints).
  - **B2.** Show c* is finitely subadditive but not countably subadditive, with witnesses. *Answer:* ℚ ∩ [0,1] as a union of singletons.
  - **C1.** Show that for every ε ∈ (0, 1) there is a closed nowhere dense F ⊆ [0,1] with c*(F) = 1 − ε. *Answer:* at stage n remove from the middle of each of the 2^{n−1} intervals an open interval of length 2ε · 4^{−n}, so the total removed is ε Σ 2^{−n} = ε. Feasibility: each stage-(n−1) interval has length (1 − ε + ε 2^{1−n})/2^{n−1} > 2ε · 4^{−n}, which is equivalent to (1 − ε)2^n + ε > 0. Then c*(F) = 1 − ε by the proof of `lem-fat-cantor-cover`. Sidecar-check.
  - **C2** (faded, last half blank; third use of **find a gap**). Let F ⊆ [0,1] be closed and nowhere dense with c*(F) = c > 0. Let F_n ↓ F be finite unions of closed intervals with |F_n| → c. Show no g ∈ ℛ[0,1] has d₁(𝟏_{F_n}, g) → 0. The first half is given: ∫_G |g| = 0 on every component G of [0,1] \ F_m, for every m. The rest is blank: "*Name the move, then complete it.*" *Answer:* find a gap, then the contradiction as in `thm-riemann-l1-incomplete`; c*(F) = c replaces `lem-fat-cantor-cover`.

## 06-the-list.md — What an integral must provide (≈ 3,300 words)

- **Opening frame.** Four sections, four failures, one missing number. This section names the remedy and writes the specification that the next chapters meet.
- **Slicing the range** (**`exm-lebesgue-slicing`**, the move "slice the range"). For 0 ≤ f ≤ M, Riemann groups x by position. Lebesgue instead groups x by the value of f:
  Σₖ yₖ · length{x : yₖ ≤ f(x) < yₖ₊₁}.
  - Run it on 𝟏_K: the slices are K and its complement, so the sum is 1 · length(K) = 1/2. That is the number §02 (lim ∫gₙ) and §03 (lim ∫𝟏_{K_n}) wanted.
  - Run it on 𝟏_{ℚ∩[0,1]}: 1 · length(ℚ ∩ [0,1]) = 0, provided length is countably additive.
  - **The catch:** the slices of a general f are arbitrary sets, not intervals. So the method needs a length for very general sets, with countable additivity, before it can be a definition. That is MT.1. MT.2 turns the sum into the definition of ∫.
  - Worked example: for f(x) = x² on [0,1], the slices are intervals [√yₖ, √yₖ₊₁) and the sums converge to 1/3. Agreement with Riemann on a continuous function, computed by hand.
- **`rem-the-list`** (labelled remark; MT.1 opens by quoting it). An integral on ℝᵈ worth having must provide:
  - **(L1)** a length (volume) m on a σ-algebra containing all open sets, that is countably additive, translation invariant and agrees with |I| on intervals (MT.1);
  - **(L2)** an integral of every nonnegative measurable function, with values in [0, ∞], agreeing with the Riemann integral on ℛ[a,b] (MT.2);
  - **(L3)** monotone and dominated convergence, with no hypothesis that the limit be integrable (MT.2; Arzelà becomes a corollary);
  - **(L4)** complete spaces L¹ and L² that contain the step functions densely: the completion of §03, made of functions (MT.7);
  - **(L5)** iterated integrals in either order for nonnegative or absolutely integrable functions (MT.4), and the change of variables (MT.5);
  - **(L6)** differentiation: (d/dx) ∫ₐˣ f = f almost everywhere, and FTC for every bounded derivative and every absolutely continuous function (MT.9, MT.10);
  - **(L7)** unbounded functions and unbounded domains in one theory, via absolute integrability (MT.2);
  - **(L8)** the same theory on abstract spaces: counting measure turns series into integrals, and probability measures (MT.3; PR.1 on the side branch).
- **Two honest limits** (also part of `rem-the-list`):
  - **(N1)** no countably additive, translation-invariant length is defined on *all* subsets of ℝ (MT.1, Vitali set; it uses the axiom of choice);
  - **(N2)** conditionally convergent improper integrals such as ∫ sin x / x stay outside the Lebesgue integral (§01; gauge integral, AN2.14, see).
- **Remark (prose; see HA.3, HA.10).** Pointwise convergence of Fourier series is settled only inside the Lebesgue theory: Kolmogorov's L¹ function with an a.e.-divergent Fourier series, and Carleson's theorem for L². This is stated as fact, with no proof and no history. It is outside the main line.
- **Quick check.** Which items of the list does `thm-arzela-bounded-convergence` address, and which hypothesis of it does (L3) remove? *Answer:* (L3); "f ∈ ℛ".
