**Purpose.**
- In finite dimension every linear map is continuous, every pointwise bounded family of linear maps is uniformly bounded, and every linear bijection has a continuous inverse. All three fail in general normed spaces, and the chapter shows that **completeness** (through Baire's theorem) restores each of them.
- Three theorems carry the chapter: the uniform boundedness principle, the open mapping theorem (with the bounded inverse theorem), and the closed graph theorem. Each is preceded by the counterexample on an incomplete space that shows why the hypothesis is there.
- The headline application answers a question the reader has carried since AN1.9: there is a continuous periodic function whose Fourier series diverges at a point. Indeed the typical continuous function does.

**Named moves** (index.md, "Named moves"):
- **The Baire-to-ball move.** Cover the space by countably many closed sets defined by a bound; Baire gives one with an interior point; translate and scale that ball to the origin to get a uniform bound. (First operator use in `thm-ubp`; reused in `thm-open-mapping` step 1.)
- **Resonance (condensation of singularities).** If the norms of a family are unbounded, the set of points where the family is unbounded is comeagre: the sets where it is bounded by n are closed and nowhere dense.
- **Successive approximation.** An approximate solution with error control at every scale is upgraded to an exact one by summing a geometric series of corrections in a complete space. (`thm-open-mapping` step 2.)
- **The closed graph test.** To show a linear map between Banach spaces is bounded, assume xₙ → x **and** Txₙ → y, and only identify y = Tx. (`thm-closed-graph`; faded triple in §5.)

## 01-baire-recalled.md — Baire's theorem recalled, and what it says about linear spaces

**Opening frame.** In AN2.6 Baire's theorem said that a complete metric space is not a countable union of nowhere dense sets, and the payoff was that the typical continuous function is nowhere differentiable. In a normed space the closed sets that appear are usually closed subspaces or closed balls, and a short lemma says when they are nowhere dense.

**Recall** (cite, no proof): `@thm-baire` from AN2.6: a nonempty complete metric space is not a countable union of nowhere dense closed sets; equivalently, a countable intersection of dense open sets is dense. Recall the words *nowhere dense*, *meagre*, *comeagre* from AN2.6.

**Statements.**
- `lem-subspace-interior` (Lemma). A linear subspace M of a normed space X with an interior point is all of X. *Proof sketch:* if x₀ + rB_X ⊂ M then rB_X ⊂ M (subtract x₀ ∈ M), and every x ≠ 0 is a multiple of a point of rB_X.
- `cor-proper-closed-subspace-nowhere-dense` (Corollary). A proper closed subspace is nowhere dense. *Sketch:* closed and with empty interior by the lemma.
- `thm-no-countable-hamel-basis` (Theorem). An infinite-dimensional Banach space has no countable Hamel basis: it is not the linear span of any countable set. *Proof sketch:* if X = span{e₁, e₂, …}, then X = ⋃ₙ Fₙ with Fₙ = span{e₁,…,eₙ}; each Fₙ is finite-dimensional, hence closed (cite FA.1a), and proper (X is infinite-dimensional), hence nowhere dense; contradiction with `@thm-baire`.

**Weird example (failure first).** `exm-c00-meagre`: c₀₀ (finitely supported sequences) with the sup norm is a countable union of the nowhere dense closed subspaces Fₙ = {x : xₖ = 0 for k > n}. So c₀₀ is meagre in itself, and no norm on it is complete. Same for the polynomials 𝒫 on [0,1] (`exm-polynomials-no-complete-norm`): no norm whatsoever makes 𝒫 a Banach space. Stress: this is a statement about **every** norm, proved without knowing any of them.

**Worked example.** `exm-baire-subspace-chain`: if X is Banach and X = ⋃ₙ Mₙ with closed subspaces M₁ ⊂ M₂ ⊂ …, then X = M_N for some N. (Baire gives an Mₙ with interior; the lemma finishes.)

**Quick checks.**
- `exr-qc-rationals-meagre` *Quick check.* ℚ is a countable union of nowhere dense sets. Why is this no contradiction with Baire? (Answer: ℚ with the usual metric is not complete.)
- `exr-qc-dense-subspace` *Quick check.* Can a proper subspace be dense? Can a proper *closed* subspace be dense? (Answer: yes, c₀₀ in c₀; no, its closure is itself.)

**Warning.** Baire's theorem needs completeness of the *whole space* in which the union is taken; showing each piece is closed in some larger space is not enough.

**Exercises.**
- A1 `exr-baire-a1` Show that ℓ¹, viewed as a subspace of ℓ², is a countable union of closed (in ℓ²) sets Eₙ = {x ∈ ℓ¹ : ‖x‖₁ ≤ n}, each nowhere dense in ℓ². *Answer:* Eₙ is closed in ℓ² by Fatou-type lower semicontinuity of finite partial sums (Σ_{k≤K}|xₖ| ≤ n passes to ℓ²-limits for each K); it has empty interior because any ℓ² ball contains a point with infinite ℓ¹ norm (add ε·(1/k)ₖ scaled into the ball). Conclude ℓ¹ is meagre in ℓ².
- A2 `exr-baire-a2` Is the subspace c₀₀ closed in ℓ^∞? Nowhere dense? *Answer:* not closed (closure is c₀); nowhere dense (closure c₀ is a proper closed subspace).
- B1 `exr-baire-b1` In ℓ² the span of the unit vectors e₁, e₂, … is dense. Show it is not all of ℓ² by exhibiting a vector outside it, and explain why `thm-no-countable-hamel-basis` predicts this without any example. *Answer:* (1/k); finite combinations have finite support; the theorem says no countable set spans.
- B2 `exr-baire-b2` Let X be Banach and f: X → ℝ continuous with X = ⋃ₙ {|f| ≤ n}. Show that f is bounded on some ball. *Answer:* the sets are closed; Baire gives interior.
- C1 `exr-baire-c1` Show that (ℓᵖ)* (1 ≤ p < ∞) and c₀* have no countable Hamel basis. *Answer:* each is Banach (FA.1a: B(X,𝕂) is complete) and infinite-dimensional, since the coordinate functionals are linearly independent; apply `thm-no-countable-hamel-basis`. (Restricted to these spaces on purpose: for a general X, infinite-dimensionality of X* needs Hahn–Banach, FA.3.)

## 02-uniform-boundedness.md — The uniform boundedness principle

**Opening frame (failure-motivated).** A family 𝒯 ⊂ B(X,Y) is *pointwise bounded* if sup_{T∈𝒯}‖Tx‖ < ∞ for each x, and *uniformly bounded* if sup_{T∈𝒯}‖T‖ < ∞. Uniform ⇒ pointwise always. The converse fails on c₀₀.

**Weird example first.** `exm-ubp-fails-c00`: on (c₀₀, ‖·‖_∞) let Tₙx = n xₙ (a functional). For each x, |Tₙx| = 0 once n exceeds the support, so the family is pointwise bounded; but ‖Tₙ‖ = n. *Definitions:* `def-pointwise-bounded`, `def-uniformly-bounded`.

**Statements.**
- `thm-ubp` (Uniform boundedness principle). Let X be a Banach space, Y a normed space, and 𝒯 ⊂ B(X,Y) a nonempty family with sup_{T∈𝒯}‖Tx‖ < ∞ for every x ∈ X. Then sup_{T∈𝒯}‖T‖ < ∞.
  - *Try it first (ten minutes): the hypothesis gives a bound M_x depending on x. Which set of x would you like to be large, and why is "large" the right word?*
  - *Scratch Work (first operator Baire argument).* Eₙ = {x : ‖Tx‖ ≤ n for all T ∈ 𝒯} = ⋂_T T⁻¹(closed ball) is closed; pointwise boundedness says ⋃ Eₙ = X. Baire: some E_N contains x₀ + rB_X. For ‖z‖ < r, ‖Tz‖ ≤ ‖T(x₀+z)‖ + ‖Tx₀‖ ≤ 2N. So ‖T‖ ≤ 2N/r for all T.
  - *Proof:* the Scratch Work, written as a proof; where completeness of X is used is marked; Y's completeness is never used (point it out).
- `cor-banach-steinhaus` (Banach–Steinhaus). X Banach, Y normed, Tₖ ∈ B(X,Y), and Tₖx converges in Y for every x. Then Tx := lim Tₖx defines T ∈ B(X,Y) with ‖T‖ ≤ liminf ‖Tₖ‖ < ∞. *Sketch:* convergent sequences are bounded, so UBP applies; linearity of T from linearity of limits; ‖Tx‖ = lim‖Tₖx‖ ≤ liminf‖Tₖ‖‖x‖.
- `cor-ubp-resonance` (Resonance). X Banach, Y normed, 𝒯 ⊂ B(X,Y) with sup_{T∈𝒯}‖T‖ = ∞. Then R = {x : sup_T ‖Tx‖ = ∞} is comeagre (in particular dense and nonempty). *Sketch:* each Eₙ is closed; if one had an interior point the proof of `thm-ubp` would bound sup‖T‖; so each Eₙ is nowhere dense, and R = X ∖ ⋃Eₙ.
- `cor-weakstar-bounded` (Corollary). X Banach and A ⊂ X* with sup_{φ∈A}|φ(x)| < ∞ for every x. Then A is bounded in X*. *Sketch:* `thm-ubp` with Y = 𝕂. Remark: the companion statement for subsets of X ("weakly bounded ⇒ bounded") needs Hahn–Banach to see X inside X**; promised to FA.5 after FA.3.
- `prp-sequence-multipliers` (Proposition). Let a = (aₖ) be a scalar sequence. (i) If Σₖ aₖxₖ converges for every x ∈ c₀, then a ∈ ℓ¹. (ii) If 1 ≤ p < ∞ and Σₖ aₖxₖ converges for every x ∈ ℓᵖ, then a ∈ ℓ^q with 1/p + 1/q = 1 (q = ∞ when p = 1). *Proof sketch:* φ_N(x) = Σ_{k≤N} aₖxₖ is a bounded functional; compute ‖φ_N‖ = ‖(a₁,…,a_N)‖_{q} (for c₀: q = 1) by Hölder (cite FA.1a) and the extremal finitely supported x (xₖ = sgn-type conjugate phase |aₖ|^{q−1}/aₖ‑form, zero for k > N; for c₀ the phases ā_k/|a_k|). The partial sums converge, so `cor-banach-steinhaus`/`thm-ubp` gives sup_N ‖φ_N‖ < ∞, which is the claim.

**Worked example.** `exm-c0-multiplier`: run (i) in full as the model of "compute the norms of the truncations, then invoke UBP". Then show the converse (a ∈ ℓ¹ ⇒ the series converges absolutely) so the proposition is an equivalence.

**Quick checks.**
- `exr-qc-ubp-hypothesis` *Quick check.* In `exm-ubp-fails-c00`, which hypothesis of `thm-ubp` fails? (Answer: c₀₀ is not complete.)
- `exr-qc-banach-steinhaus-norm` *Quick check.* Give Tₖ ∈ B(ℓ²) converging pointwise to 0 with ‖Tₖ‖ = 1 for all k. (Answer: Tₖx = xₖ e₁, or the left shift powers.) So "≤ liminf" cannot be improved to "= lim".

**Warning.** UBP gives a bound, not convergence: a uniformly bounded sequence need not converge pointwise. The convergence statement with a dense set is a separate 3ε argument (Exercise B2), and it needs Y complete.

**Find the error (true conclusion).** `exr-ubp-find-error`: "Let X be Banach and 𝒯 pointwise bounded, say ‖Tx‖ ≤ M_x. For ‖x‖ ≤ 1 we get ‖Tx‖ ≤ M_x ≤ sup_{‖x‖≤1} M_x =: M, so ‖T‖ ≤ M." *Solution:* the conclusion is true (it is UBP) but the last step assumes sup_{‖x‖≤1} M_x < ∞, which is exactly what is to be proved; illegal step: taking a sup of a pointwise bound as if it were finite. Counterexample to the step: on c₀₀ with `exm-ubp-fails-c00`, M_x = sup_n n|xₙ| is finite for each x but unbounded on the unit ball.

**Exercises.**
- A1 `exr-ubp-a1` (key-proof comprehension, §11.2) (a) Where exactly is completeness of X used in the proof of `thm-ubp`? (b) Why is Eₙ closed? (c) Run the proof on `exm-ubp-fails-c00` and say which step breaks. *Answers:* (a) to apply Baire to ⋃Eₙ = X; (b) intersection of preimages of a closed ball under continuous maps; (c) the Eₙ are closed and cover c₀₀ but c₀₀ is not complete, so no Eₙ need have interior (indeed none does).
- A2 `exr-ubp-a2` Show that a pointwise convergent sequence in X* (X Banach) is bounded. *Answer:* `cor-weakstar-bounded`.
- B1 `exr-ubp-b1` On ℓ¹ let Pₙx = (x₁, …, xₙ, 0, 0, …). Show Pₙx → x for every x, ‖Pₙ‖ = 1, and identify the limit operator given by `cor-banach-steinhaus`. Then let Qₙx = (1 − 1/n)x₁e₁ + Pₙx − x₁e₁ and compute lim Qₙ and liminf‖Qₙ‖. *Answer:* the identity, norm 1; Qₙ → identity, ‖Qₙ‖ = 1 for n ≥ 1, consistent with ‖I‖ ≤ liminf.
- B2 `exr-ubp-b2` (dense-set convergence) X Banach, Y Banach, sup‖Tₖ‖ < ∞, and Tₖx converges for every x in a dense set D. Prove Tₖx converges for every x. *Answer:* 3ε argument with the uniform bound; Y complete for the limit to exist.
- B3 `exr-ubp-b3` Using `cor-ubp-resonance`, show that the set of x ∈ c₀ whose partial sums Σ_{k≤N} xₖ are unbounded is comeagre in c₀. *Answer:* φ_N(x) = Σ_{k≤N}xₖ has ‖φ_N‖ = N → ∞.
- C1 `exr-ubp-c1` Prove `prp-sequence-multipliers` (ii) for p = 1 and p = 2 in full, including the computation of ‖φ_N‖. Hint ladder: (1) the extremal x; (2) "Let x = (ā₁,…,ā_N,0,…)/‖(a₁,…,a_N)‖₂."

## 03-divergent-fourier-series.md — A continuous function whose Fourier series diverges at a point

**Opening frame.** Pays hook Q1. Fourier series are the main subject of the side branch HA; here only what UBP needs is set up, all with the Riemann integral. *See* HA.1 for the theory.

**Setup (definitions, no new theory).** `def-fourier-partial-sum`: for f ∈ C(𝕋), f̂(n) = (1/2π)∫_{−π}^{π} f(t)e^{−int} dt and S_N f(x) = Σ_{|n|≤N} f̂(n)e^{inx}. C(𝕋) with ‖·‖_∞ is a Banach space (closed subspace of the bounded continuous functions on ℝ; cite FA.1a for C(K) and AN2.2 for closed subspaces). Dirichlet kernel D_N(t) = Σ_{|n|≤N} e^{int}.

**Statements.**
- `lem-dirichlet-kernel` (Lemma). S_N f(0) = (1/2π)∫_{−π}^{π} f(t) D_N(t) dt, and D_N(t) = sin((N+½)t)/sin(t/2) for t ∉ 2πℤ, D_N(0) = 2N+1. D_N is real, even, and (1/2π)∫D_N = 1. *Sketch:* exchange a finite sum and the integral; geometric sum times e^{it/2} − e^{−it/2}. Uses AN1.10.
- `lem-dirichlet-functional-norm` (Lemma). The functional φ_N(f) = S_N f(0) on C(𝕋) is bounded with ‖φ_N‖ = L_N := (1/2π)∫_{−π}^{π}|D_N(t)| dt. *Sketch:* ≤ is immediate. For ≥: D_N has finitely many zeros in [−π, π]; let g be continuous, 2π-periodic, |g| ≤ 1, equal to sgn D_N outside intervals of total length η around the zeros; then L_N − φ_N(g) ≤ (1/2π)·2‖D_N‖_∞·η = (2N+1)η/π, which is arbitrarily small.
- `lem-lebesgue-constants` (Lemma). L_N ≥ (4/π²) Σ_{k=1}^{N} 1/k ≥ (4/π²) log(N+1). In particular L_N → ∞. *Sketch:* L_N = (1/π)∫₀^π |D_N|; |sin(t/2)| ≤ t/2 gives |D_N(t)| ≥ 2|sin((N+½)t)|/t; substitute u = (N+½)t to get (2/π)∫₀^{(N+½)π}|sin u|/u du ≥ (2/π)Σ_{k=1}^{N}(1/(kπ))∫_{(k−1)π}^{kπ}|sin u| du = (4/π²)Σ 1/k; then Σ_{k≤N} 1/k ≥ ∫₁^{N+1} dx/x.
- `thm-divergent-fourier-series` (Theorem). There is f ∈ C(𝕋) with sup_N |S_N f(0)| = ∞; so the Fourier series of f diverges at 0. Moreover the set of such f is comeagre in C(𝕋). *Proof:* `@cor-ubp-resonance` applied to {φ_N}, with ‖φ_N‖ = L_N → ∞ by the two lemmas.
- `cor-divergence-dense-set` (Corollary). The set of f ∈ C(𝕋) whose Fourier series diverges (partial sums unbounded) at every rational multiple of π is comeagre; in particular nonempty. *Sketch:* translate (the functional at x is φ_N ∘ τ_x with the same norm); a countable intersection of comeagre sets is comeagre (cite AN2.6).

**Remarks (prose only, no citation in a proof).** The function is not constructed; explicit constructions exist and are longer. If f is C¹ (indeed Hölder), S_N f → f uniformly (*see* HA.1). In the other direction, there is an L¹ function whose Fourier series diverges almost everywhere, while the Fourier series of an L² function converges almost everywhere; both are far beyond this book's main line (pointer: the HA branch, after MT.7).

**Worked example.** `exm-l1-n-lower`: compute L₁ = 1/3 + 2√3/π ≈ 1.44 exactly (split at the zero 2π/3 of 1 + 2cos t) as a sanity check against the lower bound 4/π² ≈ 0.41 (drafter: give the numerical value and a sidecar script `verify/ch09/exm-l1-n-lower.py`), so the reader sees that the bound is crude but grows.

**Quick checks.**

**Warning.** The theorem is about *pointwise* convergence at a point; in mean square the Fourier series of every continuous function converges (*see* HA). Do not read §3 as "Fourier series are useless".

## 04-open-mapping.md — The open mapping theorem and the bounded inverse theorem

**Opening frame (pays Q2).** A bounded bijection with unbounded inverse: `exm-unbounded-inverse` the identity (c₀₀, ‖·‖₁) → (c₀₀, ‖·‖_∞) is bounded (‖x‖_∞ ≤ ‖x‖₁) and bijective, but its inverse sends eₗ + … + e_n (sup norm 1) to ℓ¹ norm n. A second, complete-domain version: `exm-diagonal-not-closed` T: ℓ² → ℓ², Tx = (xₖ/k): bounded, injective, range dense (contains c₀₀) but not all of ℓ² ((1/k) is not in the range), and the inverse on the range is unbounded (Teₖ = eₖ/k). The failure is that the range is not complete. This motivates the definition.

**Definition (failure-motivated).** `def-open-map`: T: X → Y is *open* if T(U) is open for every open U ⊂ X. For linear T this is equivalent to: T(B_X) ⊃ δB_Y for some δ > 0 (`lem-open-linear`, sketch: translate and scale).

**Statements.**
- `lem-open-linear` (Lemma). A linear map T between normed spaces is open iff T(B_X) contains a ball δB_Y.
- `thm-open-mapping` (Open mapping theorem). X, Y Banach, T ∈ B(X,Y) surjective. Then T is open.
  - *Idea.* Two steps: Baire (on Y) makes the **closure** of T(B_X) contain a ball; completeness of X removes the closure.
  - *Step 1 (Baire-to-ball, reuse from §2).* Y = ⋃ₙ closure(T(nB_X)); Baire on Y gives n, y₀, ε with y₀ + εB_Y ⊂ closure(T(nB_X)). For ‖y‖ < ε, y = (y₀ + y) − y₀ ∈ closure(T(nB_X)) − closure(T(nB_X)) ⊂ closure(T(2nB_X)). Scaling: closure(T(rB_X)) ⊃ rηB_Y for all r > 0, with η = ε/(2n).
  - *Scratch Work for Step 2 (successive approximation, first appearance).* Given ‖y‖ < η/2: pick x₁ with ‖x₁‖ < 1/2 and ‖y − Tx₁‖ < η/4 (Step 1 with r = 1/2). Then y − Tx₁ ∈ (η/4)B_Y ⊂ closure(T((1/4)B_X)): pick x₂ with ‖x₂‖ < 1/4 and ‖y − Tx₁ − Tx₂‖ < η/8. Continue: ‖xₖ‖ < 2^{−k}. Σxₖ converges absolutely, hence converges in X (completeness, cite FA.1a's series criterion), to x with ‖x‖ < 1 (strict: ‖x₁‖ < 1/2 strictly and the tail is ≤ 1/2), and Tx = y by continuity. So T(B_X) ⊃ (η/2)B_Y.
  - *Proof:* as above, then `lem-open-linear`.
- `cor-bounded-inverse` (Bounded inverse theorem). X, Y Banach, T ∈ B(X,Y) bijective. Then T⁻¹ ∈ B(Y,X). *Sketch:* T(B_X) ⊃ δB_Y gives ‖T⁻¹‖ ≤ 1/δ.
- `cor-equivalent-complete-norms` (Corollary). If ‖·‖₁, ‖·‖₂ are norms on a vector space V, both complete, and ‖v‖₁ ≤ C‖v‖₂ for all v, then the norms are equivalent. *Sketch:* the identity (V,‖·‖₂) → (V,‖·‖₁) is a bounded bijection.
- `cor-closed-range-lower-bound` (Corollary). X, Y Banach and T ∈ B(X,Y) injective. Then T(X) is closed iff there is c > 0 with ‖Tx‖ ≥ c‖x‖ for all x. *Sketch:* ⇐: a Cauchy sequence Txₙ gives Cauchy xₙ; ⇒: T(X) is closed in Y, hence Banach, and `cor-bounded-inverse` applies to T: X → T(X).

**Worked examples.**
- `exm-c1-norms`: on C¹[0,1], ‖f‖_a = ‖f‖_∞ + ‖f′‖_∞ and ‖f‖_b = |f(0)| + ‖f′‖_∞ are both complete (completeness of ‖·‖_a from AN1.9's theorem on uniform limits of derivatives; ‖·‖_b similarly), ‖f‖_b ≤ ‖f‖_a, so they are equivalent by `cor-equivalent-complete-norms`. Then the direct check: ‖f‖_∞ ≤ |f(0)| + ‖f′‖_∞ by the MVT, so ‖·‖_a ≤ 2‖·‖_b. The point: the theorem predicted a constant without computing it.
- `exm-not-complete-norms`: on C[0,1], ‖f‖₁ = ∫|f| ≤ ‖f‖_∞, but the norms are not equivalent; consistent with the corollary because (C[0,1], ‖·‖₁) is not complete (a continuous ramp approximating a jump is ‖·‖₁-Cauchy).

**Quick checks.**
- `exr-qc-open-mapping-surjective` *Quick check.* Does the open mapping theorem apply to `exm-diagonal-not-closed`? (Answer: no, T is not onto.)
- `exr-qc-projection-open` *Quick check.* Is the coordinate projection ℝ² → ℝ open? Is it closed (images of closed sets closed)? (Answer: open, yes; not closed: the hyperbola xy = 1 maps to ℝ ∖ {0}.) Open ≠ closed map.

**Warning.** "Open" here concerns images of open sets, not the graph or the range. A bounded surjection need not be injective; the theorem does not produce an inverse, only the ball inclusion.

**Proof ordering (§11.4).** `exr-open-mapping-order`: eight shuffled lines of Step 2 (choice of x₁; choice of x₂; the induction; absolute convergence; completeness gives the sum; ‖x‖ < 1; Tx = y by continuity and linearity; conclusion T(B_X) ⊃ (η/2)B_Y) plus two distractors: "choose x₁ with ‖x₁‖ < 1/2 and Tx₁ = y" (assumes what Step 1 does not give: it gives only approximate preimages) and "Σ‖xₖ‖ < ∞, so Σxₖ converges because Y is complete" (completeness of the wrong space). Solution: an admissible order, which swaps are admissible, why each distractor fails.

**Exercises.**
- A1 `exr-open-a1` (key-proof comprehension) (a) Where does Step 1 use completeness of Y, and Step 2 completeness of X? (b) Why is there a closure in Step 1? (c) Run the proof on T = identity of ℓ² and give η. *Answers:* (a) Baire on Y; convergence of Σxₖ in X. (b) Baire only gives interior of a closed set, and T(nB_X) need not be closed. (c) T(B) = B, any η ≤ 1 works in Step 1, and the conclusion T(B) ⊃ B is direct.
- A2 `exr-open-a2` Prove `lem-open-linear`.
- B1 `exr-open-b1` Show that a surjective T ∈ B(X,Y), X, Y Banach, has the "bounded preimage" property: there is C with: every y has a preimage x with ‖x‖ ≤ C‖y‖. *Answer:* scale the ball inclusion.
- B2 `exr-open-b2` On ℓ¹ define ‖x‖_w = Σ|xₖ|/k. Show ‖·‖_w ≤ ‖·‖₁, the norms are not equivalent, and conclude (ℓ¹, ‖·‖_w) is not complete. *Answer:* eₖ; contrapositive of `cor-equivalent-complete-norms`.

(This is §5's projection result proved a second way; §5 refers back.)

## 05-closed-graph.md — The closed graph theorem

**Opening frame (failure-motivated).** The differentiation map D: C¹[0,1] → C[0,1], with the sup norm on both, is unbounded (fₙ(x) = xⁿ: ‖fₙ‖_∞ = 1, ‖fₙ′‖_∞ = n). Yet it has a property boundedness would give: if fₙ → f and fₙ′ → g uniformly, then f ∈ C¹ and f′ = g (cite AN1.9's theorem on differentiating uniform limits). That property is the definition; D fails to be bounded only because (C¹[0,1], ‖·‖_∞) is not complete.

**Definitions.** `def-graph`, `def-closed-operator`: T: X → Y linear has *closed graph* if Γ(T) = {(x,Tx)} is closed in X × Y, i.e. xₙ → x and Txₙ → y imply y = Tx.

**Statements.**
- `lem-product-banach` (Lemma). X × Y with ‖(x,y)‖ = ‖x‖ + ‖y‖ is Banach when X and Y are; the coordinate projections are bounded. *Sketch:* coordinatewise Cauchy.
- `thm-closed-graph` (Closed graph theorem). X, Y Banach and T: X → Y linear with closed graph. Then T is bounded.
  - *Try it first (ten minutes): you know one theorem that turns a bounded bijection into a bounded inverse. Find a bounded bijection hidden in the data.*
  - *Scratch Work (first appearance of the closed graph test as a tool).* Γ(T) is a closed subspace of a Banach space, hence Banach (`lem-product-banach`, AN2.2). π₁: Γ(T) → X, (x,Tx) ↦ x, is a bounded bijection; `cor-bounded-inverse` makes π₁⁻¹ bounded; T = π₂ ∘ π₁⁻¹.
  - *Proof:* as in the Scratch Work.
- `thm-hellinger-toeplitz` (Hellinger–Toeplitz, on ℓ²). Let T: ℓ² → ℓ² be linear with ⟨Tx, y⟩ = ⟨x, Ty⟩ for all x, y ∈ ℓ², where ⟨x,y⟩ = Σₖ xₖ ȳₖ. Then T is bounded. *Proof:* closed graph test: if xₙ → x and Txₙ → y, then for every z, ⟨y, z⟩ = lim⟨Txₙ, z⟩ = lim⟨xₙ, Tz⟩ = ⟨x, Tz⟩ = ⟨Tx, z⟩ (continuity of ⟨·,z⟩ by Cauchy–Schwarz in ℓ², i.e. Hölder with p = q = 2 from FA.1a); take z = y − Tx. Remark: the same proof works in every Hilbert space (*see* FA.6); hence the symmetric differential operators of FA.13 cannot be defined on the whole space. This opens the FA.13 hook.
- `prp-direct-sum-projection` (Proposition). X Banach, X = M ⊕ N algebraically with M, N closed subspaces. The projection P onto M along N is bounded. *Proof sketch (closed graph test):* xₙ → x, Pxₙ → y; y ∈ M since M is closed; xₙ − Pxₙ ∈ N converges to x − y ∈ N; so x = y + (x − y) is the decomposition and Px = y. Remark (statement only, no proof): not every closed subspace has a closed complement; c₀ in ℓ^∞ has none.
- `prp-matrix-operator` (Proposition). Let (a_{jk}) be an infinite matrix such that for every x ∈ ℓ² each row sum (Ax)_j = Σₖ a_{jk}xₖ converges and Ax ∈ ℓ². Then A ∈ B(ℓ²). *Proof sketch:* each row is in ℓ² by `@prp-sequence-multipliers` (ii) (cite §2), so x ↦ (Ax)_j is continuous; closed graph test: xₙ → x, Axₙ → y gives (Axₙ)_j → (Ax)_j and (Axₙ)_j → y_j, so y = Ax.

**Faded triple (closed graph test).** Worked in full: `thm-hellinger-toeplitz`. Last step blank: B2 below (the projection; the reader identifies Px = y). Last half blank: C1 below (multiplication operators). Each blank carries "*Name the move, then complete it.*"

**Quick checks.**
- `exr-qc-graph-closed-bounded` *Quick check.* Show that every bounded T has closed graph. (Answer: Txₙ → Tx by continuity, and limits are unique.)
- `exr-qc-closed-graph-vs-continuity` *Quick check.* In the closed graph test we assume Txₙ converges. What would continuity require us to show instead? (Answer: that Txₙ converges at all, and to Tx.)

**Warning.** "Closed graph" and "closed range" are different; and a closed operator need not be bounded when its domain is not complete (the opening D).

**Find the error (false conclusion).** `exr-closed-graph-find-error`: "D: C¹[0,1] → C[0,1], with ‖·‖_∞ on both, has closed graph; C[0,1] is Banach; by the closed graph theorem D is bounded." *Solution:* the failing line is the application of `thm-closed-graph`, whose domain must be Banach; (C¹[0,1], ‖·‖_∞) is not complete. Counterexample to the step: fₙ(x) = xⁿ shows D unbounded, so the conclusion is false too.

**Exercises.**
- A1 `exr-closed-a1` (key-proof comprehension) (a) Where does the proof of `thm-closed-graph` use completeness of X, and of Y? (b) Why is π₁ injective? (c) Which step fails for the opening D? *Answers:* (a) Γ(T) is complete because X × Y is complete, which uses both; (b) the graph is a graph: one point over each x; (c) X × Y is not complete, so Γ(D) need not be Banach and `cor-bounded-inverse` does not apply.
- A2 `exr-closed-a2` Prove `lem-product-banach`.
- B1 `exr-closed-b1` Show conversely that the closed graph theorem implies the bounded inverse theorem. *Answer:* the graph of T⁻¹ is the flip of the graph of T, which is closed since T is bounded.
- B2 `exr-closed-b2` (faded, last step blank) Complete the proof of `prp-direct-sum-projection` given "xₙ → x, Pxₙ → y; y ∈ M; x − y ∈ N". *Name the move, then complete it.*
- B3 `exr-closed-b3` Let T: ℓ¹ → ℓ¹ be linear and suppose each coordinate x ↦ (Tx)_j is continuous. Show T is bounded. *Answer:* closed graph test with coordinatewise identification.
- C1 `exr-closed-c1` (faded, last half blank; exit-check style) Let g be a scalar sequence with g·x := (gₖxₖ) ∈ ℓᵖ for every x ∈ ℓᵖ (1 ≤ p < ∞). Show g ∈ ℓ^∞ and ‖M_g‖ = ‖g‖_∞. Given: "M_g is linear; suppose xₙ → x and gxₙ → y in ℓᵖ." *Name the move, then complete it.* *Answer:* coordinates identify y = gx; closed graph gives boundedness; ‖M_g eₖ‖ = |gₖ| gives |gₖ| ≤ ‖M_g‖, and ‖gx‖_p ≤ ‖g‖_∞‖x‖_p.
