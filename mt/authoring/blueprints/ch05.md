## Named moves (index.md)

- **Good sets.** To prove that every set in σ(𝒢) has a property, show that the sets with the property form a σ-algebra, or a monotone class over an algebra, that contains 𝒢. The first full use is in §1 (sections). §2 explains when the plain σ-algebra version fails and why a monotone class rescues it.
- **Tonelli first, then Fubini.** Before swapping a signed integral, run Tonelli on |f| to get ∫|f| < ∞, then swap with Fubini. This is the chapter's **main new move**, faded three times:

  - last step blank: §4 B2;
  - last half blank: §7 C2.
- **Slice and integrate (Cavalieri).** A measure of a set is the integral of the measures of its sections. Used in §3, §6 and §7.
- **Insert an integral and swap.** Write a number as an integral, such as f(x)ᵖ = ∫₀^{f(x)} p t^{p−1} dt or 1/x = ∫₀^∞ e^{−xt} dt, then exchange the order. Used in §4 and §7.

## 01-product-sigma-algebras.md — Rectangles and product σ-algebras (≈3,000 words)

- **Opening frame.** To integrate over X × Y we need a σ-algebra there that is built from 𝒮 and 𝒯.
- **Failure-motivated definition.** The measurable rectangles A × B (A ∈ 𝒮, B ∈ 𝒯; `def-measurable-rectangle`) are the obvious candidates. They are not closed under union: the union of [0,2] × [0,1] and [0,1] × [0,2] is an L-shape, which is no rectangle. So take the σ-algebra they generate: `def-product-sigma-algebra`, 𝒮 ⊗ 𝒯 = σ({A × B}).
  - Non-example: the open disk is not a rectangle but lies in ℬ(ℝ) ⊗ ℬ(ℝ). This is the content of `prp-borel-product`.
- **Sections** (`def-section`): E_x, E^y, f_x, f^y. Picture the vertical and horizontal slices of a disk.
- **`thm-sections-measurable`.**
  - Statement: for every E ∈ 𝒮 ⊗ 𝒯, *every* x ∈ X and every y ∈ Y, E_x ∈ 𝒯 and E^y ∈ 𝒮. If f: X × Y → [−∞, ∞] (or ℂ) is 𝒮 ⊗ 𝒯-measurable, then every f_x is 𝒯-measurable and every f^y is 𝒮-measurable.
  - **Scratch Work (first appearance of the good-sets move).** Name the property, collect the sets that have it, check that the collection is a σ-algebra, and check that it contains the generators. Sections commute with complements and unions, so here the check is one line each.
  - Proof: let 𝒢 = {E ⊂ X × Y : E_x ∈ 𝒯 for all x}. Then (A × B)_x is B or ∅, (Eᶜ)_x = (E_x)ᶜ, and (⋃Eₖ)_x = ⋃(Eₖ)_x. So 𝒢 is a σ-algebra containing the rectangles, and hence 𝒢 ⊇ 𝒮 ⊗ 𝒯. For functions, (f_x)⁻¹(U) = (f⁻¹(U))_x.
- **Warning.** The converse is false: a set all of whose sections are measurable need not be in 𝒮 ⊗ 𝒯. The witnesses are `exm-product-not-complete` in §6, and the optional `exm-sierpinski-set` in §5.
- **`lem-elementary-sets-algebra`.** Finite disjoint unions of measurable rectangles form an algebra. Proof: (A × B) ∩ (C × D) = (A ∩ C) × (B ∩ D), and (A × B)ᶜ = (Aᶜ × Y) ⊔ (A × Bᶜ).
- **`prp-borel-product`.** ℬ(ℝᵐ) ⊗ ℬ(ℝⁿ) = ℬ(ℝ^{m+n}).
  - ⊆: A × ℝⁿ = π₁⁻¹(A) is Borel because π₁ is continuous, and A × B = π₁⁻¹(A) ∩ π₂⁻¹(B).
  - ⊇: every open set of ℝ^{m+n} is a countable union of open boxes with rational corners, and each such box is a product of two open boxes.
- **Quick check.** Write down the sections of the diagonal D = {(x, x)} ⊂ ℝ² and say whether D ∈ ℬ ⊗ ℬ. Answer: every section is a singleton, and D is closed, so D ∈ ℬ ⊗ ℬ by `prp-borel-product`.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Compute E_x for the open unit disk and for {(x, y) : y < x}. Answer: (−√(1−x²), √(1−x²)) for |x| < 1 and ∅ otherwise; (−∞, x).
  - **A2.** If f is 𝒮-measurable and g is 𝒯-measurable, show that h(x, y) = f(x)g(y) is 𝒮 ⊗ 𝒯-measurable. Answer: f ∘ π₁ is measurable since π₁⁻¹(A) = A × Y; then use the product rule for measurable functions from MT.3.
  - **A3.** Show that 𝒫(ℕ) ⊗ 𝒫(ℕ) = 𝒫(ℕ × ℕ). Answer: each point is {m} × {n}, and every subset is a countable union of points.
  - **B1.** Show that 𝒮 ⊗ 𝒯 is the smallest σ-algebra on X × Y that makes both projections measurable.
  - **B2.** If f: X → ℝ is 𝒮-measurable, show that its graph is in 𝒮 ⊗ ℬ(ℝ). Answer: the graph is the zero set of (x, y) ↦ f(x) − y, a difference of two measurable functions.
  - **C1.** Show that every E ∈ 𝒮 ⊗ 𝒯 lies in σ(𝒞) for some *countable* family 𝒞 of rectangles. Answer: good sets. The class of E with this property is a σ-algebra (merge the countable families) containing the rectangles.
  - **C2.** Show directly that the diagonal of ℝ² equals ⋂ₙ ⋃_{k∈ℤ} [k/n, (k+1)/n)². Answer: for x ≠ y and 1/n < |x − y|, the two coordinates fall in different cells.

## 02-monotone-class.md — The monotone class theorem and the measure of a section (≈3,500 words)

- **Opening frame.** Defining (μ × ν)(E) = ∫ ν(E_x) dμ(x) needs x ↦ ν(E_x) to be measurable.
- **Scratch Work (the failure that motivates `def-monotone-class`).** Try good sets with the property P(E): "x ↦ ν(E_x) is 𝒮-measurable", for finite ν.
  - Rectangles pass: ν((A × B)_x) = ν(B)χ_A(x).
  - Complements pass: ν(Y) − ν(E_x).
  - Countable unions stall, because ν((E ∪ F)_x) = ν(E_x) + ν(F_x) − ν((E ∩ F)_x) needs E ∩ F, which nothing controls.
  - What *does* pass: finite disjoint unions (additivity), increasing unions (continuity from below) and decreasing intersections (continuity from above, which needs ν finite). Those closure properties define a monotone class.
- **`def-monotone-class`.** A class closed under increasing countable unions and decreasing countable intersections.
  - `exm-intervals-monotone-class`: the intervals of ℝ, counting ∅ and single points as intervals, form a monotone class but not an algebra.
  - Every σ-algebra is a monotone class.
  - The "algebra of sets" is recalled from MT.3, where Carathéodory's premeasures live on algebras.
- **`thm-monotone-class`.**
  - Statement: if 𝒜 is an algebra on Z, then the smallest monotone class containing 𝒜 equals σ(𝒜).
  - Idea: let ℳ be that class. Show that ℳ is an algebra; a monotone algebra is a σ-algebra.
  - Proof, in three steps:
    - {E : Eᶜ ∈ ℳ} is a monotone class containing 𝒜.
    - For A ∈ 𝒜, {E : E ∪ A ∈ ℳ} is a monotone class containing 𝒜, hence contains ℳ.
    - For F ∈ ℳ, {E : E ∪ F ∈ ℳ} contains 𝒜 by the previous step, hence contains ℳ.
  - Self-explanation prompt: "*Self-explain why the second step must come before the third.*"
- **`lem-section-measure-measurable`** (key proof 1).
  - Statement: let (X, 𝒮) be a measurable space and (Y, 𝒯, ν) a σ-finite measure space. For every E ∈ 𝒮 ⊗ 𝒯, x ↦ ν(E_x) ∈ [0, ∞] is 𝒮-measurable. The same holds with the roles of the factors exchanged.
  - Proof for ν finite: the good sets contain the elementary sets (`lem-elementary-sets-algebra`; sums of the rectangle case) and form a monotone class; apply `thm-monotone-class`.
  - Proof for ν σ-finite: take Y₁ ⊆ Y₂ ⊆ … with ν(Yₖ) < ∞. Then ν(E_x ∩ Yₖ) ↑ ν(E_x), and each ν(· ∩ Yₖ) is a finite measure.
  - "*Before reading on: which closure property needs ν finite?*"
- **Quick check.** In the finite case, where exactly does ν(Y) < ∞ enter? Answer: decreasing intersections, through continuity from above.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Every σ-algebra is a monotone class; the intervals form a monotone class that is not an algebra.
  - **A2 (proof comprehension, `lem-section-measure-measurable`).**
    - (a) Why do finite disjoint unions of rectangles pass? Answer: ν(E_x) is a finite sum of ν(Bᵢ)χ_{Aᵢ}(x).
    - (b) Which line uses ν(Y) < ∞? Answer: the decreasing-intersection line.
    - (c) Run the lemma on E = {(x, y) ∈ [0,1]² : y < x} with ν = m. Answer: ν(E_x) = x on [0,1].
    - (d) State the proof's idea in two sentences.
  - **B1.** Show that a class which is both an algebra and a monotone class is a σ-algebra.
  - **B2.** Let 𝒜 be the finite disjoint unions of intervals (a, b] ⊆ (0, 1], together with ∅. Show that the monotone class generated by 𝒜 is ℬ((0, 1]).
  - **C1.** State and prove Dynkin's π–λ theorem, and deduce `thm-monotone-class` from it. Answer sketch: an algebra is a π-system, and a monotone class containing an algebra contains the λ-system it generates.
  - **C2 (find the error; the conclusion is TRUE).** A "proof" of `lem-section-measure-measurable` for finite ν asserts that the good sets form a σ-algebra, "since ν((E ∪ F)_x) = ν(E_x) + ν(F_x)".
    - Solution: the failing line is that equation, which uses additivity without disjointness.
    - Counterexample to the step: E = F = [0,1] × [0,1] with ν = m on [0,1], where 2 ≠ 1.
    - The conclusion is true, by the lemma.

## 03-product-measure.md — Product measure: existence and uniqueness (≈3,500 words)

- **Opening frame.** With the lemma in hand, define the measure by slicing.
- **σ-finite** is recalled from MT.3. Its necessity is shown by a failure in §5.
- **`def-product-measure`.** For σ-finite μ and ν, (μ × ν)(E) = ∫_X ν(E_x) dμ(x).
- **`lem-uniqueness-on-algebra`.**
  - Statement: let 𝒜 be an algebra on Z, and let ρ₁, ρ₂ be measures on σ(𝒜) that agree on 𝒜. Suppose Z = ⋃ Zₖ with Zₖ ∈ 𝒜 increasing and ρ₁(Zₖ) < ∞. Then ρ₁ = ρ₂ on σ(𝒜).
  - Proof: for each k, the class {E : ρ₁(E ∩ Zₖ) = ρ₂(E ∩ Zₖ)} is a monotone class containing 𝒜, by continuity from above and below with finite values. Then let k → ∞.
- **`thm-product-measure`.** Let μ and ν be σ-finite. Then:
  - (i) μ × ν is a measure on 𝒮 ⊗ 𝒯.
  - (ii) (μ × ν)(A × B) = μ(A)ν(B), with 0 · ∞ = 0.
  - (iii) (μ × ν)(E) = ∫_Y μ(E^y) dν(y).
  - (iv) μ × ν is the unique measure with property (ii), and it is σ-finite.
  - Proof route:
    - (i): countable additivity by ∫Σ = Σ∫ for nonnegative terms (MT.3).
    - (ii): direct.
    - (iii): the right side is also a measure with property (ii). Apply `lem-uniqueness-on-algebra` on the elementary sets with Zₖ = Xₖ × Yₖ.
    - (iv): the same lemma.
- **Remark.** MT.3's Carathéodory extension, applied to the premeasure on elementary sets, gives the same measure; uniqueness says so. This is the Folland route, named in one sentence.
- **Warning (0 · ∞).** {0} × ℝ ⊂ ℝ² has measure 0 · ∞ = 0, and the slicing definition agrees.
- **Worked examples.**
  - Counting × counting on ℕ × ℕ is counting measure on ℕ².
  - (m × m)({0 ≤ y < x ≤ 1}) = ∫₀¹ x dx = ½.
  - (m × m)(D) = 0 for the diagonal D.
- **Quick check.** Compute (δ_a × δ_b)(E) for E ∈ 𝒮 ⊗ 𝒯. Answer: χ_E(a, b), from δ_b(E_a).
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** X = Y = {1, 2, 3, …}, μ counting, ν({n}) = 2⁻ⁿ, E = {(m, n) : m ≤ n}. Compute (μ × ν)(E) both ways.
    - Answer, by y-sections: Σ n2⁻ⁿ = 2.
    - Answer, by x-sections: Σ_m 2^{1−m} = 2.
  - **A2.** Show that μ × ν is σ-finite.
  - **B1.** Prove `lem-uniqueness-on-algebra` in the case ρ₁(Z) = ρ₂(Z) < ∞, from `thm-monotone-class`.
  - **B2.** Show that if μ' is a measure on 𝒮 ⊗ 𝒯 with μ'(A × B) = μ(A)ν(B) only for A, B of finite measure, then μ' = μ × ν (μ, ν σ-finite). Answer: the same lemma, since the rectangles of finite measure still exhaust X × Y.
  - **C1 (uniqueness needs σ-finiteness).** X = Y = [0,1] with ℬ; μ = m; c the counting measure.
    - Let ρ₂(E) = Σ_{y∈[0,1]} m(E^y).
    - Let ρ₃(E) = ρ₂(E) + ∞ · m({x : (x, x) ∈ E}), where ∞ · 0 = 0.
    - Show that both are measures on ℬ ⊗ ℬ that agree on rectangles but differ on D.
    - Answer:
      - Agreement on rectangles: if m(A ∩ B) > 0, then B is uncountable and m(A) > 0, so ρ₂(A × B) = ∞ already.
      - ρ₂(D) = 0 and ρ₃(D) = ∞.
      - The set {x : (x, x) ∈ E} is Borel, since it is the preimage under x ↦ (x, x).
    - Marking scheme outline (10): 2 each for ρ₂ a measure, ρ₃ a measure, agreement on rectangles in the three cases, the values at D, and the conclusion. Fatal error: treating c as σ-finite.

## 04-tonelli-and-fubini.md — Tonelli's and Fubini's theorems (≈4,500 words)

- **Opening frame.** Recall the hook from index.md. The two theorems answer it: Tonelli for f ≥ 0, Fubini for f ∈ L¹.
- **`thm-tonelli`** (key proof 2).
  - Statement: let (X, 𝒮, μ) and (Y, 𝒯, ν) be σ-finite, and f: X × Y → [0, ∞] be 𝒮 ⊗ 𝒯-measurable. Then:
    - x ↦ ∫_Y f_x dν is 𝒮-measurable;
    - y ↦ ∫_X f^y dμ is 𝒯-measurable;
    - ∫ f d(μ × ν) = ∫_X ∫_Y f dν dμ = ∫_Y ∫_X f dμ dν, in [0, ∞].
  - Proof by the ladder:
    - indicators: `def-product-measure`, `lem-section-measure-measurable` and `thm-product-measure` (iii);
    - simple functions: linearity;
    - general f: an increasing sequence of simple sₖ ↑ f (MT.3). Use MCT on the inner integral for each x, then MCT on the outer integral and on the product.
  - "*Before reading on: where is σ-finiteness used?*" Answer in the proof: twice, in the lemma and in (iii).
- **`thm-fubini`** (key proof 3).
  - Statement: with μ and ν σ-finite and f ∈ L¹(μ × ν), real or complex:
    - f_x ∈ L¹(ν) for μ-a.e. x;
    - the function g(x) = ∫ f_x dν, defined off the null set N = {x : ∫|f_x| dν = ∞} and set to 0 on N, is in L¹(μ);
    - symmetrically in y;
    - all three integrals agree.
  - Proof:
    - N ∈ 𝒮 and μ(N) = 0, by Tonelli applied to |f|;
    - split f into (Re f)^±, (Im f)^±;
    - apply Tonelli to each piece and subtract on X ∖ N, where every piece is finite.
  - "*Self-explain why N is measurable.*"
- **Scratch Work (first appearance of "Tonelli first, then Fubini").** The worked example `exm-sine-integral`: ∫₀^∞ (sin x)/x dx = π/2.
  - (sin x)/x ∉ L¹(0, ∞), so truncate to [0, R] and insert 1/x = ∫₀^∞ e^{−xt} dt.
  - Tonelli check: ∫₀^R ∫₀^∞ e^{−xt}|sin x| dt dx = ∫₀^R |sin x|/x dx ≤ R.
  - Swap by Fubini. The inner integral is (1 − e^{−Rt}(t sin R + cos R))/(1 + t²).
  - Let R → ∞ by DCT, with dominator 2/(1 + t²) for R ≥ 1, using (1 + t)e^{−t} ≤ 1.
  - The Scratch Work shows the three steps as a recipe. The proof-register write-up follows it, apart from it.
- **`cor-tonelli-series`.** For gₖ ≥ 0, ∫ Σ gₖ dμ = Σ ∫ gₖ dμ: Tonelli with counting measure on ℕ. Remark: this was already known from MCT in MT.3, so it is a consistency check, not a new result.
- **`cor-fubini-series`.** If Σ_{m,n} |a_{mn}| < ∞, the two iterated sums agree. This is Fubini for counting × counting. Prose revisit of AN2.12's double-series theorem, which is now a special case.
- **Warning.** The hypothesis of Fubini is ∫|f| d(μ × ν) < ∞. The existence of both iterated integrals is *not* enough: see the hook, and C2 below.
- **Quick check.** If g ∈ L¹(μ) and h ∈ L¹(ν), is g(x)h(y) in L¹(μ × ν)? Answer: yes, with norm ‖g‖₁‖h‖₁, by Tonelli and §1 A2.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1 (proof comprehension, `thm-tonelli`).**
    - (a) Why does the formula hold for simple f? Answer: linearity of all three integrals.
    - (b) Why are two applications of MCT needed? Answer: inner integrals increase for each x, then the outer integrals.
    - (c) Run the theorem on χ_{y<x} on [0,1]². Answer: ½ both ways.
  - **A2 (proof comprehension, `thm-fubini`).**
    - (a) Why is N μ-null? Answer: ∫_X ∫_Y |f| dν dμ < ∞.
    - (b) What goes wrong if g is defined on all of X? Answer: ∞ − ∞ on N.
  - **A3.** Compute ∫∫_{[0,1]×[0,2]} xy² in both orders. Answer: 4/3.
  - **B1.** For 0 < a < b, show that ∫₀^∞ (e^{−ax} − e^{−bx})/x dx = log(b/a). Answer: the integrand equals ∫_a^b e^{−xt} dt; it is nonnegative, so Tonelli applies with no check.
  - **B2 (faded, last step blank).** Show that ∫₀^∞ e^{−x}(sin x)/x dx = π/4.
    - Given: the insertion of 1/x = ∫₀^∞ e^{−xt} dt, and the Tonelli check ∫∫ e^{−x(1+t)}|sin x| ≤ ∫₀^∞ e^{−x} dx = 1, using |sin x| ≤ x.
    - "*Name the move, then complete it.*"
    - Answer: swap; the inner integral is 1/((1 + t)² + 1); integrating gives π/2 − arctan 1 = π/4.
  - **B3.** Show that Σ_{n≥2} (ζ(n) − 1) = 1. Answer: Tonelli on counting × counting gives Σ_{k≥2} Σ_{n≥2} k⁻ⁿ = Σ_{k≥2} 1/(k(k − 1)) = 1.
  - **C1 (exit check 3).** For f, g ∈ L¹[0,1], let F(x) = ∫₀ˣ f and G(x) = ∫₀ˣ g. Prove that ∫₀¹ F g = F(1)G(1) − ∫₀¹ f G.
    - Answer:
      - ∫₀¹ F g is the integral of f(x)g(y) over {x < y};
      - ∫₀¹ f G is the integral over {y < x};
      - the diagonal is null;
      - f(x)g(y) ∈ L¹ by Tonelli.
    - Marking scheme outline (10): 2 for measurability of f(x)g(y); 2 for integrability via Tonelli; 3 for the two region identities; 2 for the null diagonal; 1 for the conclusion. Fatal error: swapping without checking integrability.
    - MT.10 cites this for the integration by parts of absolutely continuous functions.
  - **C2.** On [−1,1]², let f(x, y) = xy/(x² + y²)², with f(0, 0) = 0. Show that both iterated integrals equal 0 but ∫|f| = ∞. Answer: each inner integral is odd in its variable. For |f|, Tonelli on the quarter square {x, y ≥ 0} gives inner integral ∫₀¹ xy/(x² + y²)² dy = 1/(2x) − x/(2(x² + 1)), which is not integrable near x = 0. Moral: equal iterated integrals do not prove integrability.

## 06-lebesgue-measure-as-product.md — Lebesgue measure on ℝᵐ⁺ⁿ and the complete Fubini theorem (≈4,000 words)

- **Opening frame.** MT.1 built m_d on ℝᵈ directly, in the manner of Stein–Shakarchi (spec/08 D4 keeps "ℝᵈ concretely first"). Now compare m_{m+n} with m_m × m_n.
- **`thm-lebesgue-product`.**
  - Statement:
    - (i) m_{m+n} = m_m × m_n on ℬ(ℝ^{m+n}) = ℬ(ℝᵐ) ⊗ ℬ(ℝⁿ).
    - (ii) The Lebesgue σ-algebra of ℝ^{m+n} with m_{m+n} is the completion of (ℬ ⊗ ℬ, m_m × m_n), and also of (ℒ_m ⊗ ℒ_n, m_m × m_n).
  - Proof:
    - (i): both measures agree on half-open boxes, whose finite disjoint unions form an algebra generating the Borel sets (`prp-borel-product`). Apply `lem-uniqueness-on-algebra` with Zₖ = [−k, k)^{m+n}.
    - (ii): MT.1's regularity: each Lebesgue set is a Borel set up to a null set. Also ℬ ⊗ ℬ ⊆ ℒ_m ⊗ ℒ_n ⊆ ℒ_{m+n}, and m_{m+n} restricts correctly to ℒ_m ⊗ ℒ_n by the same uniqueness lemma on that σ-algebra.
- **`exm-product-not-complete`** (failure motivating the complete version).
  - For A ⊂ ℝ non-measurable (MT.1), {0} × A is m₂-null, hence Lebesgue measurable.
  - It is not in ℒ ⊗ ℒ, because its section at 0 is A (`thm-sections-measurable`).
  - So m₁ × m₁ on ℒ ⊗ ℒ is not complete, and Fubini as stated in §4 does not apply to every Lebesgue measurable f on ℝ².
- **`thm-fubini-complete`.**
  - Statement: let (X, 𝒮, μ) and (Y, 𝒯, ν) be complete and σ-finite, and λ the completion of μ × ν on (𝒮 ⊗ 𝒯)‾. Suppose f is (𝒮 ⊗ 𝒯)‾-measurable and either f ≥ 0 or f ∈ L¹(λ). Then:
    - for μ-a.e. x, f_x is 𝒯-measurable (and in L¹(ν) in the integrable case);
    - x ↦ ∫ f_x dν, defined a.e., is 𝒮-measurable (in L¹(μ) in the integrable case);
    - symmetrically in y;
    - the iterated integrals equal ∫ f dλ.
  - Proof route:
    - f = g λ-a.e. with g 𝒮 ⊗ 𝒯-measurable (MT.3's completion lemma).
    - The set {f ≠ g} lies in an M ∈ 𝒮 ⊗ 𝒯 with (μ × ν)(M) = 0.
    - Tonelli gives ν(M_x) = 0 for μ-a.e. x.
    - Off that set, f_x = g_x ν-a.e., so f_x is measurable by completeness of ν. Completeness of μ makes the a.e.-defined function of x measurable.
- **`cor-cavalieri`.** For E ⊆ ℝ^{m+n} Lebesgue measurable, E_x is Lebesgue measurable for a.e. x ∈ ℝᵐ, and m_{m+n}(E) = ∫ m_n(E_x) dx.
- **`cor-null-sections`.** A *measurable* E ⊆ ℝ^{m+n} is null if and only if m_n(E_x) = 0 for a.e. x. The hypothesis is needed; compare the Sierpiński remark of §5.
- **`thm-riemann-fubini-recovered`** (pays the AN3.4 hook).
  - Statement: let f be Riemann integrable on a box P × Q ⊂ ℝᵐ × ℝⁿ. Then for a.e. x ∈ P, f_x is Riemann integrable on Q, and ∫_{P×Q} f = ∫_P (∫_Q f_x) dx, with the outer integral in the Lebesgue sense.
  - Proof:
    - Lebesgue's criterion (HA.9 or MT.2 on boxes): the discontinuity set Δ of f is null.
    - By `cor-null-sections`, Δ_x is null for a.e. x.
    - f_x is continuous at every y ∉ Δ_x and bounded, so f_x is Riemann integrable.
    - Conclude with Riemann = Lebesgue (MT.2) and `thm-fubini-complete`.
  - Remark: AN3.4's statement, with upper and lower integrals, is recovered, and the Dirichlet slice is explained: it sits on the null set {½}.
- **Quick check.** For f = χ_{{½}×ℚ} on [0,1]², for which x is f_x Riemann integrable? Answer: every x ≠ ½.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Show that {0} × A ∈ ℒ(ℝ²) for every A ⊂ ℝ, and that it is in ℒ ⊗ ℒ if and only if A ∈ ℒ.
  - **A2.** Use `cor-cavalieri` to compute m₂ of the region under y = x² over [0,1]. Answer: ⅓.
  - **B1.** Show that the graph of a Lebesgue measurable f: ℝ → ℝ is m₂-null. Answer: the graph is in ℒ ⊗ ℬ (§1 B2 with 𝒮 = ℒ), and each section is a point.
  - **B2.** Prove `cor-null-sections`, and explain why measurability of E cannot be dropped. Answer: the Sierpiński remark, under CH.
  - **C1.** With C the Cantor set (AN1.4), show that χ_{C×ℚ} is Riemann integrable on [0,1]² while f_x fails to be Riemann integrable for uncountably many x. Answer: the discontinuity set is C × [0,1], which is null; for x ∈ C the slice is the Dirichlet function.
  - **C2 (completeness of μ is needed).** Let N ⊂ C be a non-Borel set (MT.1, by counting). With μ = ν = m restricted to ℬ, consider f = χ_{N×[0,1]}. Show that f is measurable for the completion of μ × ν, but x ↦ ∫ f_x dν = χ_N(x) is not Borel. Answer: N × [0,1] ⊂ C × [0,1], which is null.

## 07-applications.md — Slicing at work: graphs, distribution functions, convolution (≈4,500 words)

- **Opening frame.** Three uses that later chapters cite: the area under a graph, the layer-cake formula, and convolution.
- **`thm-area-under-graph`.**
  - Statement: let (X, 𝒮, μ) be σ-finite and f: X → [0, ∞] be 𝒮-measurable. Then G_f = {(x, t) : 0 ≤ t < f(x)} ∈ 𝒮 ⊗ ℬ([0, ∞)), and (μ × m)(G_f) = ∫ f dμ.
  - Proof: G_f = ⋃_{q∈ℚ₊} ({f > q} × [0, q)); then slice. This is the integral as an area, now for any σ-finite μ.
- **`thm-layer-cake`.**
  - Statement: for (X, 𝒮, μ) σ-finite, f ≥ 0 measurable and 0 < p < ∞, ∫ fᵖ dμ = p ∫₀^∞ t^{p−1} μ({f > t}) dt.
  - Proof by "insert an integral and swap": f(x)ᵖ = ∫₀^∞ p t^{p−1} χ_{t<f(x)} dt, by FTC and Riemann = Lebesgue. The set {t < f(x)} is G_f, so Tonelli applies.
  - Remark: σ-finiteness can be dropped (C1 below).
  - Worked example: f = |x|^{−1/2} on [−1,1] with p = 1. Then μ({f > t}) = 2 min(1, t⁻²), and both sides equal 4.
  - Hook paid later: MT.8 (weak Lᵖ) and MT.9 (the maximal function in Lᵖ).
- **`def-convolution`.** (f ∗ g)(x) = ∫ f(x − y)g(y) dy, wherever the integral converges absolutely.
- **`lem-convolution-measurable`.**
  - Statement: for f, g Lebesgue measurable on ℝᵈ, (x, y) ↦ f(x − y)g(y) is ℒ_{2d}-measurable.
  - **Scratch Work (first appearance of "measurability on the product needs an argument").** For Borel f the map is Borel, since (x, y) ↦ x − y is continuous.
  - Proof for Lebesgue f: replace f by a Borel f̃ with f = f̃ off a Borel null N (MT.1). Then {(x, y) : x − y ∈ N} is Borel, and its y-sections N + y are null by translation invariance (MT.1). So it is null by Tonelli, and f(x − y) = f̃(x − y) off a null set.
  - This avoids MT.5's linear change of variables.
- **`thm-convolution-l1`.** For f, g ∈ L¹(ℝᵈ):
  - y ↦ f(x − y)g(y) is integrable for a.e. x;
  - f ∗ g ∈ L¹;
  - ‖f ∗ g‖₁ ≤ ‖f‖₁‖g‖₁.
  - Proof: Tonelli first. ∫∫ |f(x − y)||g(y)| dx dy = ‖f‖₁‖g‖₁, using translation invariance of the integral (MT.2). Then Fubini.
  - Hook paid later: MT.7 (mollifiers, Young's inequality).
  - Remark: f ∗ g = g ∗ f uses reflection invariance (MT.1); see A2.
  - See-pointer, not a dependency: the Fourier transform turns convolution into a product, in HA.5 and HA.10 (side branch).
- **Quick check.** Is f ∗ g defined everywhere when f, g ∈ L¹? Answer: no, only a.e.; f = g = |x|^{−1/2}χ_{[−1,1]} has (f ∗ g)(0) = ∫ |y|⁻¹ = ∞.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Verify the worked layer-cake example.
  - **A2.** Prove f ∗ g = g ∗ f a.e. Answer: substitute y ↦ x − y for fixed x, using reflection and translation invariance of m.
  - **B1.** Show that ∫₀^∞ e^{−x²} dx = √π/2 without polar coordinates, using F(x, y) = y e^{−(1+x²)y²} on (0, ∞)². Answer: Tonelli in one order gives ∫₀^∞ dx/(2(1 + x²)) = π/4. In the other order, x = u/y gives I². So I² = π/4. MT.5 redoes this with polar coordinates.
  - **B2.** Show that χ_{[0,1]} ∗ χ_{[0,1]} is the tent function max(0, 1 − |x − 1|).
  - **C1.** Prove `thm-layer-cake` without σ-finiteness. Answer: if μ({f > t}) = ∞ for some t > 0, both sides are ∞. Otherwise {f > 0} = ⋃ {f > 1/k} is σ-finite; restrict μ to it.
  - **C2 (faded, last half blank).** Prove associativity: (f ∗ g) ∗ h = f ∗ (g ∗ h) a.e. for f, g, h ∈ L¹(ℝᵈ).
    - Given: the statement and the first line, "both sides are double integrals of f(x − y − z)g(y)h(z)".
    - "*Name the move, then complete it.*"
    - Answer: the Tonelli check gives a triple integral equal to ‖f‖₁‖g‖₁‖h‖₁; then Fubini and the substitution u = y + z for fixed z.
  - **C3 (exit check 1).** Prove `thm-tonelli` for σ-finite μ, ν, citing `lem-section-measure-measurable`.
