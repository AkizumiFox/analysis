**Signature moves** (in `index.md`, "Named moves"):
- **Check on a basis.** To show a set is open, or a map is continuous, it suffices to check basis (or subbasis) elements: every open set is a union of basis elements, and preimages commute with unions and finite intersections.
- **Test on coordinates.** A map into a product (or any initial topology) is continuous exactly when each coordinate is. The universal property replaces every hands-on check.
- **Finite subcover, then intersect.** To separate a point from a compact set (or build a tube), cover the compact set by opens chosen pointwise, take a finite subcover, and intersect the finitely many partner opens. This is the move of `thm-compact-in-hausdorff-closed` and `lem-tube`.
- **Compact to Hausdorff upgrade.** A continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism; used to identify quotients (the circle, the torus) without computing any inverse.
- **Kill sequences with uncountability.** To show a closure point is not a sequential limit, note that a sequence touches only countably many coordinates (or points), and pick one it misses.

## 1. Devices as applied

- **Hook (index; paid in §5).** "Close to f pointwise" means: within ε of f at finitely many chosen points. This is the closeness used throughout AN1.9 and AN2.5. The index asks: is it the closeness of some metric on the set of all functions [0, 1] → ℝ? It shows a set A of functions and a function 0 that A approaches pointwise in this sense, then observes that no sequence from A converges pointwise to 0 (the uncountability argument, stated informally). In a metric space a point approached by a set is a limit of a sequence from it (AN2.1). So pointwise closeness is not metric closeness, and the language of metrics cannot describe it. **Paid** in §5 by `exm-pointwise-closure-not-sequential` and `prp-product-not-first-countable`, with a back-reference to the index. Second, smaller hook paid in §1: AN2.1's last section noted that d and min(d, 1) give the same open sets and the same convergent sequences; the index says the open sets are what survives, and §1 makes them the definition.
- **Forward hooks planted (paid in later chapters, listed for `tools/promises.py`):** Tychonoff for arbitrary products → FA.5; nets as the replacement for sequences → FA.5; one-point compactification and the topological Urysohn lemma → AN2.8; second countability as a manifold axiom → AM.3.
- **Failure-motivated definitions** (each on the main line before its definition):
  - *topology* (§1): the metric open sets of d and of min(d, 1) agree although the metrics do not; the metric itself is not what continuity and convergence see.
  - *Hausdorff* (§3): in the cofinite topology a sequence of distinct points converges to every point at once; uniqueness of limits needs an axiom.
  - *first countable* (§3): in the cocountable topology on ℝ the closure of (0, 1) is ℝ, yet every convergent sequence is eventually constant; sequences can describe closure only when each point has a countable stock of neighbourhoods.
  - *product topology* (§5): the box topology, the "obvious" product, makes the diagonal map t ↦ (t, t, t, …) discontinuous although every coordinate is the identity; the product topology is the coarsest one making the coordinate maps continuous, and that is the one with the universal property.
  - *compactness by open covers* (§6): sequential compactness gives no information in the cocountable topology (no sequence of distinct points has a convergent subsequence, yet the space has other pathologies) and does not pass through the arguments that need finitely many open sets; the open-cover form is what the proofs of AN2.3 actually used.
  - *quotient topology* (§7): gluing the ends of [0, 1] should give the circle; the quotient set of F2.5 has no topology yet, and the finest topology making q continuous is the one for which maps out of X/∼ are exactly maps out of X constant on classes.
- **Scratch Work (three, halved):** at the first appearance of *check on a basis* (`thm-basis-generates`, §2), of *finite subcover, then intersect* (`thm-compact-in-hausdorff-closed`, §6), and of *kill sequences with uncountability* (`exm-pointwise-closure-not-sequential`, §5). ## 4. index.md

- **Story (the hook, §1 above).** Two short paragraphs. The set A ⊆ ℝ^[0,1] of functions equal to 1 except at finitely many points, where they are 0. Every "pointwise neighbourhood" of the zero function (finitely many points x₁, …, x_k and an ε) contains a member of A: take the function that is 0 exactly at x₁, …, x_k. But a sequence f₁, f₂, … in A has only countably many zeros altogether, and at any other point x every f_n(x) = 1. So no sequence from A converges pointwise to 0. In a metric space that cannot happen (AN2.1). The question "what kind of space is this?" is the chapter's; §5 answers it.
- **Why now.** AN2.5 and AN2.6 worked in C(K) with the sup metric, which is metric. The next layer (AN2.8's locally compact spaces for C₀(X) and Riesz; FA.5's weak and weak* topologies) is not metric. One sentence: this chapter is segment B, read it before AN2.8 or FA.
- **What you need:** AN2.1 and AN2.3 (metric topology, compactness, connectedness); F2.5 (equivalence relations and quotient sets); pointwise convergence from AN1.9.
- **Time:** as §2.
- **Roadmap:** §1–§2 axioms and ways to specify topologies; §3 what sequences see; §4 continuity and subspaces; §5 products (hook paid); §6 compactness; §7 quotients and connectedness.

## 5. Per-section plans

### 5.1 `01-topologies.md` — Open Sets Without a Metric

- **Opening (failure motivating `def-topology`).** Recall AN2.1's preview: on any metric space (X, d), the metric e = min(d, 1) has the same open sets, the same convergent sequences and the same continuous maps, although e is bounded and d need not be. Statements about convergence and continuity in AN2.1 were proved from three properties of the family of open sets (AN2.1: ∅ and X open, arbitrary unions, finite intersections). Make those the axioms.
- `def-topology`: a family 𝒯 ⊆ 𝒫(X) containing ∅ and X, closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections. `def-open-closed-topological`: open = member of 𝒯; closed = complement open. Remark: closed sets satisfy the dual axioms (De Morgan, F2.1), and "open" and "closed" are not opposites (a set can be both, or neither); ∅ and X are clopen in every space.
- `thm-metric-topology`: the open sets of a metric space form a topology 𝒯_d. Proof: one line, cite AN2.1's union/intersection result.
- `def-metrizable`: (X, 𝒯) is metrizable if 𝒯 = 𝒯_d for some metric d.
- `prp-equivalent-metrics-topology`: if there are constants with c·d ≤ d′ ≤ C·d, or more generally if every d-ball about each point contains a d′-ball about it and conversely, then 𝒯_d = 𝒯_{d′}. Corollary in prose: d and min(d, 1) give the same topology (the second condition with radius < 1); d₁, d₂, d_∞ on ℝⁿ give the same topology. **Hook paid** (the small one): the open sets are the invariant.
- `exm-basic-topologies`, four items, each with the axioms checked in one or two lines:
  1. discrete 𝒫(X) (metrizable by the discrete metric of AN2.1);
  2. indiscrete {∅, X};
  3. cofinite on an infinite X: ∅ and sets with finite complement;
  4. cocountable on an uncountable X: ∅ and sets with countable complement (finite intersections: a finite union of countable sets is countable; F2.3).
- `def-finer-coarser`: 𝒯₁ ⊆ 𝒯₂ means 𝒯₂ is finer. On ℝ: indiscrete ⊆ cofinite, cofinite ⊆ usual and cofinite ⊆ cocountable; usual and cocountable are **not comparable** ((0, 1) is usual-open, not cocountable-open; ℝ ∖ ℚ is cocountable-open, not usual-open). State and prove this as a worked example (`exm-basic-topologies` (5) is a sub-item, not a separate label).
- **Indiscrete is not metrizable** (|X| ≥ 2): a metric separates two points by disjoint balls. One-sentence argument here; the general principle is `prp-metric-hausdorff` in §3, forward pointer within the chapter is allowed only as "see §3" prose (no proof cites it here).
- **Quick check.** On X = {a, b, c}: is {∅, {a}, {b}, X} a topology? (No: {a} ∪ {b} is missing.) Is {∅, {a}, {a, b}, X}? (Yes.)
- **Warning.** "Closed" does not mean "not open". In the discrete topology every set is both.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** List all topologies on a two-point set {p, q}. *Answer:* four: indiscrete, discrete, {∅, {p}, X}, {∅, {q}, X}.
  - **A2.** In the cofinite topology on ℕ, is {even numbers} open? closed? *Answer:* neither: its complement (the odds) is infinite, so it is not open; it is infinite and not all of ℕ, so it is not closed.
  - **B1.** Show the intersection of any family of topologies on X is a topology; show the union of two need not be (on {a, b, c}: {∅, {a}, X} ∪ {∅, {b}, X} misses {a, b}).
  - **B2.** Let d(x, y) = |x − y| and d′(x, y) = |x³ − y³| on ℝ. Show 𝒯_d = 𝒯_{d′} although no constant C gives d ≤ C·d′ on all of ℝ. *Answer:* x ↦ x³ is a homeomorphism of (ℝ, usual), so the d′-open sets are the images of usual open sets under the inverse, which are usual open; no C: near 0, |x³ − 0| = |x|³ is much smaller than |x|.
  - **B3.** On ℝ, let 𝒯 consist of ∅, ℝ and all rays (a, ∞), a ∈ ℝ. Decide whether 𝒯 is a topology, and whether the same holds for ∅, ℝ and all rays [a, ∞). *Answer:* (a, ∞): yes; a union of rays (a, ∞) over a ∈ S is (inf S, ∞) if S is bounded below (whether or not the infimum is attained) and ℝ otherwise; (a, ∞) ∩ (b, ∞) = (max(a, b), ∞). [a, ∞): no; the union of [1/n, ∞) over n ≥ 1 is (0, ∞), which is not of the form [a, ∞). (S2a item 1.)
  - **C1.** Show the cofinite topology on an infinite set is not metrizable. (Exit check 1; see §6 of this file.)

### 5.2 `02-bases-and-closure.md` — Bases, Neighbourhoods, and Closure

- **Opening.** Metric open sets are unions of balls; nobody lists all open subsets of ℝ. A topology is usually given by a smaller family.
- `def-basis`: ℬ ⊆ 𝒫(X) with (B1) ⋃ℬ = X; (B2) if x ∈ B₁ ∩ B₂ then some B₃ ∈ ℬ has x ∈ B₃ ⊆ B₁ ∩ B₂.
- `thm-basis-generates`: the sets U such that every x ∈ U has some B ∈ ℬ with x ∈ B ⊆ U form a topology 𝒯_ℬ, and 𝒯_ℬ is exactly the set of unions of members of ℬ.
  - **[Scratch work]** (first appearance of *check on a basis*): Givens/Goal table: givens (B1), (B2); goal the three axioms; the gap is the finite intersection, where (B2) is used once per point. Then the proof, kept apart.
- `prp-basis-for-given-topology`: if 𝒞 ⊆ 𝒯 and every open U and x ∈ U have C ∈ 𝒞 with x ∈ C ⊆ U, then 𝒞 is a basis and 𝒯_𝒞 = 𝒯. Example: open balls of rational radius about every point; for ℝⁿ, balls with rational centre and rational radius (a countable basis, used in §3 for second countability).
- `def-subbasis`: any 𝒮 with ⋃𝒮 = X; finite intersections of members of 𝒮 form a basis; the topology generated is the coarsest containing 𝒮. Example: rays (a, ∞) and (−∞, b) form a subbasis for ℝ. (Subbases are what §5 needs.)
- `def-neighbourhood`: N is a neighbourhood of x if x ∈ U ⊆ N for some open U. Remark: openness is local (U is open iff it is a neighbourhood of each of its points).
- `exm-sorgenfrey-line`: ℬ = {[a, b) : a < b}; check (B1), (B2). ℝ_ℓ is finer than the usual topology ((a, b) = ⋃[a + 1/n, b)), strictly ([0, 1) is not usual-open). [a, b) is clopen in ℝ_ℓ. Recorded in the table; second countability is §3.
- `def-closure-interior-topological`: int A = union of open subsets of A; cl A = intersection of closed supersets; ∂A = cl A ∖ int A. Note cl(X ∖ A) = X ∖ int A (one line).
- `def-limit-point-topological`: x is a limit point of A if every neighbourhood of x meets A ∖ {x}. A′ the set of limit points.
- `thm-closure-limit-points`: cl A = A ∪ A′; and x ∈ cl A iff every neighbourhood (equivalently every basic neighbourhood) of x meets A. The second form is `prp-closure-basis`; state them as two labels (the theorem, and the basic-neighbourhood test used repeatedly).
  - Proof route: x ∉ cl A iff x lies in an open set missing A (complement of a closed superset), which gives both. *Idea* level for the basis form.

### 5.3 `03-sequences-and-hausdorff.md` — What Sequences Can and Cannot See

- `def-convergence-topological`: x_n → x if every neighbourhood of x contains x_n for all large n.
- `exm-cofinite-sequences` (failure motivating Hausdorff): in the cofinite topology on an infinite X, a sequence of distinct points converges to **every** point. Proof: a neighbourhood misses finitely many points, hence finitely many terms. So limits are not unique.
- `def-hausdorff`: distinct points have disjoint neighbourhoods.
- `thm-hausdorff-unique-limits`: in a Hausdorff space a sequence has at most one limit. Proof in three lines.
- `prp-metric-hausdorff`: metric spaces are Hausdorff (balls of radius d(x, y)/2). Corollary: the indiscrete and cofinite topologies are not metrizable. **This settles exit check 1** in principle; the exit check asks for it written in full.
- **Weird example (converse fails):** in the cocountable topology on ℝ, convergent sequences are eventually constant (`exm-cocountable-sequences` (a)): if x_n → p, then U = ℝ ∖ {x_n : x_n ≠ p} is open and contains p, so x_n = p for large n. Hence limits are unique; but the space is not Hausdorff (two nonempty open sets have countable complements, so they meet). Unique limits do not imply Hausdorff.
- `exm-cocountable-sequences` (b) (failure motivating first countability): cl (0, 1) = ℝ but 2 is the limit of no sequence in (0, 1). And (c): the identity (ℝ, cocountable) → (ℝ, usual) carries convergent sequences to convergent sequences but is not continuous ((0, 1) has non-open preimage). Sequential continuity is weaker than continuity; this is noted again in §4.
- `def-first-countable`: each point has a countable neighbourhood base. `def-second-countable`: the topology has a countable basis. Second countable ⇒ first countable (one line). Metric spaces are first countable (balls of radius 1/n); ℝⁿ is second countable (§2's rational balls).
- `thm-first-countable-sequential-closure`: in a first-countable space, x ∈ cl A iff some sequence in A converges to x; f is continuous at x iff sequentially continuous at x (the second clause may be deferred to §4 as a corollary there; decide at S2, default: §4 `thm-continuity-equivalents` (v)).
  - Proof route: replace the countable base by a decreasing one U₁ ⊇ U₂ ⊇ … (finite intersections) and pick x_n ∈ U_n ∩ A. *Idea* level; the decreasing replacement is the step to stress.
- **ℝ_ℓ is first countable but not second countable** (worked example): {[x, x + 1/n)} is a countable base at x; if ℬ is any basis, choose for each x some B_x ∈ ℬ with x ∈ B_x ⊆ [x, x + 1); then x = min B_x, so x ↦ B_x is injective and ℬ is uncountable (F2.3).
- `def-net` (brief, one paragraph, no theory): a directed set (D, ≼) and a map D → X; convergence as for sequences with "for all n ≥ N" replaced by "for all d ≽ d₀". `prp-closure-nets`: x ∈ cl A iff some net in A converges to x (index by the neighbourhoods of x ordered by reverse inclusion; uses one choice per neighbourhood, stated). One worked line: the hook's set A and the net indexed by finite subsets of [0, 1] ordered by inclusion. Forward pointer in prose: FA.5 uses nets for weak convergence.
- **Quick check.** In the discrete topology, which sequences converge? (Eventually constant ones.) Is the discrete topology on ℝ second countable? (No: each singleton must be a basis element.)
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** In the indiscrete topology, to which points does a sequence converge? *Answer:* every sequence converges to every point.
  - **A2.** Give the two neighbourhoods required by `def-hausdorff` for x = 1, y = 1.2 in ℝ_ℓ. *Answer:* e.g. [1, 1.1) and [1.2, 1.3).
  - **B1.** Show that every subspace of a second-countable space is second countable (the traces B ∩ Y of a countable basis form a basis of the subspace) and that a second-countable space has a countable dense subset (one point from each nonempty basis element). Then show ℝ_ℓ has a countable dense subset (ℚ) although it is not second countable: separable does not imply second countable.
  - **B2.** Show that in the cofinite topology on ℝ, closure **is** given by sequences, although the space is not first countable. *Answer:* closure of a finite set is itself; of an infinite set is ℝ, and an infinite set contains a sequence of distinct points, which converges to every point. Not first countable: if U₁, U₂, … were a base at x, the countably many finite complements have countable union C; pick y ∉ C ∪ {x}; then ℝ ∖ {y} contains no U_n. So first countability is sufficient for sequential closure, not necessary.
  - **B3.** Show a finite Hausdorff space is discrete.
  - **C1.** (Find the error, true conclusion; **exit check 3**.) "Claim: in a first-countable space, if x ∈ cl A then a sequence in A converges to x. Proof: let U₁, U₂, … be a countable base at x. ### 5.4 `04-continuity-and-subspaces.md` — Continuous Maps and Subspaces

- `def-continuous-topological`: f : X → Y is continuous if preimages of open sets are open. Remark: this was a theorem in AN2.1; here it is the definition, and the ε–δ form is the metric special case.
- `thm-continuity-equivalents`: for f : X → Y, equivalent: (i) preimages of open sets open; (ii) preimages of closed sets closed; (iii) f(cl A) ⊆ cl f(A) for all A; (iv) preimages of a subbasis of Y open (*check on a basis*, Idea level); (v) for each x and neighbourhood N of f(x), some neighbourhood M of x has f(M) ⊆ N. Plus: continuous ⇒ sequentially continuous; the converse holds when X is first countable (`thm-first-countable-sequential-closure`); `exm-cocountable-sequences` (c) shows it fails in general.
- Compositions of continuous maps are continuous (one line).
- `def-homeomorphism-topological`: continuous bijection with continuous inverse; a homeomorphism is a bijection of the topologies.
- `exm-identity-not-homeomorphism`: id : (X, 𝒯₂) → (X, 𝒯₁) is continuous iff 𝒯₁ ⊆ 𝒯₂; so id : ℝ_ℓ → ℝ is a continuous bijection that is not a homeomorphism. (The circle example of AN2.3 is recalled; §6 explains it.)
- `def-subspace-topology`: for Y ⊆ X, 𝒯_Y = {U ∩ Y : U ∈ 𝒯}. Check the axioms (images under V ↦ V ∩ Y commute with unions and intersections).
- `prp-metric-subspace-topology`: for a metric space, the subspace topology on Y is the topology of the restricted metric (AN2.1's relative-openness result, restated). So no conflict of meaning.
- `thm-subspace-universal`: g : Z → Y is continuous iff i ∘ g : Z → X is continuous, where i is the inclusion; and 𝒯_Y is the only topology on Y with this property for all Z (proof of uniqueness: apply it with Z = (Y, 𝒯′) and g = id both ways). The subspace topology is the coarsest making i continuous; foreshadow `def-initial-topology` in §5.
- **Closed in a subspace:** closed subsets of Y are F ∩ Y, F closed in X; if Y is open (closed) in X, open (closed) subsets of Y are open (closed) in X. One worked example: [0, 1/2) is open in [0, 1] and not in ℝ; [0, 1/2] is closed in both.
- `lem-pasting`: if X = A ∪ B with A, B both closed (or both open) and f|_A, f|_B continuous, then f is continuous. Terse proof via (ii). Counterexample when A is closed and B is not: f = 0 on (−∞, 0], 1 on (0, ∞).
- **Quick check.** Is f : ℝ_ℓ → ℝ, f = indicator of [0, ∞), continuous? (Yes: preimages are ∅, [0, ∞), (−∞, 0), ℝ, each ℝ_ℓ-open.)
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Which maps from (X, discrete) are continuous? into (Y, indiscrete)? *Answer:* all; all.
  - **A2.** Show that f : (ℝ, cofinite) → (ℝ, cofinite) is continuous whenever every fibre f⁻¹(y) is finite, and that every injective map is continuous in the cofinite topology. *Answer:* closed sets are finite sets and ℝ; preimage of a finite set is a finite union of finite fibres.

Decide which of f(x) = ⌊x⌋ and g(x) = 1/x (x ≠ 0), g(0) = 0, is continuous into the cofinite topology. *Answer:* closed sets of the target are ℝ and finite sets, whose preimages are ℝ and finite unions of fibres. ⌊·⌋: no (its fibres [n, n + 1) are not closed). g: yes (every fibre is a single point), although g is not continuous into the usual topology.   - **B2.** Prove: if f, g : X → Y are continuous and Y is Hausdorff, then {x : f(x) = g(x)} is closed. Deduce that two continuous maps into a Hausdorff space that agree on a dense set agree everywhere. Show Hausdorff is needed (Y indiscrete on two points).

### 5.5 `05-products.md` — Products and Initial Topologies

- **Finite products first.** On X × Y the sets U × V (U, V open) form a basis; the product topology. For metric spaces, it is the topology of the product metric (AN2.1's product metric; one line by `prp-basis-for-given-topology`).
- **Failure (`exm-box-topology-fails`).** For an infinite product ∏ X_α the "obvious" basis is all products ∏ U_α of open sets: the box topology. On ℝ^ℕ, the diagonal Δ(t) = (t, t, t, …) has every coordinate equal to the identity, yet Δ⁻¹(∏ (−1/n, 1/n)) = {0}, not open. A topology on a product in which "continuous into the product" does not mean "continuous coordinatewise" is not the one wanted.
- `def-initial-topology`: given maps f_α : X → Y_α, the coarsest topology on X making each f_α continuous; subbasis {f_α⁻¹(V) : V open in Y_α}. The subspace topology is the case of one map (the inclusion). FA.5's weak topology is the case of the functionals; say so in prose.
- `def-product-topology`: the initial topology for the projections π_α : ∏ X_α → X_α. Basic open sets: ∏ U_α with U_α open and U_α = X_α for all but finitely many α. For finite products it agrees with the finite construction above.
- `thm-product-universal`: f : Z → ∏ X_α is continuous iff each π_α ∘ f is continuous; more generally for any initial topology. **Key proof** (comprehension part 5 A2). Proof route: ⇒ composition; ⇐ *check on a subbasis*: f⁻¹(π_α⁻¹(V)) = (π_α ∘ f)⁻¹(V). Idea level. Also: the product topology is the only topology on ∏ X_α with this property (same argument as `thm-subspace-universal`; exercise 5 B1).
- `thm-product-pointwise`: in Y^I = ∏_{i∈I} Y (Y a topological space), a sequence (or net) f_n converges to f iff f_n(i) → f(i) for every i. For Y = ℝ and I = [0, 1]: the product topology on ℝ^[0,1] is the topology of pointwise convergence of AN1.9. Proof: basic neighbourhoods of f constrain finitely many coordinates.
- `exm-pointwise-closure-not-sequential` (**hook paid**): A = functions equal to 1 except at finitely many points where they are 0. The zero function is in cl A (`prp-closure-basis`: a basic neighbourhood of 0 constrains finitely many points x₁, …, x_k; the member of A vanishing exactly there lies in it). No sequence in A converges to 0 (`thm-product-pointwise`; F2.3: the zero sets of f₁, f₂, … have countable union; pick x ∈ [0, 1] outside it).
  - **[Scratch work]** (first appearance of *kill sequences with uncountability*): Givens/Goal; the gap row is "find one coordinate where every f_n is far from 0"; the countable union is what closes it.
  - Close the hook: by `thm-first-countable-sequential-closure`, ℝ^[0,1] is not first countable, hence not metrizable. Back-reference to the index.
- `prp-product-not-first-countable`: direct proof (Idea level, *kill sequences with uncountability* again): if U₁, U₂, … were a base at 0, each U_n contains a basic set constraining a finite set F_n of points; pick x ∉ ⋃F_n; the neighbourhood {g : |g(x)| < 1} contains no U_n.
- `thm-countable-product-metrizable`: if (X_n, d_n), n ≥ 1, are metric spaces, then D(x, y) = Σ 2^{−n} min(d_n(x_n, y_n), 1) is a metric on ∏ X_n whose topology is the product topology. Proof route: a D-ball of radius r about x contains the basic set constraining coordinates 1, …, N to within r/2 each, where 2^{−N} < r/2; conversely a basic set constraining coordinates 1, …, N to within ε contains the D-ball of radius 2^{−N} min(ε, 1). Full proof; ℝ^ℕ with pointwise convergence is metrizable. Contrast in one sentence with the hook: countably many coordinates is what makes the difference.
- `prp-product-hausdorff`: a product of Hausdorff spaces is Hausdorff (separate in one coordinate). Subspaces of Hausdorff spaces are Hausdorff (in the exercises).

### 5.6 `06-compactness.md` — Compactness

- **Opening (failure motivating the open-cover definition).** AN2.3 showed three conditions equivalent for metric spaces. In the cocountable topology on ℝ, convergent sequences are eventually constant (`exm-cocountable-sequences`), so a sequence of distinct points has no convergent subsequence: sequential compactness fails, and it fails for a reason (sequences are blind) that has nothing to do with the finiteness arguments AN2.3 used for extreme values and uniform continuity. Those arguments used finite subcovers; take that as the definition.
- `def-compact-topological`: every open cover has a finite subcover; K ⊆ X is compact if it is compact in the subspace topology, equivalently every cover by open sets of X has a finite subcover (one line).
- `thm-compact-fip`: X is compact iff every family of closed sets with the finite intersection property has nonempty intersection. Terse proof (De Morgan). Forward pointer in prose: FA.5's Tychonoff proof uses this form.
- **Examples.** Cofinite topology: every subspace is compact (one set of a cover leaves finitely many points). Indiscrete: compact. Cocountable on ℝ: not compact (take distinct x₁, x₂, … and U_n = ℝ ∖ {x_m : m ≥ n}; these are open, increasing, cover ℝ, and a finite subfamily lies in some U_N, which misses x_N). [0, 1] in ℝ_ℓ: not compact ({[0, 1 − 1/n)} ∪ {[1, 2)}).
- `prp-closed-in-compact`: a closed subset of a compact space is compact.
- `thm-compact-in-hausdorff-closed`: a compact subset K of a Hausdorff space X is closed. **Key proof** (comprehension 6 A2).
  - **[Scratch work]** (first appearance of *finite subcover, then intersect*): Givens/Goal: x ∉ K; want an open set around x missing K. For each y ∈ K, Hausdorff gives U_y ∋ y, V_y ∋ x disjoint. Gap row: "infinitely many V_y; their intersection need not be open". Compactness reduces to finitely many.
  - Proof kept apart. Then: the hypothesis is needed (in the cofinite topology on ℕ, the compact set {even numbers} is not closed).
- `thm-compact-image`: continuous images of compact spaces are compact. Corollary: a continuous real function on a compact space attains its bounds (one line, via compact subsets of ℝ being closed and bounded; AN1.4).
- `lem-tube`: if Y is compact and N is an open subset of X × Y containing {x} × Y, then some open W ∋ x has W × Y ⊆ N. *Idea* level (the move named, the finite subcover of basic boxes, intersect the finitely many first factors). Counterexample when Y is not compact: N = {(x, y) : |xy| < 1} ⊆ ℝ × ℝ contains {0} × ℝ and no tube.
- `thm-finite-product-compact`: X × Y compact if both are; by induction, finite products. Terse.
- **Tychonoff stated** (no label; prose): an arbitrary product of compact spaces is compact. The proof needs Zorn's lemma or an equivalent (ST.4 records the equivalence with choice) and is in FA.5. The countable case is within reach now:
- `thm-hilbert-cube-compact`: a countable product of compact metric spaces is compact; in particular [0, 1]^ℕ. Proof route: metrizable by `thm-countable-product-metrizable`, so compact ⇔ sequentially compact (AN2.3); given a sequence, extract nested subsequences converging in coordinate 1, 2, …, take the diagonal (named: the diagonal argument, already used in AN2.5's Arzelà–Ascoli; cite it as the same move); `thm-product-pointwise` gives convergence. Full proof.
- `thm-compact-hausdorff-homeomorphism`: a continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism. **Key proof** (comprehension 6 A3). *Try it first (ten minutes): what must be shown about f(F) for F closed?* Then Idea from the obstacle (one cannot compute f⁻¹), proof by `prp-closed-in-compact`, `thm-compact-image`, `thm-compact-in-hausdorff-closed`. This generalizes AN2.3's metric version (recall it).
- `exm-circle-bijection`: t ↦ (cos 2πt, sin 2πt) from [0, 1) to S¹ is a continuous bijection, not a homeomorphism (the image of [0, 1/2) is not open in S¹, or: the inverse is discontinuous at (1, 0)). The domain is not compact. And id : ([0, 1], usual) → ([0, 1], indiscrete) is a continuous bijection, not a homeomorphism: the codomain is not Hausdorff. Both hypotheses are needed.
- **Compactness and sequential compactness in general.** Remark, no proof: they coincide for metrizable spaces (AN2.3), and in general neither implies the other; the standard examples ([0, 1]^[0,1] is compact by Tychonoff but not sequentially compact; the space of countable ordinals is sequentially compact but not compact) need Tychonoff or ordinals and are left to FA.5 and the literature. The cocountable topology shows "neither" is possible.

  - **B3.** Show that disjoint compact sets in a Hausdorff space have disjoint neighbourhoods (*finite subcover, then intersect*, twice; terse).
  - **C1.** (**Exit check 2.**) Prove `thm-compact-hausdorff-homeomorphism` and show by examples that neither hypothesis can be dropped. *Hint 1:* closed sets map to closed sets. *Hint 2:* compact in, compact out, closed in Hausdorff.
  - **C2.** Show that a compact Hausdorff topology is maximal among compact topologies and minimal among Hausdorff topologies on the same set: if 𝒯 is compact Hausdorff and 𝒯′ ⊊ 𝒯, then 𝒯′ is not Hausdorff; if 𝒯 ⊊ 𝒯′, then 𝒯′ is not compact. *Hint 1:* the identity map. *Hint 2:* `thm-compact-hausdorff-homeomorphism`. *Answer:* id : (X, 𝒯) → (X, 𝒯′) (first case) or (X, 𝒯′) → (X, 𝒯) (second) would be a continuous bijection from compact to Hausdorff, hence a homeomorphism, forcing equality.

### 5.7 `07-quotients-and-connectedness.md` — Quotients and Connectedness

- **Opening (failure motivating the quotient topology).** F2.5 built X/∼ and q : X → X/∼ for an equivalence relation. Glue 0 and 1 in [0, 1]: the set [0, 1]/∼ should be a circle, but it has no topology. Ask for the finest topology making q continuous.
- `def-quotient-topology`: V ⊆ X/∼ is open iff q⁻¹(V) is open in X. Check the axioms (preimages commute with unions and intersections). More generally for a surjection q : X → Y.
- `thm-quotient-universal`: g : X/∼ → Z is continuous iff g ∘ q is continuous; maps out of X/∼ are exactly the continuous maps out of X constant on classes (F2.5's well-definedness, `thm-classes-partition`). Terse proof.
- `prp-interval-mod-ends-circle`: [0, 1]/(0 ∼ 1) ≅ S¹. Proof: t ↦ (cos 2πt, sin 2πt) is constant on classes, so it induces a continuous bijection [0, 1]/∼ → S¹ (`thm-quotient-universal`); the domain is compact (`thm-compact-image` applied to q), S¹ is Hausdorff; `thm-compact-hausdorff-homeomorphism` (*compact to Hausdorff upgrade*). Contrast with `exm-circle-bijection`: the quotient repairs exactly what failed there.
- `exm-torus-two-ways`: [0, 1]²/∼ (opposite edges glued) ≅ S¹ × S¹, the same argument with `prp-product-hausdorff` and `thm-finite-product-compact`. One paragraph, with a figure (`.tikz` or SVG: the square with arrows). The Möbius band, projective plane and Klein bottle by gluing are mentioned in a fold (`::: {.fold}` "Other gluings"), not on the main line.
- `exm-line-two-origins`: X = ℝ × {0, 1}, (x, 0) ∼ (x, 1) for x ≠ 0. Every point has a neighbourhood homeomorphic to an open interval, yet the two origins have no disjoint neighbourhoods, and the sequence 1/n converges to both. Quotients of Hausdorff spaces need not be Hausdorff. Forward pointer: manifolds (AM.3) require Hausdorff for this reason.
- **Connectedness revisited.** `def-connected-topological`: X is not the union of two disjoint nonempty open sets (equivalently, no clopen sets but ∅ and X). Same definition as AN2.3, now for any space; the results of AN2.3 whose proofs used only open sets carry over (list them: continuous images, unions with a common point, closure of connected, connected subsets of ℝ are intervals, path-connected ⇒ connected).
- `thm-connected-image`: continuous images of connected spaces are connected. One line (cited from AN2.3's argument, re-run in the topological setting).
- `thm-finite-product-connected`: X × Y is connected if X and Y are. Proof: fix (a, b) ∈ X × Y. For each x ∈ X, X × {b} and {x} × Y are connected (homeomorphic to X and Y) and meet at (x, b), so T_x = (X × {b}) ∪ ({x} × Y) is connected; every T_x contains (a, b), and X × Y is the union of the T_x. Terse. Arbitrary products: exercise 7 C1.
- `def-component`: x ≈ y iff some connected subset contains both. `thm-components-partition`: ≈ is an equivalence relation; its classes (the components) are connected, closed, and partition X (`thm-classes-partition`). Transitivity uses the union of two connected sets with a common point (AN2.3). Examples: components of ℚ are singletons (totally disconnected); components of ℝ_ℓ are singletons (a subset containing a < b is split by the disjoint open sets (−∞, b) and [b, ∞)).
- **Quick check.** Is the line with two origins connected? (Yes: it is the union of two copies of ℝ, each connected, overlapping in ℝ ∖ {0}.) Is it path-connected? (Yes.)
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Describe the quotient of ℝ by x ∼ y ⇔ x − y ∈ ℤ, and show it is homeomorphic to S¹. *Answer:* t ↦ (cos 2πt, sin 2πt) induces a continuous bijection; it is a homeomorphism because q|[0, 1] is surjective, so ℝ/∼ is the image of the compact [0, 1] (compact) and S¹ is Hausdorff.
  - **A2.** In `exm-line-two-origins`, show both origins are limits of (1/n, 0) mapped to the quotient.
  - **B1.** Collapse [0, 1] ⊆ ℝ to a point. Show the quotient ℝ/[0, 1] is homeomorphic to ℝ. *Answer:* g(x) = x for x ≤ 0, 0 on [0, 1], x − 1 for x ≥ 1 is continuous, constant on classes, induces a continuous bijection; its inverse is continuous (check on intervals; or: g is a closed map). (S2a item 6.)
  - **B2.** Show the quotient of ℝ by x ∼ y ⇔ x − y ∈ ℚ has the indiscrete topology. *Answer:* if V is open and nonempty, q⁻¹(V) is open, nonempty and a union of classes; it contains an interval, which meets every class x + ℚ (each class is dense), so q⁻¹(V) contains every class and equals ℝ.
  - **B3.** Show a space is connected iff every continuous map to the discrete space {0, 1} is constant.
  - **C1.** Prove that an arbitrary product of connected spaces is connected. *Hint 1:* fix a point a; the points differing from a in finitely many coordinates form a connected set. *Hint 2:* that set is dense. *Answer:* finite-difference sets are unions of connected sets through a by `thm-finite-product-connected` and the common-point lemma; density by basic open sets constraining finitely many coordinates; closure of connected is connected.
  - **C2.** Show the components of a space need not be open (ℚ), but are open when there are finitely many.
- **R. Review** (STYLE §11.5; the problem texts name no source chapter, the solutions do):

Solution: AN2.3's proof by Lebesgue number; uniform continuity needs a metric (or a uniform structure), so it has no meaning for topological spaces.
  - **R4** (no hint of method): Let f : [0, 1] → ℝ have a closed graph in [0, 1] × ℝ and be bounded. Show f is continuous. Solution: the graph is closed and bounded in ℝ², hence compact (Heine–Borel); the projection to the first coordinate restricted to the graph is a continuous bijection from a compact space onto [0, 1], Hausdorff, hence a homeomorphism; f is its inverse composed with the second projection. Why look-alikes fail: sequences work too (AN1.6), but the compact-to-Hausdorff route avoids the subsequence argument; boundedness is needed (f(x) = 1/x, f(0) = 0).
