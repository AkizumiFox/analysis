**Purpose.**
- Convergence is the only "sum" the reader has met so far, and it says nothing about 1 − 1 + 1 − ⋯. Formal manipulation gives values, but it gives contradictory ones (1 + 2 + 4 + ⋯ = −1). The chapter replaces manipulation with **summation methods**: definite rules that agree with convergence wherever convergence applies.
- Two methods are developed: **Cesàro** (average the partial sums) and **Abel** (let x → 1⁻ in Σ aₙxⁿ). They form a strict chain, convergent ⇒ (C,1) ⇒ Abel, with the same value at every step.
- The question the reader is left with is the converse. When does summability give convergence back? Positivity is enough. So is a size condition on the terms: n aₙ → 0 (Tauber), and the much harder |n aₙ| ≤ C (Hardy for Cesàro, Littlewood for Abel).
- **Littlewood's theorem is proved in full**, using only polynomials. The polynomial brackets needed for the Karamata–Wielandt argument are built by hand from binomial tails (§05). This avoids the Weierstrass approximation theorem, which comes later, in AN2.6.

## Named moves (index.md)

1. **Split the average into head and tail** (`thm-cesaro-regular`). Fix N so the tail is uniformly small. The head is a fixed finite sum, and the normalizing factor kills it. Reused in `thm-toeplitz-sufficient`, `lem-product-average`, `thm-cesaro-implies-abel` and the AN1.10 Abel proof. This is the chapter's one new move, so it gets the faded triple.
2. **Tie the parameter to the index** (`thm-tauber`). Compare sₙ with f(x) at x = 1 − 1/(n + 1), so that the error terms and n balance.
3. **Delayed means** (`thm-hardy-tauberian`). Average the partial sums over a window n < k ≤ m with m − n proportional to n. The window is long enough for the Cesàro means to move by a controlled amount, and short enough that the O(1/n) condition keeps sₖ close to sₙ.
4. **Polynomial sandwich** (`thm-littlewood`). Write the target quantity as Σ aₙ g(xⁿ) with g a step function. Bracket g between polynomials whose Σ aₙ p(xⁿ) is known. The one-sided Tauberian condition turns the one-sided brackets into a two-sided estimate.

## Hooks and where they are paid

- **Chapter hook (index.md and §01): 1 − 1 + 1 − ⋯.** Two honest computations give 1/2. The averages of the partial sums 1, 0, 1, 0, … tend to 1/2, and 1/(1 + x) = Σ (−1)ⁿxⁿ tends to 1/2 as x → 1⁻. A careless manipulation also gives 1 + 2 + 4 + ⋯ = −1. Three questions follow: what makes a method trustworthy, do the two methods always agree, and when does a "sum" of this kind force honest convergence?
  - **Paid:** trustworthiness (regularity) in §01 (`thm-cesaro-regular`) and §03 (`thm-abel-regular`); agreement in §03 (`cor-summability-chain`); convergence in §04 and §05.
- **Section hook (§02, from AN2.12): the Cauchy product.** Mertens needs one of the two factors to converge absolutely, and the square of Σ (−1)ⁿ/√(n + 1) diverges. If the product series of two convergent series *does* converge, must its sum be AB?
  - **Paid** in §02 (`thm-cesaro-product`, `cor-cauchy-product-sum`): the product is always (C,1)-summable to AB, so it is AB whenever it converges. Paid again in §03 by Abel's route, and in §04 with the size condition (`cor-product-hardy`).
- **Section hook (§03 end → §05): the converse of Abel's theorem.** Grandi's series shows that an Abel limit does not force convergence, even when f extends continuously to 1. Even aₙ → 0 is not enough (the witness is built in §05).
  - **Paid:** Tauber in §04 (o(1/n)); Littlewood in §05 (O(1/n)), with `exm-sqrt-sine` showing that O(n^{−1/2}) is not enough.
- **Hooks opened here, paid later (prose only, by atlas id):**
  - HA.2: Fejér's theorem is Cesàro summability of Fourier series.
  - HA.3: Hardy's theorem with BV coefficients.
  - CA.1: what Σ aₙzⁿ does on the circle of convergence.

## index.md

- **Story.** 1 − 1 + 1 − ⋯ has no sum, yet two different natural rules both give it the value 1/2. The chapter makes such rules precise and proves that they never contradict convergence. Then it asks the reverse question, which turns out to be deep: what must be added to a "summable" series to make it convergent? The answer is a condition on the size of the terms. The best such condition, |n aₙ| ≤ C, needs a new idea, which is to approximate a step function by polynomials from above and below.

## 01-cesaro-summability.md — Averaging the partial sums (target 4,000 words)

- **Opening frame / hook.** Grandi's series.
  - The "rule" S = 1 − S gives 1/2. The same kind of rule applied to 1 + 2 + 4 + ⋯ gives S = 1 + 2S, so S = −1, which is absurd for a series of positive terms.
  - **Failure-motivated definition:** manipulation is not a definition. A summation method must be a rule that produces a number from the sequence (aₙ), and it is only trustworthy if it is
    - **regular** (it gives the usual sum to every convergent series), and
    - **linear**.
  - Say plainly that a method has to *earn* these properties: they are theorems.
- **Definition** `def-cesaro-summable`. σₙ = (s₀ + ⋯ + sₙ)/(n + 1). Σ aₙ is (C,1)-summable with sum s if σₙ → s. Give the symbols form (∀ε > 0 ∃N ∀n ≥ N |σₙ − s| < ε). Slogan: *average the partial sums, not the terms.*
- **`prp-cesaro-weights`.** Two formulas, each proved by exchanging a finite double sum.
  - σₙ = Σ_{k=0}^{n} (1 − k/(n + 1)) aₖ: the triangle weights.
  - The Kronecker identity: sₙ − σₙ = (1/(n + 1)) Σ_{k=1}^{n} k aₖ.
  - Remark: the triangle weights reappear in HA.2 as the Fejér kernel's coefficients (prose pointer only).
- **`exm-grandi`.** σₙ = 1/2 or (n + 2)/(2(n + 1)) → 1/2. Quick check: the same for 1 − 1 + 0 + 1 − 1 + 0 + ⋯? (Answer: sₙ = 1, 0, 0, 1, 0, 0, …, so the sum is 1/3.) This seeds the zero-insertion example below.
- **`thm-cesaro-regular`.** If sₙ → s then σₙ → s. Real or complex sₙ.
  - **Scratch Work** (first appearance of named move 1; set apart from the proof):
    - write σₙ − s = (1/(n + 1)) Σ (sₖ − s);
    - the terms with k ≥ N are uniformly < ε/2;
    - the first N terms form a *fixed* number H_N, and H_N/(n + 1) → 0;
    - the order of choice is N first, then n.
  - **Proof:** typeset, using exactly that split.
  - **Key proof 1** (§11.2): the A exercise has comprehension parts.
- **`prp-cesaro-necessary`.** If Σ aₙ is (C,1)-summable, then sₙ/n → 0 and aₙ/n → 0. Proof from sₙ = (n + 1)σₙ − nσₙ₋₁.
  - **Non-example:** 1 − 2 + 3 − 4 + ⋯ is not (C,1)-summable, since sₙ/n ↛ 0. Its Abel sum comes in §03.
- **`prp-cesaro-linear`.** (C,1) is linear. Prepending a term a turns the sum s into a + s. Proof: σ′ₙ is σₙ₋₁ rescaled, plus a.
- **`exm-cosine-series`.** For θ ∈ (0, 2π), Σ_{n≥1} cos nθ is (C,1)-summable to −1/2, although its terms do not tend to 0.
  - Route: the identity 1/2 + Σ_{k=1}^{n} cos kθ = sin((n + ½)θ)/(2 sin(θ/2)), proved by telescoping with 2 sin(θ/2) cos kθ = sin((k + ½)θ) − sin((k − ½)θ) (AN1.10 addition formulas).
  - Index as a₀ = 0, aₙ = cos nθ, so that sₙ = −1/2 + sin((n + ½)θ)/(2 sin(θ/2)). A second telescoping gives Σ_{k=0}^{n} sin((k + ½)θ) = sin²((n + 1)θ/2)/sin(θ/2), so σₙ + 1/2 = sin²((n + 1)θ/2)/(2(n + 1) sin²(θ/2)) ≤ 1/(2(n + 1) sin²(θ/2)) → 0. (Sidecar: both closed forms.)
  - Prose pointer: HA.2 meets this sum again as the Dirichlet kernel.
- **`exm-zero-insertion`** (weird example).
  - 1 − 1 + 1 − ⋯ has (C,1) sum 1/2.
  - 1 + 0 − 1 + 1 + 0 − 1 + ⋯ has partial sums 1, 1, 0, 1, 1, 0, …, so its (C,1) sum is 2/3.
  - Inserting zeros never changes an ordinary sum. **Warning:** a summation method sees the indexed sequence, not the multiset of terms. Weird row W2.
- **Warning: the converse of regularity is false.** Grandi is the witness, and it opens §04.
- **Quick checks:**
  1. After `thm-cesaro-regular`: "Can N depend on n?" No: N is chosen first, and only then is n large enough to beat H_N.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A.**
    - (a) Compute σₙ for 1 + 0 + 0 + ⋯ and for 0 + 1 + 0 + 0 + ⋯; check regularity by hand.
    - (b) Use `prp-cesaro-necessary` to show that Σ (−1)ⁿ√(n + 1) is not (C,1)-summable.
    - (c) Comprehension of key proof 1:
      - where is "N before n" used?
      - run the proof on sₖ = 1/(k + 1) with ε = 1/10 and give an admissible N and n₀;
      - which step fails if sₖ is only bounded?

      (Answer: the tail estimate. Grandi's partial sums are bounded and their averages converge, but the tail bound |sₖ − s| < ε/2 is never available.)
  - **B.**
    - (a) The (C,1) sum of the period-3 series 1 − 2 + 1 + 1 − 2 + 1 + ⋯ (partial sums 1, −1, 0, …; the sum is 0).
    - (b) If sₙ → +∞ then σₙ → +∞ (the head–tail split with M in place of ε).
    - (c) Prove `prp-cesaro-linear` in full.
    - (d) Insert a zero after *every* term of a (C,1)-summable series and show that the (C,1) sum does not change. The partial sums are doubled, s₀, s₀, s₁, s₁, …, so σ is a mixture of σ_{⌊n/2⌋} and its neighbour.
  - **C.**
    - (a) Weighted means: if sₙ → s then (Σ_{k=0}^{n} (k + 1)sₖ) / ((n + 1)(n + 2)/2) → s. Hint ladder: (1) the same split; (2) bound the head by (N + 1)² max|sₖ − s| / ((n + 1)(n + 2)).
    - (b) The terms 1, −1, 1, −1, … with zeros inserted *before* each +1 after the first (1, −1, 0, 1, −1, 0, …) have (C,1) sum 1/3. So one multiset of terms can be given the sums 1/3, 1/2 and 2/3. Explain in two sentences why convergence is immune to this.
    - (c) **Find the error (true conclusion; §11.3, item 1 of 2).**
      - The claim: if aₙ ≥ 0 and Σ aₙ is (C,1)-summable to s, then Σ aₙ = s.
      - The flawed proof uses σₙ ≤ sₙ ≤ σₙ, where the second inequality is the illegal step, and concludes by a squeeze.
      - The solution names that line and gives a counterexample *to the step*: a₀ = 0, a₁ = 1 and aₙ = 0 for n ≥ 2, where s₁ = 1 > σ₁ = 1/2.
      - Correct proof: sₙ is non-decreasing. If it were unbounded, B(b) would give σₙ → ∞. So sₙ converges to some t, and regularity gives t = s.
      - Remark in the solution: this is the first *Tauberian* theorem of the chapter; §03 and §04 name the idea.

## 02-products-and-matrix-methods.md — Multiplying series, and averaging in general (target 3,800 words)

- **Opening frame / hook (spiral from AN2.12).**
  - Recall Mertens (`thm-mertens`†) and the witness `exm-cauchy-product-diverges`†: the product series of Σ (−1)ⁿ/√(n + 1) with itself has |cₙ| ≥ 1. (Re-derive it in one line from (k + 1)(n − k + 1) ≤ ((n + 2)/2)², in case AN2.12's label differs.)
  - **Question:** what survives of "the sum of the product is the product of the sums" when neither factor converges absolutely?
- **Definition** `def-matrix-method`. An array (t_{nk}) assigns to a sequence (sₖ) the means τₙ = Σₖ t_{nk} sₖ. The sequence (sₖ) is T-convergent to s if each τₙ converges and τₙ → s. Cesàro is t_{nk} = 1/(n + 1) for k ≤ n and 0 otherwise. Failure-motivated: the proof of `thm-cesaro-regular` never used the exact weights. What did it use?
- **`thm-toeplitz-sufficient`.** Suppose:
  - (i) sup_n Σₖ |t_{nk}| ≤ M < ∞;
  - (ii) t_{nk} → 0 as n → ∞, for each fixed k;
  - (iii) Σₖ t_{nk} → 1.

  Then sₖ → s implies τₙ → s.
  - The proof is the head–tail split (named move 1, second use; **no Scratch Work**). (i) controls the tail, (ii) kills the head, and (iii) moves s across.
  - **Remark (stated, not proved, nothing depends on it):** the three conditions are also necessary for regularity (Silverman–Toeplitz).
  - **Counterexample per hypothesis** (STYLE §10):
    - drop (iii): t_{nk} = 2/(n + 1) doubles every limit;
    - drop (ii): t_{n0} = 1 gives τₙ = s₀;
    - drop (i): t_{n,n} = n + 1, t_{n,n+1} = −n, and 0 otherwise. The row sums are 1 and (ii) holds, but sₖ = (−1)ᵏ/√(k + 1) → 0 gives τₙ = (−1)ⁿ(√(n + 1) + n/√(n + 2)), which is unbounded. (Sidecar.)
- **`lem-product-average`.** If Aₙ → A and Bₙ → B, then (1/(n + 1)) Σ_{k=0}^{n} Aₖ B_{n−k} → AB.
  - Proof: write Aₖ = A + αₖ and Bₖ = B + βₖ. Three of the four terms are handled by `thm-cesaro-regular` applied to αₖ → 0 and βₖ → 0. The cross term is ≤ K·(1/(n + 1)) Σ |αₖ|, where K bounds |βⱼ|.
- **`thm-cesaro-product`** (Cesàro's product theorem). If Σ aₙ = A and Σ bₙ = B converge and cₙ = Σ_{k=0}^{n} aₖ b_{n−k}, then Σ cₙ = AB (C,1).
  - Proof: Cₙ = Σ_{i+j≤n} aᵢbⱼ. The counting identity Σ_{m=0}^{n} C_m = Σ_{k=0}^{n} Aₖ B_{n−k} (both sides equal Σ_{i+j≤n} (n + 1 − i − j) aᵢbⱼ) is displayed and checked. Then apply `lem-product-average`.
- **`cor-cauchy-product-sum`.** If, in addition, Σ cₙ converges, then Σ cₙ = AB. Regularity and uniqueness of limits. **Pays the §02 hook.**
- **`exm-log2-squared`** (worked example).
  - Square Σ (−1)ⁿ/(n + 1) = log 2 (AN1.10, `exm-log-series-endpoint`†). Partial fractions give cₙ = (−1)ⁿ · 2H_{n+1}/(n + 2), where H_m = 1 + ⋯ + 1/m.
  - |cₙ| is non-increasing, since H_{n+2}/(n + 3) ≤ H_{n+1}/(n + 2) ⇔ 1 ≤ H_{n+1}, and |cₙ| → 0. So Σ cₙ converges by the alternating series test, and `cor-cauchy-product-sum` gives Σ (−1)ⁿ 2H_{n+1}/(n + 2) = (log 2)².
  - Note in prose: neither factor is absolutely convergent, so Mertens does not apply. (Sidecar: the identity for cₙ in exact fractions for n ≤ 40; monotonicity; a numerical partial sum.)
- **`exm-sqrt-product-summable`** (weird example).
  - The divergent square of Σ (−1)ⁿ/√(n + 1) is (C,1)-summable to L², where L = Σ (−1)ⁿ/√(n + 1).
  - Its terms do not tend to 0, yet it has a (C,1) sum. This is consistent with `prp-cesaro-necessary`, which only asks for aₙ/n → 0. Weird row W3.
- **Warning: Cesàro's theorem needs both factors convergent.** Grandi times Grandi is Σ (−1)ⁿ(n + 1), which is not (C,1)-summable (§01). It is (C,2)-summable to 1/4 = (1/2)² (C exercise).
- **Quick checks:**
  1. Is the "running maximum" rule τₙ = max(s₀, …, sₙ) a matrix method? (No: it is not linear.)
  2. Check (i)–(iii) for Cesàro.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A.**
    - (a) Check (i)–(iii) for t_{nk} = 2(k + 1)/((n + 1)(n + 2)) for k ≤ n.
    - (b) Compute c₀, …, c₃ for the square of Σ (−1)ⁿ/(n + 1).
    - (c) Which hypothesis of `thm-toeplitz-sufficient` fails for t_{nk} = 1 if k = n and 0 otherwise? (None. It is the identity method, and it is regular.) A short trap item.
  - **B.**
    - (a) **Faded item 2** (named move 1, last step blank): the proof that the weights of A(a) are regular, with "*Name the move, then complete it*" at the head estimate.
    - (b) Use `thm-cesaro-product` to find the (C,1) sum of the square of Σ (−1)ⁿ/2ⁿ, and compare with Mertens.
    - (c) Prove the counting identity of `thm-cesaro-product` for n = 3 by listing terms, then in general.
  - **C.**

## 03-abel-summability.md — Power series as a summation method (target 4,300 words)

- **Opening frame (spiral from AN1.10).**
  - Recall `thm-abel-limit`†: if Σ aₙ converges, then Σ aₙxⁿ → Σ aₙ as x → 1⁻. In AN1.10 it was a continuity statement.
  - Here it is read as a **summation method** and placed beside Cesàro.
  - One sentence on what changes: the parameter is continuous, x → 1⁻, instead of n → ∞.
- **Definition** `def-abel-summable`. Σ aₙ is Abel-summable to s if Σ aₙxⁿ converges for 0 ≤ x < 1 and f(x) → s as x → 1⁻. Slogan: *put the series on the boundary of its own power series.*
- **`prp-abel-means`.** If Σ aₙxⁿ converges for |x| < 1, then for |x| < 1:
  - Σ aₙxⁿ = (1 − x) Σ sₙxⁿ = (1 − x)² Σ (n + 1)σₙxⁿ, with all three series converging.
  - Proof: convergence for all |x| < 1 means the radius is ≥ 1, so Σ aₙxⁿ converges absolutely there (AN1.10). Its Cauchy product with the absolutely convergent Σ xⁿ is Σ sₙxⁿ, which converges absolutely, with value f(x)/(1 − x) (`thm-mertens`†). One more product with Σ xⁿ gives Σ (n + 1)σₙxⁿ = f(x)/(1 − x)².
  - Then (1 − x)xⁿ (n ≥ 0) and (1 − x)²(n + 1)xⁿ are positive weights summing to 1. This is a continuous-parameter Toeplitz family.
- **`thm-abel-regular`** = `thm-abel-limit`† restated: convergence implies Abel summability with the same value. Cite AN1.10's proof; do not re-prove it. A remark says that the AN1.10 proof is the head–tail split applied to the first formula of `prp-abel-means`. C exercise (c) asks for that second proof (faded item 3).
- **`thm-cesaro-implies-abel`** (Frobenius). If Σ aₙ = s (C,1), then Σ aₙ = s (A).
  - *Try it first (ten minutes): use the second formula of `prp-abel-means` and the split you used for `thm-cesaro-regular`.* (§11.8, item 1 of 2.)
  - Idea: it starts from the obstacle that a reader meets, which is that Σ aₙxⁿ need not converge absolutely for |x| < 1. The answer is that σₙ is bounded, so Σ (n + 1)σₙxⁿ converges by comparison with Σ (n + 1)xⁿ. Then f(x) − s = (1 − x)² Σ (n + 1)(σₙ − s)xⁿ, and the split applies with (1 − x)² Σ_{n<N} (n + 1)|σₙ − s| → 0.
  - Proof typeset.
- **`cor-summability-chain`.** Convergent ⇒ (C,1) ⇒ (A), with the same value at every step.
  - **Pays the chapter hook's second question:** two methods never disagree on a series that both sum, because (C,1)-summability already forces the Abel value.
  - A diagram (three boxes, two arrows, the strictness witnesses on the arrows; text-only SVG) is optional.
- **`exm-alternating-integers`** (weird example). For 1 − 2 + 3 − 4 + ⋯, Σ (−1)ⁿ(n + 1)xⁿ = 1/(1 + x)², which tends to 1/4. So the series is Abel-summable but not (C,1)-summable (§01). The second arrow is strict. Weird row W4.
- **`exm-abel-grandi-cosine`.** The Abel sums of Grandi's series (1/2) and of Σ_{n≥1} cos nθ (−1/2, from Re 1/(1 − xe^{iθ}) − 1, with the closed form (1 − x cos θ)/(1 − 2x cos θ + x²) − 1). Both agree with §01, as `cor-summability-chain` predicts. (Sidecar: the closed form.)

- **`thm-abel-positive`** (first Tauberian theorem).
  - If aₙ ≥ 0 and f(x) → A as x → 1⁻, then Σ aₙ converges to A.
  - Moreover, if Σ aₙ diverges, then f(x) → +∞.

## 04-tauberian-theorems.md — From summable to convergent: Tauber and Hardy (target 4,500 words)

- **Opening frame (pays §03's hook, part 1).**
  - A **Tauberian condition** is an extra hypothesis on the terms that turns summability into convergence.
  - `thm-abel-positive` and §01 C(c) were the positivity versions. Here the conditions are on size: n aₙ → 0 or |n aₙ| ≤ C.
  - Name the shape: *summable + Tauberian condition ⇒ convergent.*
- **`thm-cesaro-tauber-general`.** Σ aₙ converges if and only if it is (C,1)-summable and (1/(n + 1)) Σ_{k=1}^{n} k aₖ → 0.
  - Proof: the Kronecker identity (`prp-cesaro-weights`) and `thm-cesaro-regular`. Both directions are two lines.
  - **`cor-cesaro-tauber-little-o`:** (C,1) together with n aₙ → 0 implies convergence, because (k aₖ) → 0 has Cesàro means → 0.
- **`thm-tauber`** (Tauber's first theorem, Apostol 9.33). If f(x) = Σ aₙxⁿ → s as x → 1⁻ and n aₙ → 0, then Σ aₙ = s.
  - **Scratch Work** (named move 2, first appearance).
    - Write sₙ − s = (f(x) − s) + Σ_{k≤n} aₖ(1 − xᵏ) − Σ_{k>n} aₖxᵏ.
    - Bound the middle term with 1 − xᵏ ≤ k(1 − x), and the tail with |aₖ| ≤ ε/k ≤ ε/n.
    - The middle term wants 1 − x small relative to 1/n, and the tail wants it large. Choose x = 1 − 1/(n + 1) (any x with n(1 − x) bounded and bounded away from 0 works).
    - Show the three bounds side by side before the proof.
  - **Proof:** typeset. The middle term uses (1/(n + 1)) Σ_{k≤n} k|aₖ| → 0 by `thm-cesaro-regular`, which reuses §01.
  - **Key proof 2** (§11.2).
  - Self-explain prompt: "*Before reading on: which hypothesis controls which of the three terms?*"
- **`thm-hardy-tauberian`** (Hardy's theorem for Cesàro). If Σ aₙ = s (C,1) and |n aₙ| ≤ C for n ≥ 1, then Σ aₙ = s.
  - **Scratch Work** (named move 3, first appearance).
    - Try Tauber's idea: the Kronecker term (1/(n + 1)) Σ k aₖ is only *bounded* by C, not small.
    - A fixed average is too blunt. Average over a **window** instead: for m > n, ((m + 1)σₘ − (n + 1)σₙ)/(m − n) is the mean of s_{n+1}, …, sₘ.
    - On the window, |sₖ − sₙ| ≤ C(k − n)/(n + 1).
    - Take m − n ≈ εn. The window mean is σₘ + ((n + 1)/(m − n))(σₘ − σₙ), and (n + 1)/(m − n) ≤ 2/ε is bounded.
  - **Proof:** typeset. With m = n + ⌈εn⌉:
    |sₙ − s| ≤ |σₘ − s| + (2/ε)|σₘ − σₙ| + C(ε + 1/(n + 1)),
    so limsup |sₙ − s| ≤ Cε. (Sidecar: a numeric check of the inequality chain on sₙ = Σ_{k≤n} (−1)ᵏ/(k + 1).)

- **`cor-product-hardy`.** If Σ aₙ = A and Σ bₙ = B converge and the product terms satisfy |n cₙ| ≤ C, then Σ cₙ = AB. (`thm-cesaro-product` + `thm-hardy-tauberian`.) **Pays the §02 hook a third time.**
- **`exm-tauberian-not-necessary`** and **`exm-tauberian-alone-insufficient`** (weird rows W5, W6).
  - Σ (−1)ⁿ/√(n + 1) converges, but n aₙ is unbounded: the condition is sufficient, not necessary.

- **Remark (stated, not proved, nothing depends on it):** Tauber's second theorem. Abel-summable together with (1/(n + 1)) Σ_{k≤n} k aₖ → 0 implies convergence, and the converse holds too.
- **Quick checks:**
  1. In `thm-tauber`, could we take x = 1 − 1/√n? Which term breaks? (The middle term. The tail bound ε/(n(1 − x)) = ε/√n is fine, but the middle term is ≤ (1 − x)·Σ_{k≤n} k|aₖ| = √n·(1/n)Σ k|aₖ|, which is √n times something tending to 0, and need not be small.)

## 05-littlewood.md — Littlewood's theorem (target 5,000 words; last section: R group, weird table, List of Results)

- **Opening frame (pays §03's hook, part 2).**
  - Hardy's theorem asks for (C,1). Many series arise only with an Abel limit: a function given by a power series, known near x = 1.
  - *Try it first (ten minutes): run the proof of `thm-tauber` with |n aₙ| ≤ C in place of n aₙ → 0. Which term refuses to become small?* (§11.8, item 2 of 2.)
  - The Idea answers: the middle term (1 − x) Σ_{k≤n} k|aₖ| stays of size C, and §04 C(b) shows that the best the old method gives is boundedness of sₙ. **Failure-motivated:** one value f(x) per n cannot see enough. The new idea uses f at *many* points x^j at once.
- **The reformulation** (prose and display; no label).
  - With x = c^{1/N}, the weight 𝟙_{[c,1]}(xⁿ) is 1 exactly when n ≤ N.
  - So s_N = Σ aₙ g(xⁿ) with g = 𝟙_{[c,1]}: a partial sum is an Abel-type sum with a step weight.
- **`lem-polynomial-test`.** If f(x) → s as x → 1⁻ and p is a polynomial with p(0) = 0, then Σ aₙ p(xⁿ) → s·p(1) as x → 1⁻. Proof: linearity and f(xʲ) → s for each power j ≥ 1.
- **`lem-geometric-riemann`.** For every polynomial r, (1 − x) Σ_{n≥0} xⁿ r(xⁿ) → ∫₀¹ r(t) dt as x → 1⁻. Proof: for r = tʲ the left side is (1 − x)/(1 − x^{j+1}) → 1/(j + 1); then use linearity.
- **Scratch Work** (named move 4, first appearance).
  - We know Σ aₙ p(xⁿ) for polynomials p and want it for the step g.
  - Bracket p ≤ g ≤ q. With aₙ ≥ −1/n, the error aₙ(g − q) is ≤ (1/n)(q − g)(xⁿ), and that is ≤ (1/n)(q − p)(xⁿ).
  - The sum Σ (1/n)(q − p)(xⁿ) is controlled by `lem-geometric-riemann` once q − p vanishes at 0 and at 1, through (1 − xⁿ)/n ≤ 1 − x.
  - So we need polynomial brackets that agree with g at both ends and have a small *weighted* gap ∫ (q − p)/(t(1 − t)).
- **`lem-binomial-moments`.** For m ≥ 1 and t ∈ [0, 1]:
  - Σⱼ C(m,j) tʲ(1 − t)^{m−j} = 1;
  - Σⱼ j·(⋯) = mt;
  - Σⱼ (j − mt)²·(⋯) = mt(1 − t).

  Proof: the binomial theorem, and differentiating (t + u)^m twice, then setting u = 1 − t. (Sidecar: a symbolic check.)
- **`cor-binomial-tail`.** For δ > 0, Σ_{|j − mt| ≥ mδ} C(m,j) tʲ(1 − t)^{m−j} ≤ t(1 − t)/(mδ²) (Chebyshev's argument: divide the third moment identity by (mδ)²).
- **`lem-polynomial-brackets`.** Let 0 < c < 1 and ε > 0. Then there are polynomials p, q with:
  - p(0) = q(0) = 0 and p(1) = q(1) = 1;
  - p ≤ 𝟙_{[c,1]} ≤ q on [0, 1];
  - ∫₀¹ (q(t) − p(t))/(t(1 − t)) dt < ε. The integrand is a polynomial ≥ 0.

  Construction:
  - Choose δ > 0 with [c − 3δ, c + 3δ] ⊂ (0, 1). Let M bound 1/(t(1 − t)) on that interval.
  - Take m ≥ 1/δ and integers k ≤ m(c − δ) and k′ ≥ m(c + δ), with k ≥ m(c − 2δ), k′ ≤ m(c + 2δ) and 1 ≤ k < k′ ≤ m. Put λ = 1/(mδ²).
  - q = B_{m,k} + λt(1 − t), and p = B_{m,k′} − λt(1 − t).

  Checks:
  - On [c, 1], 1 − B_{m,k}(t) ≤ t(1 − t)/(mδ²) by `cor-binomial-tail`, because mt − k ≥ mδ. So q ≥ 1, and similarly p ≤ 0 on [0, c). The other inequalities are trivial.
  - The endpoint values hold because k ≥ 1 and B(1) = 1.
  - (q − p)/(t(1 − t)) = (B_{m,k} − B_{m,k′})/(t(1 − t)) + 2λ. The difference B_{m,k} − B_{m,k′} is the probability mass on k ≤ j < k′. Outside [c − 3δ, c + 3δ] it is ≤ t(1 − t)/(mδ²); inside, it is ≤ 1.
  - So the integral is ≤ 6δM + 3/(mδ²). Choose δ, then m.
  - (Sidecar: a numeric check that p ≤ 𝟙 ≤ q on a fine grid and of the integral for c = 1/2, δ = 0.05, m = 4000. The integral is about 0.60, well under the bound.)
  - Remark: this is the one place where the chapter approximates by polynomials, and it needs nothing beyond the variance identity. AN2.6 proves the full Weierstrass theorem with the same identity (prose pointer).
- **`thm-littlewood`** (Littlewood's Tauberian theorem, in Hardy–Littlewood's one-sided form). Suppose Σ aₙxⁿ converges for |x| < 1, f(x) → s as x → 1⁻, and n aₙ ≥ −C for all n ≥ 1. Then Σ aₙ converges and Σ aₙ = s.
  - **`cor-littlewood`:** the case |n aₙ| ≤ C.
  - **Proof.**
    - Scale to C = 1 (if C = 0, then aₙ ≥ 0, which is `thm-abel-positive`).
    - Fix c = 1/2 and ε. Take p, q from `lem-polynomial-brackets` and put g = 𝟙_{[1/2,1]}. For 0 < x < 1, Σ aₙ g(xⁿ) is a finite sum.
    - Upper bound: Σ aₙ g(xⁿ) ≤ Σ aₙ q(xⁿ) + Σ_{n≥1} (1/n)(q − p)(xⁿ). The n = 0 terms agree because g(1) = q(1) = 1.
    - Write q − p = t(1 − t)r with r ≥ 0. Then Σ (1/n)(q − p)(xⁿ) ≤ (1 − x) Σ xⁿ r(xⁿ).
    - Let x → 1⁻: by `lem-polynomial-test` and `lem-geometric-riemann`, limsup Σ aₙ g(xⁿ) ≤ s + ε. Symmetrically, liminf ≥ s − ε.
    - Finally, x_N = 2^{−1/N} → 1⁻ and Σ aₙ g(x_Nⁿ) = s_N.
  - **Key proof 3** (§11.2).
  - Self-explain prompt: "*Self-explain the inequality aₙ(g − q)(xⁿ) ≤ (1/n)(q − g)(xⁿ): which sign is used where?*"
- **`cor-littlewood-hardy`.** `thm-hardy-tauberian` is the special case reached through `thm-cesaro-implies-abel`. One line, and say what the reader gains: §04's proof is elementary, §05's is general.
- **`exm-sqrt-sine`** (sharpness; weird row W7). sₙ = sin√n.
  - aₙ = sin√n − sin√(n − 1) = O(n^{−1/2}), by the mean value theorem.
  - σₙ → 0: compare Σ_{k≤n} sin√k with ∫₀ⁿ sin√t dt = 2 sin√n − 2√n cos√n = O(√n). The difference is O(Σ k^{−1/2}) = O(√n), by the MVT on each [k, k + 1].
  - So the series is (C,1)- and Abel-summable to 0, but sₙ does not converge: √(n + 1) − √n → 0, so sin√n comes within any η of 1 and of −1 infinitely often.
  - Hence O(1/n) cannot be weakened to O(n^{−1/2}).
