No footnote jokes, no historical-anecdote footnotes, no first person.

**Purpose.**
- A series is the limit of its partial sums, nothing more. The chapter turns that one definition into a toolkit: the Cauchy criterion, comparison and condensation, the integral test, the root and ratio tests, summation by parts with the Dirichlet and Abel tests, and the Cauchy product.
- It separates the two kinds of convergence, absolute and conditional, by what each survives. Absolute convergence survives rearrangement, regrouping into subseries and multiplication. Conditional convergence survives none of them, and the Riemann rearrangement theorem says exactly how badly it fails.
- It supplies what AN2.12 (double series, products), AN1.9 (series of functions, the M-test), AN1.10 (power series, exp), AN2.11 (Euler summation) and AN2.13 (Cesàro, Tauber) take for granted.

**Not in this chapter.** Sequences, limsup and liminf, monotone convergence and the Cauchy criterion for sequences are AN1.2 (Apostol 8.2–8.4 live there). Double series and infinite products are AN2.12. Cesàro summability is AN2.13. Power series and the radius of convergence are AN1.10; here they appear only as Quick checks that point forward.

## 01-partial-sums.md — Series as limits of partial sums (Apostol 8.5, 8.6, 8.9, 8.11)

Budget ≤ 3,500 main-line words.

- **Failure-motivated definition.** Try to "add" 1 − 1 + 1 − 1 + ⋯ by grouping: (1 − 1) + (1 − 1) + ⋯ = 0, but 1 + (−1 + 1) + (−1 + 1) + ⋯ = 1. Grouping is a rule of finite arithmetic that has no licence here. The fix is to make a definition that uses only finite sums and one limit.
- **`def-series`.** For a sequence (aₙ)_{n≥0} in ℂ, the series Σ aₙ is the sequence of partial sums sₙ = a₀ + ⋯ + aₙ. It **converges** to s if sₙ → s; s is its **sum**, written Σ_{n≥0} aₙ = s. Otherwise it **diverges**. Say that the symbol Σ aₙ names both the sequence (sₙ) and, when it exists, the limit, and that the text always says which.
  - Real terms are the main case; complex terms cost nothing because convergence in ℂ is convergence in ℝ² (AN1.2).
- **`thm-geometric-series`.** For z ∈ ℂ, Σ zⁿ converges iff |z| < 1, and then Σ_{n≥0} zⁿ = 1/(1 − z).
  - Proof: sₙ = (1 − zⁿ⁺¹)/(1 − z) for z ≠ 1 (F4.6); |z|ⁿ → 0 (AN1.2 `exm-power-to-zero`). For |z| ≥ 1, |zⁿ| ≥ 1, so the terms do not tend to 0 (use `cor-terms-to-zero` below; order the section so the geometric series comes after it, or prove divergence directly from |sₙ − sₙ₋₁| ≥ 1).
- **`thm-series-linearity`.** Σ(aₙ + bₙ) = Σaₙ + Σbₙ and Σ caₙ = cΣaₙ when the right sides converge. One line from AN1.2's limit laws.
- **`thm-series-tail`.** Changing finitely many terms does not affect convergence (it may change the sum). If Σaₙ converges to s, the tail rₙ = Σ_{k>n} aₖ converges and rₙ = s − sₙ → 0.
- **Telescope** (named move 1). Worked example: Σ_{n≥1} 1/(n(n+1)) = 1, from 1/(n(n+1)) = 1/n − 1/(n+1).
- **`thm-cauchy-criterion-series`.** Σ aₙ converges iff ∀ε > 0 ∃N ∀n > m ≥ N: |a_{m+1} + ⋯ + aₙ| < ε. In symbols as well (STYLE §10).
  - Proof: AN1.2 `thm-cauchy-criterion` applied to (sₙ), since sₙ − sₘ = a_{m+1} + ⋯ + aₙ. For complex terms, ℂ = ℝ² is complete (AN1.2).
  - Remark: this is the only convergence test that needs no candidate for the sum.
- **`cor-terms-to-zero`.** If Σaₙ converges then aₙ → 0 (take n = m + 1). **Warning:** the converse is false; the next theorem is the witness.
- **`thm-harmonic-diverges`.** Σ_{n≥1} 1/n diverges. Proof by the Cauchy criterion: H_{2n} − Hₙ = Σ_{k=n+1}^{2n} 1/k ≥ n · 1/(2n) = 1/2 for every n, so the criterion fails with ε = 1/2. Second proof (named move 2, dyadic blocks): H_{2ᵏ} ≥ 1 + k/2.
  - Proof Idea only (no Scratch Work here; the AN2 halving puts this section's scratch budget into §02).
  - ⟨W⟩ row 1: *terms tend to 0, series diverges* — Σ 1/n.
- **`thm-series-grouping`** (Apostol 8.6). If Σaₙ converges to s, every series obtained by inserting parentheses (grouping consecutive terms into finite blocks) converges to s. Proof: its partial sums are a subsequence of (sₙ). The converse fails: Grandi's series 1 − 1 + 1 − ⋯ (⟨W⟩ row 2: *grouping creates convergence*). The partial converse (removal of parentheses) is exercise C1.
- **`thm-complex-series-parts`** (Apostol 8.9). For zₙ = xₙ + iyₙ, Σzₙ converges iff Σxₙ and Σyₙ converge, and then Σzₙ = Σxₙ + iΣyₙ. Proof from |xₙ|, |yₙ| ≤ |zₙ| ≤ |xₙ| + |yₙ| applied to blocks, or from coordinatewise convergence in ℝ² (AN1.2).
- **Quick checks.**
  - The series Σ_{n≥0} (−1/2)ⁿ: sum? (2/3.)
  - "If sₙ is bounded, the series converges." True or false? (False: Σ(−1)ⁿ.)
  - Swap the quantifiers in the Cauchy criterion to "∃N ∀ε …". What does the new statement say? (That all terms after N are 0.)
- **Exercises** (answers in folded solutions; all original).
  - **A1.** Find sₙ and the sum of Σ_{n≥1} (1/n − 1/(n+2)). *Answer:* sₙ = 1 + 1/2 − 1/(n+1) − 1/(n+2); sum 3/2.
  - **A2.** For each series say whether the n-th-term test settles it: Σ n/(n+1); Σ (1 + 1/n)ⁿ; Σ 1/√n; Σ iⁿ. *Answer:* diverges, diverges (terms → e), not settled (terms → 0), diverges (|iⁿ| = 1).
  - **A3.** Negate the Cauchy criterion in symbols, and state what one must exhibit to show a series diverges by it.
  - **B1.** Sum Σ_{n≥0} (2ⁿ + (−3)ⁿ)/5ⁿ. *Answer:* 5/3 + 5/8 = 55/24.
  - **B2.** Show Σ_{n≥1} 1/(n(n+1)(n+2)) = 1/4. *Answer:* the term is ½[1/(n(n+1)) − 1/((n+1)(n+2))]; telescope.
  - **B3.** Show by the Cauchy criterion that Σ_{n≥0} 1/(2n+1) diverges. *Answer:* the block k = n+1, …, 2n has n terms each ≥ 1/(4n+1), so its sum is ≥ n/(4n+1) ≥ 1/5.
  - **B4.** For z ∈ ℂ with |z| < 1, find Σ_{n≥0} (z̄)ⁿ zⁿ in closed form. *Answer:* zz̄ = |z|², so the sum is 1/(1 − |z|²).
  - **C1** (exit-adjacent). Suppose aₙ → 0 and a grouping of Σaₙ into blocks of length at most L converges to s. Prove Σaₙ converges to s. Show by example that "aₙ → 0" cannot be dropped and that "length at most L" cannot be dropped. *Answer sketch:* any sₙ differs from the partial sum at the last completed block by at most L terms, each small; Grandi's series; for unbounded blocks take blocks (1, −1), (1/2, 1/2, −1/2, −1/2), … with block sums 0 but partial sums reaching 1 inside every block (terms → 0, still diverges). *Marking scheme* (10): 3 locating sₙ between block ends, 4 bounding by L·max|aₖ| over the incomplete block, 3 the two counterexamples. Fatal: assuming the original converges.
  - **C2.** Prove that Σ aₙ converges iff for every strictly increasing sequence of indices m₁ < n₁ < m₂ < n₂ < ⋯ the block sums a_{mⱼ+1} + ⋯ + a_{nⱼ} tend to 0. *Answer sketch:* ⇒ from the Cauchy criterion; ⇐ by contraposition, building disjoint bad blocks inductively from the negated criterion.

## 02-nonnegative-series.md — Series of nonnegative terms: comparison, condensation, the integral test (Apostol 8.10–8.13)

Budget ≤ 4,500 main-line words.

- **Opening frame.** With nonnegative terms the partial sums increase, so AN1.2's monotone convergence theorem turns convergence into boundedness. Every test in this section is a way to bound partial sums.
- **`thm-nonneg-bounded`.** If aₙ ≥ 0 for all n, Σaₙ converges iff (sₙ) is bounded above; otherwise sₙ → +∞.
- **`thm-comparison-test`.** If 0 ≤ aₙ ≤ bₙ for all n ≥ N, then Σbₙ converges ⇒ Σaₙ converges, and Σaₙ diverges ⇒ Σbₙ diverges.
- **`def-big-o`** (Apostol 8.13). For bₙ > 0: aₙ = O(bₙ) if |aₙ|/bₙ is bounded; aₙ = o(bₙ) if aₙ/bₙ → 0; aₙ ∼ bₙ if aₙ/bₙ → 1. One sentence on why: AN2.11 (Euler summation) and AN2.13 (Tauber) state their results in this language.
- **`thm-limit-comparison`.** If aₙ ≥ 0, bₙ > 0 and aₙ/bₙ → L with 0 < L < ∞ (in particular if aₙ ∼ bₙ), then Σaₙ and Σbₙ converge or diverge together. If aₙ = O(bₙ) and Σbₙ converges, Σ|aₙ| converges.
  - **Warning** (⟨W⟩ row 3, placed in §04 where signed terms exist, forward-referenced here): positivity cannot be dropped.
- **`thm-cauchy-condensation`.** Let (aₙ)_{n≥1} be nonnegative and nonincreasing. Then Σ_{n≥1} aₙ converges iff Σ_{k≥0} 2ᵏ a_{2ᵏ} converges.
  - *Try it first (ten minutes):* the terms with indices from 2ᵏ to 2ᵏ⁺¹ − 1 — how many are there, and between which two values does each lie? (STYLE §11.8, first of two.)
  - **Scratch Work** (first appearance of named move 2 as a proof tool; kept apart from the proof, Cummings' device): draw the index line cut at powers of 2; in block k the 2ᵏ terms lie between a_{2ᵏ⁺¹} and a_{2ᵏ}; this gives one inequality in each direction; then decide which partial sums to compare (s_{2ᵏ⁺¹−1} for the upper bound, s_{2ᵏ} for the lower).
  - Proof: s_{2^{k+1}−1} ≤ Σ_{j=0}^{k} 2ʲ a_{2ʲ} and s_{2ᵏ} ≥ a₁/2 + ½ Σ_{j=1}^{k} 2ʲ a_{2ʲ}; conclude by `thm-nonneg-bounded`.
  - Hypothesis check (STYLE §10): drop "nonincreasing". Witness bₙ = 1/n if n is a power of 2, bₙ = 0 otherwise: Σbₙ = Σ 2^{−k} converges, but Σ 2ᵏ b_{2ᵏ} = Σ 1 diverges (⟨W⟩ row 4: *condensation without monotonicity*).
- **`cor-p-series`.** For real p, Σ_{n≥1} n^{−p} converges iff p > 1. Proof: for p ≤ 0 the terms do not tend to 0; for p > 0 the terms decrease (F4.6 real powers) and condense to Σ (2^{1−p})ᵏ, a geometric series.
- **Example under the borrowed box.** Σ_{n≥2} 1/(n (log n)^p) converges iff p > 1: for p > 0 the terms decrease and condense to Σ 2ᵏ/(2ᵏ (k log 2)^p) = (log 2)^{−p} Σ k^{−p}; for p ≤ 0 compare with 1/n. log is used under F4.7's borrowed box; mark it provisional (paid in AN1.10).
- **`thm-integral-test`** (Apostol 8.12; b AN1.8). Let f : [1, ∞) → [0, ∞) be nonincreasing. Then Σ_{n≥1} f(n) converges iff the improper integral ∫₁^∞ f converges. Moreover Dₙ = Σ_{k=1}^{n} f(k) − ∫₁ⁿ f is nonincreasing with 0 ≤ Dₙ ≤ f(1), so Dₙ converges to a limit in [0, f(1)].
  - Proof: f is integrable on each [k, k+1] (AN1.8, monotone ⇒ integrable), and f(k+1) ≤ ∫ₖ^{k+1} f ≤ f(k); sum and use additivity. Dₙ − Dₙ₊₁ = ∫ₙ^{n+1} f − f(n+1) ≥ 0.
  - Proof Idea only (a picture of rectangles above and below the graph; sidecar figure).
  - Second proof of `cor-p-series` for p > 0, p ≠ 1, as Quick check.
- **`exm-euler-constant`.** With L(x) = ∫₁ˣ dt/t, Hₙ − L(n) decreases to a limit γ ∈ [0, 1]. (In fact γ ∈ (0, 1); exercise B3.) Say that L is renamed log in AN1.10 and that γ returns in AN2.11 (Euler summation).
- **Quick checks.**
  - Σ 1/(n² + n + 1): which test, compared with what? (Comparison with 1/n².)
  - Is Σ 1/n^{1+1/n} covered by `cor-p-series`? (No: the exponent is not constant. See R4 in §06.)
  - Can the integral test decide Σ (2 + sin n)/n²? (Not directly: the term is not a nonincreasing function of n; use comparison.)
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Decide, naming the test and checking its hypotheses: Σ 1/(3ⁿ + n); Σ (n + 1)/(n³ − 2) (n ≥ 2); Σ 1/√(n(n+1)). *Answer:* converges (≤ 3^{−n}); converges (∼ 1/n²); diverges (∼ 1/n).
  - **A2** (proof comprehension on condensation). In the condensation proof, (a) why does block k contain exactly 2ᵏ terms? (b) where is "nonincreasing" used? (c) run the two inequalities for aₙ = 1/n with k = 3. *Answer:* (b) to bound each term of a block by its first and last term; (c) s₁₅ ≤ 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 4 and s₈ ≥ 1/2 + ½(1+1+1) = 2.
  - **B1.** For which p > 0 does Σ_{n≥2} 1/(n^p log n) converge? *Answer:* p > 1 converges (comparison with n^{−p}, since log n ≥ 1 for n ≥ 3); p = 1 diverges (condensation, as above); p < 1 diverges (eventually log n ≤ n^{1−p}, so the term is ≥ 1/n; this inequality is quoted from F4.7's borrowed box).
  - **B2.** Show that Σ aₙ converges (aₙ ≥ 0) implies Σ √(aₙ aₙ₊₁) converges. *Answer:* AM–GM, √(aₙaₙ₊₁) ≤ (aₙ + aₙ₊₁)/2.
  - **B3.** Show 1/6 ≤ γ ≤ 11/12. *Answer:* Dₙ = 1/n + Σ_{k=1}^{n−1} (1/k − ∫ₖ^{k+1} dt/t) with every bracket ≥ 0, so γ ≥ 1 − L(2); and γ ≤ D₂ = 3/2 − L(2). Bounding 1/t by step functions on [1, 3/2] and [3/2, 2] gives 7/12 ≤ L(2) ≤ 5/6.
  - **B4.** Prove the scaling identity L(nx) − L(n) = L(x) for n ≥ 1, x ≥ 1, directly from Riemann sums by scaling a partition of [1, x] by n (needed in §05; skip if AN1.8 proves the substitution rule, and cite it instead).
  - **C1.** Let aₙ ≥ 0 and nonincreasing. Prove that Σaₙ converges iff Σ 3ᵏ a_{3ᵏ} converges, and generalize to any integer base b ≥ 2. *Answer sketch:* blocks [bᵏ, b^{k+1}) have (b − 1)bᵏ terms.
  - **C2.** Let aₙ ≥ 0 and nonincreasing with Σaₙ convergent. Prove n aₙ → 0. Show by example that without monotonicity n aₙ need not tend to 0. *Answer sketch:* a_{n+1} + ⋯ + a_{2n} ≥ n a_{2n} → 0 by Cauchy; witness bₙ above (n bₙ = 1 at powers of 2).

## 03-root-and-ratio.md — The root and ratio tests, and series that name numbers (Apostol 8.14; PMA 3.30–3.37; Apostol 1 decimals)

Budget ≤ 4,000 main-line words.

- **Opening frame.** The geometric series is the yardstick. The root and ratio tests measure how fast |aₙ| shrinks compared with βⁿ, and they use AN1.2's limsup so that no limit needs to exist.
- **`thm-root-test`.** Let α = limsup |aₙ|^{1/n} (α = +∞ allowed). If α < 1, Σaₙ converges absolutely. If α > 1, |aₙ| > 1 for infinitely many n, so Σaₙ diverges. If α = 1, the test gives no information (Σ 1/n diverges, Σ 1/n² converges).
  - Absolute convergence is named only in §04. Here the conclusion is stated as "Σ|aₙ| converges"; §04's `thm-absolute-implies-convergence` then gives convergence of Σaₙ, and §04 restates both tests in that language in one sentence. Every example in §03 has nonnegative terms, so nothing in §03 waits for §04.
  - Proof Idea, then proof by named move 3 (**slip a number between**): choose β with α < β < 1; by `thm-limsup-properties` |aₙ|^{1/n} < β for n ≥ N; compare with Σβⁿ. Proof Idea only, no Scratch Work (AN2 halving).
- **`thm-ratio-test`.** Let aₙ ≠ 0. If limsup |aₙ₊₁/aₙ| < 1, Σaₙ converges absolutely. If |aₙ₊₁/aₙ| ≥ 1 for all n ≥ N, Σaₙ diverges (in particular if liminf |aₙ₊₁/aₙ| > 1).
- **`thm-ratio-root-inequality`.** For aₙ ≠ 0: liminf |aₙ₊₁/aₙ| ≤ liminf |aₙ|^{1/n} ≤ limsup |aₙ|^{1/n} ≤ limsup |aₙ₊₁/aₙ|. Prove the last inequality (β-argument: |aₙ| ≤ |a_N| β^{n−N}, take n-th roots, n^{1/n}-type limit c^{1/n} → 1 from AN1.2); the first is exercise B2.
  - Consequence in one sentence: the root test decides every series the ratio test decides.
- **Weird example (⟨W⟩ row 5: *ratio test silent, root test decisive*).** aₙ = 2^{−n + (−1)ⁿ}. The ratios alternate between 1/8 and 2, so limsup ratio = 2 and liminf ratio = 1/8; |aₙ|^{1/n} = 2^{−1 + (−1)ⁿ/n} → 1/2. The series converges (it is ≤ 2·2^{−n}).

Π_{j<k} (1 − j/n) ≤ sₙ; for fixed m ≤ n, tₙ ≥ Σ_{k≤m} (1/k!) Π_{j<k}(1 − j/n), whose limit as n → ∞ is sₘ. So sₘ ≤ lim tₙ ≤ lim sₙ for all m.

## 04-absolute-and-conditional.md — Absolute and conditional convergence; summation by parts (Apostol 8.7, 8.8, 8.15, 8.16)

Budget ≤ 4,500 main-line words. **Key proof:** `thm-dirichlet-test`.

- **Failure-motivated definition.** The tests of §02–§03 need nonnegative terms. For signed or complex terms the obvious move is to test Σ|aₙ| instead. That move sometimes says "diverges" for a series that converges (Σ(−1)ⁿ⁺¹/n, shown convergent below), so two notions are needed.
- **`def-absolute-convergence`, `def-conditional-convergence`.** Σaₙ converges absolutely if Σ|aₙ| converges; conditionally if Σaₙ converges but Σ|aₙ| does not.
- **`thm-absolute-implies-convergence`.** If Σ|aₙ| converges then Σaₙ converges and |Σaₙ| ≤ Σ|aₙ|. Proof: Cauchy criterion and the triangle inequality on blocks.
- **`thm-alternating-series-test`** (Leibniz; Apostol 8.7). If (aₙ) is nonincreasing and aₙ → 0, then Σ(−1)ⁿaₙ converges to some s and |s − sₙ| ≤ aₙ₊₁ for all n.
  - Proof: even partial sums decrease, odd ones increase, s_{2m+1} ≤ s_{2m}; nested intervals (AN1.1) or monotone convergence; the gap s_{2m} − s_{2m+1} = a_{2m+1} → 0.
  - Example: Σ(−1)ⁿ⁺¹/n converges conditionally. Its value is found in §05 (hook).
  - ⟨W⟩ row 6: *alternating, terms → 0, yet divergent* — cₙ with c_{2k−1} = 1/k, c_{2k} = 1/k²; Σ(−1)ⁿ⁺¹cₙ diverges because the pair sums 1/k − 1/k² have a divergent sum. Names the hypothesis "nonincreasing".
  - ⟨W⟩ row 3 (from §02): *aₙ ∼ bₙ, one series converges and the other diverges* — aₙ = (−1)ⁿ/√n, bₙ = aₙ + 1/n; bₙ/aₙ → 1, Σaₙ converges, Σbₙ diverges. Names the hypothesis "positive terms" of `thm-limit-comparison`.
- **`lem-summation-by-parts`** (named move 4). For sequences (aₖ), (bₖ) and Aₙ = Σ_{k≤n} aₖ, A₋₁ = 0: Σ_{k=m}^{n} aₖbₖ = Aₙbₙ − A_{m−1}bₘ − Σ_{k=m}^{n−1} Aₖ(bₖ₊₁ − bₖ).

- **`thm-dirichlet-test`** (Apostol 8.15). Let aₙ ∈ ℂ have bounded partial sums, |Aₙ| ≤ M for all n, and let (bₙ) be real, nonincreasing, with bₙ → 0. Then Σ aₙbₙ converges, and |Σ_{k=m}^{n} aₖbₖ| ≤ 2M bₘ.
  - *Try it first (ten minutes):* the partial sums of Σaₙ are bounded but do not converge; what does summation by parts turn Σaₖbₖ into, and why are the new terms summable? (Second and last try-it-first item.)
  - Proof Idea: after summing by parts the series becomes Σ Aₖ(bₖ − bₖ₊₁), whose terms are bounded by M times a telescoping nonnegative sequence. *Before reading on: where is "nonincreasing" used?* (One self-explanation prompt; STYLE §10.)
  - Proof: the block bound M bₙ + M bₘ + M Σ_{k=m}^{n−1}(bₖ − bₖ₊₁) = 2M bₘ, then the Cauchy criterion.

- **`thm-abel-test`** (Apostol 8.15). If Σaₙ converges and (bₙ) is real, monotone and bounded, then Σaₙbₙ converges. Proof: bₙ → b by monotone convergence; apply the Dirichlet test to Σ aₙ(bₙ − b) (or to Σ aₙ(b − bₙ)) and add bΣaₙ. This is the second member of the faded triple (see below).
- **`lem-geometric-unit-circle`** (Apostol 8.16). If |z| = 1 and z ≠ 1, then |Σ_{k=1}^{n} zᵏ| ≤ 2/|1 − z| for all n. Proof: closed form z(1 − zⁿ)/(1 − z).

With z = cos x + i sin x (F4.10, de Moivre), Σ cos(nx)/n and Σ sin(nx)/n converge for x ∉ 2πℤ, and Σ sin(nx)/n also converges (trivially) for x ∈ 2πℤ. ## 05-rearrangements.md — Rearrangements and subseries (Apostol 8.17–8.19)

Budget ≤ 4,500 main-line words. **Key proof:** `thm-riemann-rearrangement`. **Pays the chapter hook.**

- **Failure-motivated definition.** The index.md table shows that reordering can change a sum. Define what a reordering is, then find the hypothesis that forbids the change.
- **`def-rearrangement`.** For a bijection σ : ℕ → ℕ, the series Σ a_{σ(n)} is a rearrangement of Σ aₙ. A series is **unconditionally convergent** if every rearrangement converges to the same sum.
- **`thm-rearrangement-absolute`** (Apostol 8.17). If Σaₙ converges absolutely to s, every rearrangement converges absolutely to s.
  - Proof: given ε, take N with Σ_{n>N}|aₙ| < ε; take M with σ({0, …, M}) ⊇ {0, …, N}; for m ≥ M the partial sum tₘ of the rearrangement differs from s_N by a sum of terms with indices > N.
  - **Proof-ordering item** (STYLE §11.4; the chapter's one ordering item): seven shuffled lines of this proof plus two distractors (one choosing M before N; one bounding |tₘ − s| by Σ_{n≤N}|aₙ|). The folded solution gives an admissible order and says why each distractor fails.
- **`lem-positive-negative-parts`.** For real aₙ, put aₙ⁺ = max(aₙ, 0), aₙ⁻ = max(−aₙ, 0). If Σaₙ converges absolutely, Σaₙ⁺ and Σaₙ⁻ converge and Σaₙ = Σaₙ⁺ − Σaₙ⁻. If Σaₙ converges conditionally, both Σaₙ⁺ and Σaₙ⁻ diverge (to +∞). Proof: aₙ⁺ = (|aₙ| + aₙ)/2 and aₙ⁻ = (|aₙ| − aₙ)/2.
- **`thm-riemann-rearrangement`** (Apostol 8.18, liminf/limsup form; full proof, where the old blueprint had an outline). Let Σaₙ be a conditionally convergent series of real numbers, and let −∞ ≤ α ≤ β ≤ +∞. Then some rearrangement Σa_{σ(n)} has partial sums tₙ with liminf tₙ = α and limsup tₙ = β. In particular, for any real c, some rearrangement converges to c, and some diverges to +∞.
  - **Scratch Work** (first appearance of named move 5): with target c = 1 and Σ(−1)ⁿ⁺¹/n, run the greedy rule by hand for four turns in a table (sidecar checks it); see that every turn overshoots by at most the last term used, and that the terms used tend to 0. Then decide what targets to use when α < β: real sequences αⱼ < βⱼ with αⱼ → α, βⱼ → β (for example αⱼ = α − 1/j, βⱼ = β + 1/j when finite; αⱼ = j, βⱼ = j + 1 when α = β = +∞; and so on).
  - Proof: let p₁, p₂, … be the terms aₙ ≥ 0 in their original order and q₁, q₂, … the values −aₙ for the terms aₙ < 0. Both Σpₖ and Σqₖ diverge (`lem-positive-negative-parts`), and pₖ, qₖ → 0 (`cor-terms-to-zero`). Take the fewest p's that push the running total above β₁, then the fewest q's that bring it below α₁, then above β₂, and so on. Each stage is possible because the remaining Σp and Σq diverge; each stage uses at least one new term of each kind, so every term is used once. At the j-th turning points the total lies within p_{(last)} of βⱼ and within q_{(last)} of αⱼ; between turning points it lies between them. Hence limsup = β and liminf = α.
  - *Before reading on: where is conditional (not absolute) convergence used?* (Self-explanation prompt.)
  - Hypothesis check: for absolutely convergent series the conclusion fails (`thm-rearrangement-absolute`). "Real" is needed for the statement as given; the complex case is exercise B2's territory and is not stated beyond that.
- **`cor-unconditional-iff-absolute`.** A real series converges unconditionally iff it converges absolutely. (For complex series, apply it to real and imaginary parts: exercise B2.)
- **Paying the hook.**
  - **`prp-alternating-harmonic-sum`** (b AN1.8). Σ_{n≥1} (−1)ⁿ⁺¹/n = L(2) = ∫₁² dt/t. Proof: s_{2n} = H_{2n} − Hₙ = (H_{2n} − L(2n)) − (Hₙ − L(n)) + (L(2n) − L(n)); the first two brackets tend to γ (`exm-euler-constant`), and L(2n) − L(n) = L(2) (exercise B4 of §02, or AN1.8's substitution rule); s_{2n+1} − s_{2n} → 0.

- **`thm-subseries`** (Apostol 8.19). ## 06-cauchy-product.md — Multiplying series: the Cauchy product (Apostol 8.24; PMA 3.48–3.51) and chapter close

Budget ≤ 4,000 main-line words. **Key proof:** `thm-mertens`.

- **Failure-motivated definition.** Multiplying (a₀ + a₁ + ⋯)(b₀ + b₁ + ⋯) term by term produces the doubly infinite array aⱼbₖ, and an array has no order of its own. Two natural orders: squares (partial products AₙBₙ, which always work when both converge, since AₙBₙ → AB) and diagonals j + k = n. The square order is useless for power series, because the coefficient of xⁿ in (Σaⱼxʲ)(Σbₖxᵏ) is the diagonal sum. So the diagonal order is the one worth a definition, and §05 warns that orders matter.
- **`def-cauchy-product`.** cₙ = Σ_{k=0}^{n} aₖ b_{n−k}; Σcₙ is the Cauchy product of Σaₙ and Σbₙ.
- **`thm-cauchy-product-absolute`.** If Σaₙ and Σbₙ converge absolutely to A and B, Σcₙ converges absolutely to AB. Proof: Σ_{n≤N}|cₙ| ≤ (Σ_{k≤N}|aₖ|)(Σ_{j≤N}|bⱼ|) gives absolute convergence; the value follows from `thm-mertens` (or directly). Order: state here, prove after Mertens.
- **`thm-mertens`** (Apostol 8.24). If Σaₙ converges absolutely to A and Σbₙ converges to B, then Σcₙ converges to AB.
  - **Scratch Work** (first appearance of named move 6, **split at N**): write C_N = Σ_{n≤N}cₙ = Σ_{k≤N} aₖ B_{N−k} = A_N B + Σ_{k≤N} aₖ β_{N−k} with βⱼ = Bⱼ − B → 0. The last sum has a part where β is small (N − k large) and a part where a is a tail (k close to N). Decide which constant bounds which part before writing ε.
  - Proof: |βⱼ| ≤ K for all j; given ε choose J with |βⱼ| < ε for j > J; then |Σ_{k≤N} aₖβ_{N−k}| ≤ ε Σ|aₖ| + K Σ_{N−J≤k≤N}|aₖ|, and the second term → 0 as N → ∞ because it is a sum of J + 1 terms each → 0 (or a tail bound).
  - *Before reading on: which hypothesis makes Σ|aₖ| finite, and where would the proof break with two conditionally convergent factors?*
- **Weird example (⟨W⟩ row 8: *Cauchy product of two convergent series diverges*).** aₙ = bₙ = (−1)ⁿ/(n+1)^{1/3}. Both converge (alternating test). By AM–GM, (k+1)(n−k+1) ≤ ((n+2)/2)², so |cₙ| ≥ (n+1)·(2/(n+2))^{2/3} → ∞; the terms do not tend to 0. (The textbooks' witness uses the exponent 1/2; this chapter uses 1/3 in the text and leaves the general exponent to exercise B2.)
- **`exm-cauchy-geometric`.** For |z| < 1, Σ_{n≥0}(n+1)zⁿ = 1/(1 − z)², the Cauchy square of the geometric series.
- **`thm-exp-series-product`.** For real (or complex) x, E(x) = Σ xⁿ/n! converges absolutely (ratio test), and E(x)E(y) = E(x + y). Proof: Cauchy product, `thm-cauchy-product-absolute`, and the binomial theorem (F4.6) give cₙ = (x + y)ⁿ/n!. E(1) = e by `thm-e-series`. One sentence: AN1.10 defines exp as E and cites this theorem for the functional equation.
