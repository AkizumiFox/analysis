**Purpose.**
- Define the integral of a bounded function on a compact interval by Darboux's upper and lower sums, prove Riemann's criterion, and show the Darboux and Riemann-sum definitions agree.
- Decide which functions are integrable: continuous, monotone, finitely many discontinuities, Thomae, the Cantor indicator. State Lebesgue's criterion exactly; its full proof is the closing section of AN2.10.
- Prove the algebra of the integral and both fundamental theorems, with the hypotheses that matter and a witness for each.
- Extend to improper integrals, so that AN1.3 can state the integral test and AN2.11 can define Γ.
- **Standalone before Stieltjes** (spec/08 C3): the integrator is always dx. Every definition is phrased so that AN2.10 can replace Δxᵢ by Δαᵢ and keep the proof; the one place where that replacement breaks (mesh versus refinement) is flagged and becomes a hook.

## index.md

- **Story (chapter hook).** Two functions on [0, 1], both discontinuous on a dense set:
  - the Dirichlet function χ_ℚ (1 on rationals, 0 elsewhere), discontinuous everywhere;
  - Thomae's function t (t(p/q) = 1/q in lowest terms, t(0) = 1, t = 0 on irrationals), continuous exactly at the irrationals (AN1.5 example).
  "Area under the graph" sounds meaningful for both. The chapter builds an integral that gives Thomae's function area 0 and refuses the Dirichlet function. **The hook question:** *which bounded functions does the integral accept, and what exactly separates these two?* Paid in three steps: §03 states the answer (Lebesgue's criterion: the discontinuities form a set of measure zero); AN2.10's closing section proves it; MT (via HA.9) explains why χ_ℚ should have had an integral after all.
- **Second hook (planted §02, paid AN2.10).** For dx it makes no difference whether "fine partition" means "small mesh" or "refines a given partition" (`thm-darboux-equals-riemann`). With an integrator that jumps, it does: AN2.10 shows the two definitions part company.
- **Third hook (planted §04, paid AN1.9 and MT).** Integrable functions fₙ, each with integral 0, increasing pointwise to χ_ℚ. A pointwise limit of integrable functions need not be integrable. AN1.9 finds the hypothesis (uniform convergence) that rescues ∫lim = lim∫; MT finds the theorem that rescues it for monotone limits.
- **What you need:** AN1.6, AN1.7; behind them AN1.1 (sup and its ε-characterization), AN1.2, AN1.4 (Cantor set, Heine–Borel), AN1.5.
- **Roadmap** (six sections, one line each).
- **Named moves:**
  - **Common refinement.** To compare two partitions, pass to P ∪ Q; lower sums go up and upper sums go down (`lem-refinement`).
  - **Isolate the bad set.** Cover the points where f misbehaves by cells of small total length; on those cells use only the bound |f| ≤ M; on the rest, use the good behaviour. Budget: total bad length < ε/(4M), good oscillation < ε/(2(b − a)).
  - **Sandwich.** A number that lies between L(f, P) and U(f, P) for *every* P equals ∫f, once f is integrable.
  - **Telescoping with the MVT.** Write F(b) − F(a) = Σ (F(xᵢ) − F(xᵢ₋₁)) and replace each term by F′(tᵢ)Δxᵢ.

## 01-darboux-sums.md — Upper and lower sums

**Budget:** ≤ 3,500 main-line words. **Hook paid:** none; plants the chapter hook.

- **Failure-motivated definition.** Try to define "area under f" as a limit of rectangle sums with heights f(left endpoint) on the uniform partition. For χ_ℚ the uniform-partition endpoints are rational, so every sum is 1; with endpoints shifted by an irrational, every sum is 0. The answer depends on where you sample. **Repair:** stop sampling. Use the largest and the smallest heights on each cell, and ask whether the two estimates can be made to agree.
- **Definitions.**
  - `def-partition-of-interval`: a finite set P = {x₀ < x₁ < ⋯ < xₙ} ⊆ [a, b] with x₀ = a, xₙ = b; cells [xᵢ₋₁, xᵢ], Δxᵢ = xᵢ − xᵢ₋₁; P′ **refines** P if P ⊆ P′; P ∪ Q is the **common refinement**.
  - `def-mesh`: ‖P‖ = maxᵢ Δxᵢ (used from §02).
  - `def-darboux-sums`: for f bounded on [a, b], Mᵢ = sup f on the i-th cell, mᵢ = inf; U(f, P) = Σ MᵢΔxᵢ, L(f, P) = Σ mᵢΔxᵢ. Sups exist by `ax-least-upper-bound` because f is bounded: say so, it is why boundedness is a standing hypothesis.
  - Picture: lower rectangles inside, upper rectangles outside the region.
- **`lem-refinement`.** P ⊆ P′ ⇒ L(f, P) ≤ L(f, P′) ≤ U(f, P′) ≤ U(f, P).
  - *Proof sketch:* induction on |P′ ∖ P|; adding one point y to the cell [xᵢ₋₁, xᵢ] replaces mᵢΔxᵢ by m′(y − xᵢ₋₁) + m″(xᵢ − y), and m′, m″ ≥ mᵢ because they are infima over smaller sets (inf over a subset is larger, the mirror of `thm-sup-monotone`).
  - **Scratch Work (first appearance 1 of 4):** draw one cell split in two; show the lower rectangle can only rise and the upper only fall; then say "one point at a time" is the induction. The proof itself is three lines.
- **`thm-lower-le-upper`.** For any partitions P, Q: L(f, P) ≤ U(f, Q).
  - *Proof:* L(f, P) ≤ L(f, P ∪ Q) ≤ U(f, P ∪ Q) ≤ U(f, Q). Name the move **common refinement** here.
- **`def-upper-lower-integral`.** The lower integral is sup over P of L(f, P), the upper integral inf over P of U(f, P). Both exist (every lower sum is bounded by any upper sum), and lower ≤ upper by `thm-lower-le-upper` and AN1.1's "sup ≤ inf when every element of A is ≤ every element of B" (prove in one line from the definitions, or cite the AN1.1 exercise if it is labelled there).
- **`def-riemann-integrable`.** f is integrable on [a, b] if lower and upper integrals agree; the common value is ∫ₐᵇ f. Notation: ℛ[a, b] for the class. Convention ∫ₐᵃ f = 0, ∫ᵦᵃ f = −∫ₐᵇ f stated here, used from §04.
  - Give the definition also in symbols (STYLE §10).
  - Slogan: *the integral is the one number that every lower sum stays below and every upper sum stays above.*
- **Examples.**
  - Constant c: U = L = c(b − a) for every P.
  - A step function with one jump at x = 1/2 on [0, 1] (0 then 1): f = 0 on [0, 1/2), f = 1 on [1/2, 1]: for the uniform partition into n cells, U − L = 1/n whether 1/2 is interior to a cell (n odd) or a partition point (n even, the cell ending at 1/2 has M = 1, m = 0). Check both.
  - **`exm-dirichlet-not-integrable`.** Every cell contains rationals and irrationals (density, AN1.1), so U(χ_ℚ, P) = 1, L(χ_ℚ, P) = 0 for every P: upper integral 1, lower 0. Table row 1.
- **Warning.** The upper integral is an inf over all partitions, not a limit along the uniform ones. (Here they agree for integrable f, by §02; the warning is about the definition, so the reader does not prove things about uniform partitions only.)
- **Warning.** f must be bounded: for unbounded f some Mᵢ is not a real number. Unbounded integrands wait for §06.
- **Quick check.** For f(x) = x on [0, 1] and P = {0, 1/2, 1}, compute U and L. *(U = 3/4, L = 1/4.)*
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** For f(x) = 1 − x on [0, 2] and P = {0, 1/2, 2}, compute L(f, P), U(f, P), and a refinement that lowers U. *(Cells [0, 1/2]: m = 1/2, M = 1; [1/2, 2]: m = −1, M = 1/2. L = 1/4 − 3/2 = −5/4, U = 1/2 + 3/4 = 5/4. Adding the point 1 gives U = 1/2 + 1/4 + 0 = 3/4.)*
  - **A2.** Which step of `lem-refinement` uses that f is bounded? *(The existence of m′, m″ as real numbers.)*
  - **B1.** Prove the upper integral of −f is minus the lower integral of f. *(sup(−A) = −inf A on each cell, AN1.1 `thm-inf-neg-sup`.)*
  - **B2.** Show: f integrable and g = f except at one point ⇒ g integrable, same integral. *(The changed point lies in at most two cells; cells of length < ε/(4(M+1)) around it. Forward reference to `move-isolate-bad-set`: just do it by hand.)*
  - **B3.** Compute the lower and upper integrals of f = χ_{ℚ} · x on [0, 1]. *(Lower 0, upper 1/2: upper sums are U(x, P).)*
  - **C1.** Prove that U(f + g, P) ≤ U(f, P) + U(g, P), and give f, g with strict inequality for every P. *(sup of a sum ≤ sum of sups, AN1.1 `thm-sup-sum` direction; f = χ_ℚ, g = 1 − χ_ℚ: U(f + g) = b − a, U(f) + U(g) = 2(b − a).)*
  - **C2.** Prove that if f ≥ 0 is bounded and the upper integral of f is 0, then f(x) = 0 at every point where f is continuous. *(If f(c) > 0 at a continuity point c, then f > f(c)/2 on J = [c − δ, c + δ] ∩ [a, b], of length ≥ δ. For any P, refining by the endpoints of J and using f ≥ 0 elsewhere: U(f, P) ≥ U(f, P ∪ ∂J) ≥ (f(c)/2)δ > 0. So the upper integral is positive.)*

## 02-riemann-criterion.md — Riemann's criterion and Riemann sums

**Budget:** ≤ 4,000 main-line words. **Plants** the mesh-versus-refinement hook (paid AN2.10).

- **Lead-in.** The definition quantifies over all partitions twice (a sup and an inf). A one-partition test is what makes integrability provable.
- **`thm-riemann-criterion`** (key proof). Bounded f is integrable on [a, b] iff ∀ε > 0 ∃P: U(f, P) − L(f, P) < ε. In symbols too.
  - *Proof (⇒):* `cor-sup-approximation`-style: pick P₁ with L(f, P₁) > I − ε/2 and P₂ with U(f, P₂) < I + ε/2 (ε-characterization of sup and inf, `thm-sup-epsilon`), then P = P₁ ∪ P₂ by **common refinement**.
  - *(⇐):* 0 ≤ upper − lower ≤ U(f, P) − L(f, P) < ε for every ε.
  - Self-explanation prompt: "*Before reading on: where does the common refinement enter, and why can't P₁ alone serve?*"
  - A-group comprehension parts: why the ε/2 split; run the proof on f(x) = x with ε = 1/10 (name a P that works).
- **`cor-sequential-criterion`.** If Pₙ are partitions with U(f, Pₙ) − L(f, Pₙ) → 0, then f ∈ ℛ[a, b] and ∫f = lim U(f, Pₙ) = lim L(f, Pₙ). (Sequence limits, AN1.2; **sandwich** between lower integral and upper integral.)
- **`exm-integral-x-squared`.** ∫₀¹ x² = 1/3 via the uniform partition: U = (n + 1)(2n + 1)/(6n²), L = (n − 1)(2n − 1)/(6n²), U − L = 1/n. Uses Σk² = n(n+1)(2n+1)/6 (F2 induction exercise; prose pointer). Worked example in full.
- **Worked example: f(x) = x on [a, b]** with the uniform partition, ∫ = (b² − a²)/2. Short.
- **Riemann sums.** `def-tagged-partition`: tags tᵢ ∈ [xᵢ₋₁, xᵢ]; S(f, P, t) = Σ f(tᵢ)Δxᵢ. Always L(f, P) ≤ S(f, P, t) ≤ U(f, P).
- **`lem-mesh`.** If f ∈ ℛ[a, b], then for every ε > 0 there is δ > 0 with U(f, P) − L(f, P) < ε for every P with ‖P‖ < δ.
  - *Proof sketch:* M = sup|f| (assume M > 0); fix P₀ with U − L < ε/2 and k interior points. For ‖P‖ < δ, passing from P to P ∪ P₀ adds ≤ k points, each changing U by at most 2Mδ and L by at most 2Mδ. So U(f, P) − L(f, P) ≤ U(f, P ∪ P₀) − L(f, P ∪ P₀) + 4Mkδ < ε/2 + 4Mkδ; take δ = ε/(8Mk). (k = 0 case: P₀ = {a, b} works for every P by refinement.)
  - **Scratch Work (first appearance 2 of 4):** the "how many cells can a new point disturb" count, with a picture of P and P₀ overlaid. The ε/(8Mk) is found here, not pulled from a hat.
- **`thm-darboux-equals-riemann`.** f: [a, b] → ℝ (not assumed bounded). f ∈ ℛ[a, b] with ∫f = I iff ∀ε ∃δ: every tagged P with ‖P‖ < δ has |S(f, P, t) − I| < ε.
  - (⇒) `lem-mesh` plus the sandwich L ≤ S ≤ U and L ≤ I ≤ U.
  - (⇐) First f is bounded: if unbounded on a cell of some P with ‖P‖ < δ, moving that cell's tag makes S arbitrarily large. Then, choosing tags with f(tᵢ) > Mᵢ − ε/(b − a) (sup approximation on each cell) gives U(f, P) ≤ I + 2ε, similarly L ≥ I − 2ε; apply `thm-riemann-criterion`.
  - **Remark (hook, paid AN2.10).** The proof of (⇒) used that adding a point changes a sum by at most 2M × (cell length). For ∫f dα with α jumping at a point c, a cell of tiny length containing c still carries weight α(c+) − α(c−). The mesh definition and the refinement definition then disagree; AN2.10 shows how, with the integrand and integrator jumping at the same point.
- **Warning.** "Riemann sums converge along the uniform partitions" is weaker than integrability: χ_ℚ with tags at rational points of the uniform partitions gives sums 1 for every n. The criterion needs all tags (or all partitions).
- **Quick check.** Swap the quantifiers in `lem-mesh` (∃δ ∀ε …): what does it say, and is it true? *(It would force U − L = 0 for all fine P, false for f(x) = x.)* (STYLE §10: uniform notion first appearance, the swap check.)
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1 (comprehension, key proof).** In `thm-riemann-criterion` (⇒), why is P₁ ∪ P₂ used rather than P₁? Run the proof for f(x) = x on [0, 1], ε = 1/10. *(P₁ controls only L, P₂ only U; refinement keeps both. Uniform n = 11 works: U − L = 1/n.)*
  - **A2.** For f(x) = x on [0, 1], uniform n, compute S with midpoint tags. *(Exactly 1/2 for every n.)*
  - **B1.** Prove ∫₀ᵇ x³ = b⁴/4 by `cor-sequential-criterion`. *(U − L = b⁴/n; Σk³ = (n(n+1)/2)².)*
  - **B2.** Show that if f ∈ ℛ[a, b], then S(f, Pₙ, tₙ) → ∫f for any tagged uniform partitions. *(`lem-mesh` and the sandwich.)*
  - **B3.** f(x) = 1/x on (0, 1], f(0) = 0. Show directly that no partition makes U(f, P) finite: f is not in ℛ[0, 1]. *(sup on the first cell is ∞; the definition needs boundedness. Points to §06.)*
  - **C1.** Show `lem-mesh` fails without integrability: for χ_ℚ, U − L = 1 for every P. Then prove the converse of `lem-mesh` holds trivially. *(Converse: mesh → 0 implies the criterion.)*
  - **C2.** Prove: if g ∈ ℛ[a, b] and f differs from g at finitely many points, then f ∈ ℛ[a, b] and ∫f = ∫g. *(Induction on the number of points, using §01 B2.)*

## 03-what-is-integrable.md — Which functions are integrable

**Budget:** ≤ 4,500 main-line words. **Hook:** states the answer to the chapter hook; the proof is promised to AN2.10 by `@ref`-able prose pointer.

- **`thm-continuous-integrable`** (key proof). f continuous on [a, b] ⇒ f ∈ ℛ[a, b].
  - *Proof:* bounded by EVT (AN1.6). Given ε, Heine–Cantor (AN1.6) gives δ with |x − y| < δ ⇒ |f(x) − f(y)| < ε/(2(b − a)). For ‖P‖ < δ, each Mᵢ − mᵢ ≤ ε/(2(b − a)) (EVT on the cell makes Mᵢ, mᵢ values of f), so U − L ≤ ε/2 < ε.
  - **Scratch Work (first appearance 3 of 4):** the budget is found backwards: want Σ(Mᵢ − mᵢ)Δxᵢ < ε, and ΣΔxᵢ = b − a, so ask each cell for Mᵢ − mᵢ < ε/(b − a). Show why *continuity at each point* is not enough (δ depends on the point; no single partition serves), with the quantifier order written out. Comprehension parts: where uniformity enters; which step fails for f(x) = 1/x on (0, 1].
  - Self-explanation prompt: "*Self-explain why Mᵢ − mᵢ ≤ ε/(2(b − a)) and not < : which theorem attains the sup?*"
- **`thm-monotone-integrable`.** f monotone on [a, b] ⇒ f ∈ ℛ[a, b]. Uniform partition: U − L = |f(b) − f(a)|(b − a)/n (telescoping). Remark: a monotone function can have infinitely many discontinuities (AN1.5: countably many jumps); example f(x) = 1 − 1/n on [1 − 1/n, 1 − 1/(n+1)), f(1) = 1: integrable anyway. Remark (see AN2.9): bounded variation = difference of monotone, hence integrable once §04's linearity is in; prose only.

## 05-fundamental-theorem.md — The fundamental theorems of calculus

**Budget:** ≤ 4,500 main-line words. **Ordering item** here (the chapter's one, halved fading).

- **Lead-in (Apostol 7.19).** Fix f ∈ ℛ[a, b]; the integral as a function of its upper limit, F(x) = ∫ₐˣ f.
- **`thm-integral-function-lipschitz`.** |F(x) − F(y)| ≤ M|x − y| with M = sup|f|; F is continuous (Lipschitz).
- **`thm-ftc-1`.** If f ∈ ℛ[a, b] is continuous at c ∈ [a, b], then F is differentiable at c and F′(c) = f(c) (one-sided at a, b).
  - *Proof:* (F(c + h) − F(c))/h − f(c) = (1/h)∫_c^{c+h}(f − f(c)), bounded in absolute value by sup of |f − f(c)| over the short interval.
  - Witnesses for the hypothesis "continuous at c": table row 6 (H, jump: F has a corner) and row 2 (Thomae: F ≡ 0 is differentiable, but F′ ≠ f at rationals).
- **`cor-antiderivative-continuous`.** Every continuous f on an interval has an antiderivative. (Remark: it is the *existence* theorem that AN2.4 reuses in the integral form of an IVP.)
- **`thm-ftc-2`** (key proof). If F is continuous on [a, b], differentiable on (a, b), and F′ (extended arbitrarily at a, b) is in ℛ[a, b], then ∫ₐᵇ F′ = F(b) − F(a).

  - F′ need not be continuous: F(x) = x² sin(1/x), F(0) = 0 on [−1, 1] has bounded F′, discontinuous at 0, integrable by `thm-finitely-many-discontinuities`; FTC 2 applies (sin borrowed).

## 06-improper-integrals.md — Improper integrals
