# Blueprint: AN2.8 — Separation, Urysohn–Tietze, and Locally Compact Spaces

**Purpose.**
- AN2.7 freed topology from the metric and paid a price: a topological space may have *too few continuous functions*. On the cofinite line every continuous real function is constant. This chapter asks which spaces have enough continuous functions to separate points and closed sets, and to extend functions from closed sets, and proves the two classical answers: **Urysohn's lemma** and the **Tietze extension theorem** for normal spaces.
- It then builds the setting that measure theory and functional analysis use for the rest of the series: **locally compact Hausdorff (LCH) spaces**, the one-point compactification, compactly supported functions, the LCH Urysohn lemma, partitions of unity on a compact set, and C₀(X) as the sup-norm closure of C_c(X).
- It does **not** prove Tychonoff (FA.5) or the Urysohn metrization theorem (stated with an outline in §06), and it does not treat Stone–Čech compactification (one remark).

**Why it sits here.** spec/08 C3 keeps AN2.7–AN2.8 in AN2 because the qualifying-exam exit criterion (spec/08, Context item 12) treats Urysohn and Tietze as prerequisites. Nothing in AN2's Apostol chapters depends on it; FA.2 (Radon measures and the dual of C₀(X)) is its main customer.

**Named moves** (in `index.md`):
- **Build a function from its sublevel sets.** Choose open sets U_r indexed by a dense set of levels with cl U_r ⊆ U_s for r < s, and set f(x) = inf{r : x ∈ U_r}. Continuity comes from the closure condition.

## index.md

- **Hook (chapter): "Does this space have any continuous functions?"** On ℝ with its usual topology the function d(x, A) does everything: it separates points, it separates closed sets, it extends. Recall from AN2.7 the cofinite topology on ℝ (`exm-cofinite-topology`): it is compact and T1, and **every continuous f: ℝ_cof → ℝ is constant** (`exm-cofinite-constant-functions`, proved in §01). Topology without a metric can have too few functions for analysis to start.
  - The question the chapter answers: *which separation property guarantees enough continuous functions, and what do we get from it?*
  - **Paid:** in §02 (`cor-compact-hausdorff-separates-points`: every compact Hausdorff space has enough functions, so the Stone–Weierstrass theorem of AN2.6 makes sense on it), and in §05 (C_c(X) is rich on every LCH space). **Paid later:** FA.2 builds a measure out of a positive linear functional on C_c(X); every step of that construction calls `thm-lch-urysohn` or `thm-partition-of-unity-compact`. Say this in one sentence as an outlook; no forward `@ref`.
- **Second hook (§04): "ℝ is a circle with a point missing."** Functions on ℝ that vanish at ±∞ are continuous functions on a circle that vanish at one point. The one-point compactification makes this precise and gives C₀(X).
- **What you need:** AN2.7 throughout; uniform convergence, the M-test and completeness of B(S) from AN1.9; completeness from AN2.2.

## 01-separation-axioms.md — How many continuous functions? The separation axioms

**Budget:** ≤ 3,800 main-line words.

- **Opening (spiral, STYLE §10).** Recall `def-hausdorff` from AN2.7 and the metric facts from AN2.1/AN2.3: closed sets in a metric space are zero sets of d(·, A). Say what changes: in a topological space, Hausdorff separates points by open sets but says nothing about functions.
- **Failure that motivates the definitions.**
  - `exm-cofinite-constant-functions`: on an infinite set X with the cofinite topology, every continuous f: X → ℝ is constant. *Proof sketch:* if f(a) ≠ f(b), disjoint open intervals I ∋ f(a), J ∋ f(b) give disjoint non-empty open sets f⁻¹(I), f⁻¹(J); two non-empty cofinite sets meet. So: T1 is not enough.
  - Then the reader asks whether Hausdorff is enough. `exm-k-topology` answers no for the next step: ℝ_K, the topology on ℝ generated by the open intervals and the sets (a, b) \ K, K = {1/n : n ≥ 1}. It is Hausdorff (finer than the usual topology), K is closed, and **0 and K cannot be separated by disjoint open sets** (proof is exercise C1; give the idea: any open set around K contains an interval around each 1/n, and these accumulate at 0). Mark: a continuous function separating 0 from K would separate them by open sets, so ℝ_K has no such function either.
- **Definitions** (each its own node, explain template for *normal*):
  - `def-t1-space` with `prp-t1-points-closed` (T1 ⇔ singletons closed; two lines).
  - `def-regular-space`: T1, and a point and a closed set not containing it have disjoint open neighbourhoods.
  - `def-normal-space`: T1, and two disjoint closed sets have disjoint open neighbourhoods. **Slogan:** *normal spaces separate closed sets the way metric spaces do.*
  - `def-completely-regular`: T1, and for x ∉ C closed there is a continuous f: X → [0, 1] with f(x) = 0, f = 1 on C. Introduced here as the property we *want*; §02 shows normal ⇒ completely regular.
  - Implications normal ⇒ regular ⇒ Hausdorff ⇒ T1 (each one line, since points are closed). Remark naming T1–T4.
- **`lem-normal-shrinking`** (and `lem-regular-shrinking`): X (T1) is normal iff for every closed A ⊆ open U there is an open V with A ⊆ V ⊆ cl V ⊆ U. *Proof:* apply normality to A and X \ U; cl V misses the open neighbourhood of X \ U. Name the move **shrink inside an open set**. Picture: three nested regions.
- **`prp-metric-normal`:** every metric space is normal. *Proof:* for disjoint closed A, B (non-empty) f(x) = d(x, A)/(d(x, A) + d(x, B)) is continuous (the denominator is never 0 because A, B are closed and disjoint), f = 0 on A, f = 1 on B; take {f < 1/2} and {f > 1/2}. If AN2.3 keeps its metric Urysohn function, cite it instead of reproving; either way this is the metric case the chapter generalizes.
- **`thm-compact-hausdorff-normal`:** a compact Hausdorff space is normal.
  - *Route:* (1) point x and compact C with x ∉ C: for each c ∈ C take disjoint U_c ∋ x, V_c ∋ c; finitely many V_c cover C; intersect the matching U_c. (2) two disjoint compact sets: repeat (1) with points of one set. Closed subsets of a compact space are compact (`thm-closed-in-compact`).
  - Both uses of compactness turn an infinite intersection of open sets into a finite one. Say this.
  - Hypotheses: drop Hausdorff → cofinite ℝ (compact, T1, not normal); drop compact → ℝ_K (Hausdorff, not regular).
- **Weird examples (table started; columns T1 / Hausdorff / regular / normal / non-constant continuous functions separating points):** indiscrete 2-point space; cofinite ℝ; ℝ_K; Sorgenfrey line (`exm-sorgenfrey-line` of AN2.7; normal: stated, marked "(stated)" in the table); `exm-sorgenfrey-plane` ℝ_ℓ × ℝ_ℓ (regular, **not normal**: statement; the proof is exercise C3 via the countable-dense-set counting argument); metric spaces; compact Hausdorff spaces.
- **Warning.** "Hausdorff for closed sets" is not automatic: separating two points does not separate two closed sets, even when each point of one can be separated from each point of the other (ℝ_K: 0 versus every 1/n separately, but not versus K).
- **Quick checks.**
  - Is the cofinite topology on a finite set normal? (Yes: it is discrete.)
  - Which clause of regularity fails for the indiscrete topology on {a, b}? (T1: {a} is not closed.)
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1 (parts):** (a) state the definitions of regular and normal; (b) true/false: a subspace of a Hausdorff space is Hausdorff (true); (c) true/false: a closed subspace of a normal space is normal (true: closed in the subspace means closed in X); (d) in `thm-compact-hausdorff-normal`, where is "Hausdorff" used? (step (1), to get U_c, V_c) (e) negate "X is regular" in symbols.
  - **B1:** decide which of the five tabled properties hold for: ℝ with the topology {∅, ℝ} ∪ {(a, ∞)}; the discrete topology; {0, 1} with {∅, {0}, {0, 1}} (Sierpiński). Answers: right-ray topology is not T1 (so none of regular/normal; continuous real functions are constant, because two non-empty open rays meet); discrete has all; Sierpiński is not T1, its continuous real functions are constant.
  - **B2:** prove that a regular space is Hausdorff and that a closed subspace of a normal space is normal.
  - **C1:** the K-topology: (a) prove ℝ_K is Hausdorff and K is closed; (b) prove 0 and K have no disjoint neighbourhoods. *Answer to (b):* a neighbourhood of 0 contains a basic set (a, b) \ K ∋ 0; an open set W ⊇ K contains for each n an interval around 1/n; pick n with 1/n < b; then W meets (a, b) \ K near 1/n at a non-K point. Hint ladder: rung 1 "work with basic open sets"; rung 2 "choose n with 1/n < b".
  - **C2: find the error (true conclusion).** A "proof" that a compact Hausdorff space is normal: for disjoint closed A, B and each b ∈ B choose disjoint U_b ⊇ A, V_b ∋ b (by the point–compact step); "then U = ⋂_{b ∈ B} U_b and V = ⋃ V_b are disjoint open sets." *Answer:* the conclusion is true (`thm-compact-hausdorff-normal`), but ⋂ U_b need not be open; the illegal step is an infinite intersection of open sets. Counterexample to the step: ⋂ (−1/n, 1/n) = {0}. Repair: compactness of B gives finitely many V_b. Marking scheme.
  - **C3 (optional difficulty, hint ladder):** the Sorgenfrey plane is not normal. (a) The antidiagonal L = {(x, −x)} is closed and discrete; ℚ² is dense. (b) If the plane were normal, every subset S ⊆ L gives disjoint open U_S ⊇ S, V_S ⊇ L \ S; show S ↦ U_S ∩ ℚ² is injective. (c) Conclude 2^𝔠 ≤ 2^{ℵ₀} = 𝔠, a contradiction (cite F2.3's Cantor theorem). Rung 1: "count subsets of L against subsets of ℚ²"; rung 2: "if S ≠ T, a point of S \ T lies in U_S ∩ V_T, an open set that meets ℚ²." Marking scheme. This is the source for the statement in the table.

## 02-urysohn-lemma.md — Urysohn's lemma

**Budget:** ≤ 4,500 main-line words. **Key proof.**

- **Lead-in.** In a metric space the function d(x, A)/(d(x, A) + d(x, B)) is written down. In a normal space there is no formula; the function has to be *assembled*. The question: from open sets alone, how do we build a continuous function?
- **Scratch Work (first appearance of the move "build a function from its sublevel sets"; set apart from the proof, as a `{.scratch}` block).** Suppose f: X → [0, 1] is continuous with f = 0 on A, f = 1 on B. For each r, U_r = {f < r} is open, and cl U_r ⊆ {f ≤ r} ⊆ U_s whenever r < s. So *if* f exists, it comes with a family of open sets nested *with closures*. Turn this around: build the family first, then define f(x) = inf{r : x ∈ U_r}. Only countably many levels are needed, so use the dyadic rationals; they are dense, so the infimum will be honest.
- **`thm-urysohn-lemma`.** X normal, A, B disjoint closed. Then there is a continuous f: X → [0, 1] with f = 0 on A and f = 1 on B.
  - *Proof route:* D = dyadic rationals in [0, 1]. Set U_1 = X \ B; by `lem-normal-shrinking` choose U_0 open with A ⊆ U_0 ⊆ cl U_0 ⊆ U_1. Induct on the denominator 2ⁿ: for r = (2k+1)/2ⁿ, apply `lem-normal-shrinking` to cl U_{k/2^{n−1}} ⊆ U_{(k+1)/2^{n−1}}. Result: cl U_r ⊆ U_s for r < s in D. Put U_r = X for r > 1. Define f(x) = inf{r ∈ D ∪ (1, ∞) ∩ ℚ-dyadic : x ∈ U_r} (write the index set carefully: dyadic rationals in [0, 1] and every dyadic r > 1).
  - Two facts: (i) x ∈ cl U_r ⇒ f(x) ≤ r; (ii) x ∉ U_r ⇒ f(x) ≥ r. Continuity at x₀: for an interval (c, d) ∋ f(x₀), pick dyadic p, q with c < p < f(x₀) < q < d; then W = U_q \ cl U_p is an open neighbourhood of x₀ with f(W) ⊆ [p, q] ⊆ (c, d). Proof of x₀ ∈ W uses (i) and (ii) and density of D.
  - The recursion is a definition by induction on n with a choice at each of finitely many steps per level; flag in one clause that it uses dependent choices (STYLE §10).
  - **Self-explanation prompt** before the continuity paragraph: "*Before reading on: where will cl U_p ⊆ U_q be used?*"
- **`exm-urysohn-nesting-fails` (warning, failure-motivated).** Nested open sets without the closure condition give a discontinuous f. In ℝ: U_r = (−∞, 0) for r < 1/2, U_r = ℝ \ {0} for 1/2 ≤ r ≤ 1. Then f = 0 on x < 0, f = 1/2 on x > 0, f(0) = 1 (in no U_r with r ≤ 1). Discontinuous at 0. The failing condition: cl U_{1/2} = ℝ ⊄ U_1.
- **`cor-urysohn-characterizes-normal`:** a T1 space is normal iff every two disjoint closed sets are separated by a continuous function into [0, 1]. (⇐ is one line: preimages of [0, 1/2) and (1/2, 1].)
- **`cor-normal-completely-regular`:** normal ⇒ completely regular (points are closed).
- **`cor-compact-hausdorff-separates-points`:** if K is compact Hausdorff, C(K) separates points of K. **Pays the chapter hook.** Remark (prose `see` AN2.6): the Stone–Weierstrass theorem of AN2.6 was proved for compact metric K; its proof uses compactness of K and point separation by the algebra, so it holds verbatim for compact Hausdorff K. *S2a must confirm this meta-claim against AN2.6's frozen proof route; if that route uses the metric, weaken to "the proof adapts; see exercise C2".*
- **Remark.** Urysohn functions are not unique; in a metric space the explicit formula is one of them. Stone–Čech compactification: one sentence, no development.
- **Quick checks.**
  - Why are the dyadic rationals used rather than all rationals in [0, 1]? (Any countable dense set works with an enumeration; dyadics make the induction by levels natural.)
  - If A is empty, what does the lemma give? (f ≡ 1 works; the lemma is trivial.)
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1 (key-proof comprehension):** (a) where does the proof use that X is normal? (in every application of `lem-normal-shrinking`); (b) why must cl U_r ⊆ U_s hold, not just U_r ⊆ U_s? (point at `exm-urysohn-nesting-fails`); (c) run the construction on X = [0, 1], A = {0}, B = {1} with U_r chosen as [0, (1 + r)/2) for r ∈ D ∩ [0, 1) and U_1 = [0, 1): compute f. *Answer:* f(x) = inf{r : x < (1 + r)/2} = max(0, 2x − 1) for x < 1 and f(1) = 1, continuous; state the check; (d) give the proof's idea in two sentences.
  - **B1:** prove `cor-urysohn-characterizes-normal` (⇐ direction).
  - **B2:** (a) In a normal space, given disjoint closed A, B and real a < b, find continuous g: X → [a, b] with g = a on A, g = b on B. (g = a + (b − a) f.) (b) Prove that for continuous f: X → [0, 1] the zero set f⁻¹(0) is a countable intersection of open sets (f⁻¹(0) = ⋂ {f < 1/n}); so a Urysohn function can vanish *exactly* on A only if A is such an intersection.
  - **C1 (Exit check: Urysohn's lemma).** Prove Urysohn's lemma in full. Marking scheme: the family (3), the definition of f and facts (i), (ii) (3), continuity (3), the closure condition named where used (1).
  - **C2:** let K be compact Hausdorff and 𝒜 ⊆ C(K) a subalgebra containing constants and separating points. Assuming AN2.6's lattice lemma "if 𝒜 is closed under max and min then …" holds on compact Hausdorff K, deduce density. (Exists as the fallback for the S2a meta-claim check; the solution is the AN2.6 route with "metric" deleted.)

## 03-tietze-extension.md — The Tietze extension theorem

**Budget:** ≤ 4,500 main-line words. **Key proof.**

- **Hook (failure first).** A continuous function on a closed set wants to be the restriction of one on the whole space. On a non-closed set this fails: `exm-tietze-nonclosed`, sin(1/x) on (0, 1] has no continuous extension to [0, 1]. On closed sets of a metric space it holds (prose spiral pointer to AN2.3 if it keeps metric Tietze; otherwise "the metric case is no easier than the general one"). In a normal space Urysohn gives one function at a time; an extension needs infinitely many, summed.
- **`lem-uniform-limit-topological`:** if f_n: X → ℝ are continuous on a topological space X and f_n → f uniformly, f is continuous. *Route:* the ε/3 proof of AN1.9 with "δ-ball" replaced by "neighbourhood". This is why AN1.9 is an s-edge (spec/08 C6). Also: C_b(X) with ‖·‖_∞ is complete (closed in B(X), AN1.9 + this lemma, AN2.2).
- **Scratch Work (first appearance of geometric successive approximation).** Given g: A → [−1, 1], try one Urysohn step: pick a threshold t, let A₊ = {g ≥ t}, A₋ = {g ≤ −t} (disjoint closed in X because A is closed), and h: X → [−t, t] with h = t on A₊, h = −t on A₋. Error on A: on A₊, g − h ∈ [0, 1 − t]; on A₋ the same; in between, |g| < t and |h| ≤ t, so |g − h| < 2t. Balance 1 − t = 2t: **t = 1/3**, error ≤ 2/3. Then repeat on the error with everything scaled by 2/3. The steps form a geometric series; the M-test sums it.
- **`lem-tietze-step`:** X normal, A closed, g: A → [−c, c] continuous. There is continuous h: X → [−c/3, c/3] with |g − h| ≤ 2c/3 on A.
- **`thm-tietze-bounded`:** X normal, A ⊆ X closed, g: A → [−1, 1] continuous. There is a continuous G: X → [−1, 1] extending g.
  - *Route:* h₁ from the step with c = 1; h₂ from the step applied to g − h₁ with c = 2/3; inductively h_n with |h_n| ≤ (1/3)(2/3)^{n−1} and |g − (h₁ + ⋯ + h_n)| ≤ (2/3)ⁿ on A. M-test (AN1.9): G = Σ h_n converges uniformly, continuous by `lem-uniform-limit-topological`; |G| ≤ Σ (1/3)(2/3)^{n−1} = 1; G = g on A.

## 04-locally-compact-spaces.md — Locally compact spaces and the one-point compactification

**Budget:** ≤ 4,000 main-line words.

- **Hook (paid in this section and in §05).** Compact Hausdorff spaces are normal and carry many functions, but ℝ, ℝⁿ and their open subsets are not compact. Analysis on them uses functions that vanish outside a compact set or at infinity. Picture: stereographic projection of the circle minus its north pole onto ℝ.
- **Failure-motivated definition.** "Compact" is too strong for ℝ; "Hausdorff" is too weak for ℝ_K. What ℝ has is a compact neighbourhood at each point.
  - `def-locally-compact`: X is locally compact if every point has a neighbourhood whose closure is compact (state this form; remark that for Hausdorff X it is equivalent to "every point has a compact neighbourhood", since compact sets are closed there).
  - Examples: ℝⁿ, open and closed subsets of ℝⁿ (open: closed balls inside; closed: intersect with closed balls), discrete spaces, compact Hausdorff spaces.
  - Non-examples: `exm-rationals-not-lc` (ℚ: a compact subset of ℚ has empty interior, since every interval of ℚ contains a sequence converging to an irrational); `exm-ell2-not-lc` (ℓ², or any infinite-dimensional normed space: a closed ball is not compact; cite the AN2.3/AN1.9 non-compact unit ball, whichever survives, see coordinator notes).
- **`lem-lch-shrinking`** (shrink inside an open set, compact version): X LCH, K ⊆ U with K compact and U open. There is an open V with compact closure and K ⊆ V ⊆ cl V ⊆ U. *Route:* point case via a compact neighbourhood N of x, the compact Hausdorff (hence regular, `thm-compact-hausdorff-normal`) space cl N, and openness bookkeeping between cl N and X; then finitely many points cover K. **This is the hardest bookkeeping in the chapter**; give a figure and do it in the "compactify, work, restrict" form after §04's theorem as an alternative proof (preferred: prove the lemma *after* `thm-one-point-compactification`, using regularity of X_∞; then the route is three lines).
- **`def-one-point-compactification`:** X_∞ = X ⊔ {∞}; open sets are the open sets of X and the sets X_∞ \ C with C ⊆ X compact and closed (closedness is automatic if X is Hausdorff).
- **`thm-one-point-compactification`:** for any topological space X: (a) these sets form a topology on X_∞ and the inclusion X → X_∞ is a homeomorphism onto an open subspace; (b) X_∞ is compact; (c) X is dense in X_∞ iff X is not compact; (d) X_∞ is Hausdorff iff X is locally compact Hausdorff.
  - *Route for (b):* the open set containing ∞ leaves a compact set to cover finitely. *(d):* separating x from ∞ is exactly a neighbourhood of x with compact closure.
- **`prp-one-point-unique`:** if Y is compact Hausdorff, X ⊆ Y with Y \ X a single point and X carrying the subspace topology, then Y ≅ X_∞. *Route:* the obvious bijection is continuous Y → X_∞ (check at ∞) and `thm-compact-hausdorff-homeomorphism` finishes. This is the tool for the examples.
- **Examples.**
  - `exm-one-point-real-line`: ℝ_∞ ≅ S¹ by inverse stereographic projection; ℝⁿ_∞ ≅ Sⁿ stated, n = 2 as a B exercise (the Riemann sphere; prose pointer: CA meets it again).
  - `exm-one-point-naturals`: ℕ_∞ ≅ {0} ∪ {1/n : n ≥ 1}; a sequence converges iff it extends continuously to ℕ_∞ (convergent sequences *are* continuous functions on ℕ_∞).
  - (0, 1]_∞ ≅ [0, 1]; (0, 1)_∞ ≅ S¹, not [0, 1]. Quick check below.
  - **Weird:** ℚ_∞ is compact but not Hausdorff (by (d) and `exm-rationals-not-lc`).
- **`cor-lch-completely-regular`:** an LCH space is completely regular (subspace of the normal X_∞; Urysohn there, restrict). Remark: an LCH space need not be normal (statement only, SSB "deleted Tychonoff plank"; flagged as unproved here). What survives is the compact–open version in §05.
- **Warning.** X_∞ ≅ Y_∞ does not give X ≅ Y. Witness: X = [0, 1) and Y = [0, 1] \ {1/2}. Both are [0, 1] with one point removed, so X_∞ ≅ [0, 1] ≅ Y_∞ by `prp-one-point-unique`; X is connected and Y is not.
- **Quick checks.**
  - What is (0, 1)_∞? (S¹: removing one point from S¹ gives an open interval.)
  - Is [0, 1] its own one-point compactification? (No: X compact ⇒ ∞ is isolated, X_∞ = [0, 1] ⊔ {∞}.)
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1:** (a) state `def-locally-compact`; (b) true/false: an open subset of an LCH space is LCH (true); (c) true/false: a dense subset of an LCH space is LCH (false: ℚ ⊆ ℝ); (d) which part of `thm-one-point-compactification` fails for ℚ? ((d)).
  - **B1:** prove ℝ²_∞ ≅ S² with stereographic projection and `prp-one-point-unique`.
  - **B2:** describe the open neighbourhoods of ∞ in ℕ_∞ and prove the sequence criterion of `exm-one-point-naturals`.
  - **C1:** (a) prove that a closed subspace and an open subspace of an LCH space are LCH; (b) deduce that a subspace of a compact Hausdorff space is LCH iff it is open in its closure (one direction is enough for the exercise; the other with hint ladder).
  - **C2:** prove `lem-lch-shrinking` via X_∞ (compactify, work, restrict).

## 05-lch-urysohn-and-partitions-of-unity.md — Urysohn and partitions of unity on locally compact spaces

**Budget:** ≤ 4,500 main-line words. **Key proof.**

- **Lead-in (spiral).** The normal-space Urysohn lemma needs normality, which LCH spaces may lack (§04 remark). What analysis uses is weaker and always available: a compact set inside an open set. Recall `lem-lch-shrinking`.
- **Definitions.** `def-support` (supp f = cl{f ≠ 0}); `def-cc-c0`: C_c(X) continuous with compact support; C₀(X) continuous with {|f| ≥ ε} compact for every ε > 0 ("vanishing at infinity"); C_c ⊆ C₀ ⊆ C_b. Notation K ≺ f ≺ U, in words first.
  - **Weird:** C_c(ℚ) = C₀(ℚ) = {0} (an open set {|f| > ε} inside a compact subset of ℚ is empty). Add to the table.
- **`thm-lch-urysohn`.** X LCH, K ⊆ U, K compact, U open. There is f ∈ C_c(X) with K ≺ f ≺ U.
  - *Route:* `lem-lch-shrinking` gives V open, cl V compact, K ⊆ V ⊆ cl V ⊆ U. cl V is compact Hausdorff, hence normal (`thm-compact-hausdorff-normal`); K and cl V \ V are disjoint closed subsets of it; `thm-urysohn-lemma` gives g on cl V with g = 1 on K, g = 0 on cl V \ V. Extend by 0 on X \ V. Continuity by the pasting lemma on the closed cover {cl V, X \ V} (the two definitions agree on cl V \ V). supp f ⊆ cl V ⊆ U, compact.
  - If AN2.7 lacks the pasting lemma, `lem-pasting-closed` is proved here (finite closed cover; preimage of a closed set is a finite union of closed sets).
  - **Self-explanation prompt:** "*Before reading on: why can Urysohn not be applied in X directly?*"
- **Scratch Work (first appearance of the telescoping product partition).** Suppose K ⊆ U₁ ∪ U₂ and we have g₁ ≺ U₁, g₂ ≺ U₂ with g₁ + g₂ ≥ 1 on K (not equal). Dividing by the sum needs a non-zero denominator off K. Instead: h₁ = g₁, h₂ = (1 − g₁) g₂. Then 1 − h₁ − h₂ = (1 − g₁)(1 − g₂), which vanishes wherever some g_i = 1. So arrange that each point of K has some g_i = 1. Check: 0 ≤ h_i ≤ 1, supp h_i ⊆ supp g_i.
- **`thm-partition-of-unity-compact`.** X LCH, K compact, K ⊆ U₁ ∪ ⋯ ∪ U_n open. There are h_i ≺ U_i (h_i ∈ C_c(X)) with Σ h_i = 1 on K and Σ h_i ≤ 1 on X.
  - *Route:* each x ∈ K lies in some U_i; `lem-lch-shrinking` gives W_x ∋ x with compact closure in that U_i; finitely many W_x cover K; let K_i = union of the closures of those assigned to U_i (compact, ⊆ U_i); `thm-lch-urysohn` gives K_i ≺ g_i ≺ U_i; telescoping product. Every x ∈ K is in some K_i, so some g_i(x) = 1.
  - Remark: this is the finite, compact version FA.2 uses; smooth partitions on manifolds are AM's (prose).
- **`prp-c0-complete`:** C₀(X) is a closed subspace of (C_b(X), ‖·‖_∞), hence complete (AN2.2 closed-in-complete, AN1.9 completeness of B(X), `lem-uniform-limit-topological`). *Route:* if f_n → f uniformly and ‖f − f_n‖ < ε/2, then {|f| ≥ ε} is a closed subset of the compact {|f_n| ≥ ε/2}.
- **`thm-c0-closure-cc`:** for X LCH, C₀(X) is the closure of C_c(X) in C_b(X).
  - *Route:* ⊆ by `prp-c0-complete` (C_c ⊆ C₀ closed). ⊇: given f ∈ C₀ and ε > 0, K = {|f| ≥ ε} is compact; `thm-lch-urysohn` with U = X gives K ≺ φ; then φf ∈ C_c and ‖f − φf‖_∞ ≤ ε (on K they agree; off K, |f − φf| ≤ |f| < ε).
  - Say where LCH enters: only through `thm-lch-urysohn`. Make no claim that the conclusion fails without it (in the natural non-LCH examples, ℚ and ℓ², both spaces are {0}).
- **`prp-c0-one-point`:** for X LCH, C₀(X) ≅ {F ∈ C(X_∞) : F(∞) = 0} isometrically, by extending with F(∞) = 0. **Pays the §04 hook** ("ℝ is a circle with a point missing"). Remark: hence C₀(X) ⊕ ℝ ≅ C(X_∞), which is how FA.2 reduces the LCH case to the compact one in places (prose outlook).
- **Quick checks.**
  - Is x ↦ 1/(1 + x²) in C_c(ℝ)? In C₀(ℝ)? (No; yes.)
  - Is supp f ⊆ U the same as f = 0 off U? (No: f = 0 off U only gives supp f ⊆ cl U; witness f(x) = x(1 − x) on [0, 1], zero elsewhere, U = (0, 1).)
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1 (key-proof comprehension):** (a) in `thm-lch-urysohn`, which space is normal and why? (cl V, compact Hausdorff); (b) why is f continuous at points of cl V \ V? (pasting on the closed cover); (c) **proof ordering**: seven shuffled lines of the proof of `thm-partition-of-unity-compact` plus one distractor with the quantifiers in the wrong order ("there is one open W with compact closure such that for every x ∈ K, x ∈ W ⊆ cl W ⊆ U_i"); the solution gives an admissible order, the other admissible orders, and why the distractor fails (no single U_i contains K); (d) compute h₁, h₂, h₃ for g₁, g₂, g₃ in terms of the g's and check 1 − Σh = Π(1 − g).
  - **B1:** on X = ℝ, write an explicit piecewise-linear f with [0, 1] ≺ f ≺ (−1, 2), and a partition of unity for K = [0, 3] subordinate to U₁ = (−1, 2), U₂ = (1, 4).
  - **B2:** prove fg ∈ C_c(X) for f ∈ C_c(X), g ∈ C(X), and fg ∈ C₀(X) for f ∈ C₀(X), g ∈ C_b(X). Give an example with f ∈ C₀(ℝ), g ∈ C(ℝ) unbounded and fg ∉ C₀(ℝ) (f = 1/(1 + x²), g = 1 + x²).
