# Blueprint: AN2.5 — Equicontinuity, the Arzelà–Ascoli Theorem, and Peano's Theorem

**Directory:** `an2/src/ch11-arzela-ascoli-peano/` (AN2 position 11 in `an2/authoring/design/layout-apostol.md`; was `src/ch28-arzela-ascoli-peano/`). **State:** blueprint (S1 done; S2a next). Main line, segment A.

**Place in the volume.** AN2 follows Apostol 6–9 and then adds function spaces. This chapter is outside Apostol's skeleton (spec/08 C1, last paragraph). It comes right after AN2.4 because it answers the question AN2.4 leaves open: Picard–Lindelöf needs a Lipschitz right-hand side, and what happens when f is only continuous? It comes before AN2.6, which studies density and category in the same space C(K). The house manner is spec/08 C0/C5, AN2 column: hooks are opened and paid; Scratch Work appears only where a technique first appears (halved compared with AN1); fading is used only for the one new move (the diagonal argument); definitions are introduced by a failure; there is a weird-examples table; there is a List of Results at the end. There are **no footnote jokes, no historical-anecdote footnotes and no first-person persona.**

**Purpose.**
- Bounded sequences in ℝⁿ have convergent subsequences. Bounded sequences in C[0,1] do not. Every existence proof that runs "build approximate solutions, extract a convergent subsequence, pass to the limit" needs a substitute for Bolzano–Weierstrass in C(K).
- Equicontinuity is that substitute. It is introduced as exactly what the failing examples lack. The Arzelà–Ascoli theorem then gives two results: a bounded equicontinuous sequence in C(K) has a uniformly convergent subsequence, and a subset of C(K) is compact iff it is closed, bounded and equicontinuous.
- Payoff: Peano's existence theorem for y′ = f(t, y) with f merely continuous. Solutions exist, but they need not be unique.

## Named moves (index.md)

1. **The diagonal argument** (`lem-diagonal-extraction`). Nested subsequences, one for each point of a countable set; the k-th term of the k-th row. This is the chapter's one new move, so it gets the faded triple (see §03).
2. **Propagation through a finite net** (`thm-propagation`). Equicontinuity moves control from finitely many points to all of K, with ε/3 as the bookkeeping. This is AN1.9's 3ε argument with a net in place of a single point.
3. **The subsequence principle** (`lem-subsequence-principle`). A sequence converges to x iff every subsequence has a further subsequence converging to x. Combined with uniqueness, this turns "some subsequence converges" into "the sequence converges".
4. **Approximate, extract, pass to the limit** (the compactness method; `thm-peano`). Build approximate solutions, use Arzelà–Ascoli to get a uniformly convergent subsequence, and check that the limit solves the equation exactly.

## Notation

- C(K) = C(K, ℝ) and C(K, ℝⁿ), with ‖f‖∞ = max_{x∈K} |f(x)| (|·| the Euclidean norm), as in AN1.9. Lip(f) is the best Lipschitz constant. Add one NOTATION.md row: `Lip(f)`, if AN2.4 has not already added it.
- A **family** is any subset ℱ ⊆ C(K, ℝⁿ). A sequence (fₙ) is treated as the family {fₙ}.
- Euler polygons are y_N, with π_N(s) the left grid point (§06).

## Hook and where it is paid

- **Hook (§01).** AN2.4 proved: if f is Lipschitz in y, the IVP has exactly one solution. It also showed that y′ = y^{1/3}, y(0) = 0, which is not Lipschitz at 0, has many solutions. The chapter opens with the opposite worry: when f is only continuous, is there **any** solution? Euler's polygons give approximate solutions. Do they converge to something?
- **Paid** in §06 (`thm-peano`): yes, a subsequence converges, and its limit is a solution. The non-uniqueness of AN2.4 reappears as the reason only a *subsequence* can be promised (`cor-euler-unique-converges`, `exm-euler-picks-one`).
- **Hooks opened here, paid later:** Montel's theorem (CA.5); compact operators and the spectral theorem (FA.7); Peano fails in infinite dimensions (FA, remark only); compactness methods for PDE (PDE.5). Each one is a single prose sentence, with no forward `@ref` beyond the atlas id.

## 01-the-question.md — Approximate solutions and the failure of Bolzano–Weierstrass in C[0,1] (target 2,600 words)

- **Hook** (as above). Opening frame: the integral form y(t) = y₀ + ∫_{t₀}^{t} f(s, y(s)) ds (cite AN2.4 `thm-ivp-integral-equation`†) and Euler's scheme with step h/N.
- **Worked example.** y′ = y, y(0) = 1 on [0, 1], with N steps. The node values are (1 + 1/N)^k, and y_N(1) = (1 + 1/N)^N → e. The polygons converge here, and the limit is eᵗ (AN1.10). The general case gives no formula to take a limit of.
- **The plan, stated as a question.** In ℝⁿ, "bounded ⇒ a convergent subsequence" (AN1.4) would finish the argument. Is the same true in C[0,1] with ‖·‖∞?
- **Failure 1** (`exm-tent-sequence`). Let τₙ be the tent of height 1 supported on [2^{−n−1}, 2^{−n}]. Then ‖τₙ‖∞ = 1 and ‖τₙ − τ_m‖∞ = 1 for n ≠ m, so no subsequence is Cauchy.
- **Failure 2** (`exm-power-sequence`). gₙ(x) = xⁿ on [0, 1]. Every subsequence converges pointwise to the indicator of {1}, which is discontinuous. By AN1.9 (`thm-uniform-limit-continuous`†), no subsequence converges uniformly.
- **Diagnosis, which motivates the definition.** In both failures the graphs get steeper and steeper somewhere: the tents near 0, and xⁿ near 1. The Euler polygons for y′ = f(t, y) with |f| ≤ M all have slopes bounded by M: they share one Lipschitz constant. So the property to isolate is "one δ works for every member of the family". That is §02's definition, and the chapter's theorem says it is exactly what is missing.
- **`prp-unit-ball-not-compact`.** The closed unit ball of C[0,1] is not compact. Proof: `exm-tent-sequence` together with `thm-metric-compact-equiv` (it is not sequentially compact). Remark: the closed unit ball of ℝⁿ *is* compact, so the difference is infinite dimension. Name this in one sentence; FA proves the general fact (Riesz), as a prose pointer only.
- **Quick check.** Euler polygon with N = 2 for y′ = t + y, y(0) = 0 on [0, 1]: find y₂(1). *Answer:* y₂(1/2) = 0 and y₂(1) = 0 + ½(½ + 0) = ¼.
- **Warning.** "Bounded" in C[0,1] means sup-norm bounded. It does **not** give a convergent subsequence. Heine–Borel ("closed and bounded ⇒ compact") is a theorem about ℝⁿ, not about metric spaces.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Compute ‖τₙ − τ_m‖∞ for n ≠ m. *Answer:* 1, since the supports overlap at most in one endpoint, where both functions are 0.
  - **A2.** Is {sin(nx) : n ≥ 1} bounded in C[0, π]? *Answer:* yes, ‖·‖∞ = 1. (Its failure is the subject of §02.)
  - **B1.** Prove that no subsequence of (xⁿ) is uniformly Cauchy on [0, 1]. *Answer:* For n < m, at x = 2^{−1/n} we get xⁿ − x^m = ½ − 2^{−m/n}. Choosing m ≥ 2n for each n in the subsequence gives a gap ≥ ¼. Alternatively, use the discontinuous pointwise limit together with AN1.9.
  - **B2.** Show that on [0, ½] the sequence (xⁿ) converges uniformly. Name the feature that changed. *Answer:* ‖xⁿ‖∞ = 2^{−n} → 0. The steep part near 1 has been cut off.
  - **C1 (exit check).** Show that the closed unit ball of C[0, 1] is not totally bounded by producing infinitely many points at mutual distance 1, and conclude from `thm-metric-compact-equiv`. *Answer:* the tents τₙ, so no finite ½-net exists.

## 02-equicontinuity.md — Equicontinuity (target 3,800 words)

- **Lead-in.** Each tent is uniformly continuous, but the δ for ε = ½ shrinks to 0 as n → ∞. The fix is to demand one δ for the whole family.
- **Definitions.** Let (K, d) be a compact metric space and ℱ ⊆ C(K, ℝⁿ).
  - `def-equicontinuous-at`: ℱ is *equicontinuous at x₀* if ∀ε > 0 ∃δ > 0 ∀f ∈ ℱ ∀x ∈ K (d(x, x₀) < δ ⇒ |f(x) − f(x₀)| < ε).
  - `def-equicontinuous`: equicontinuous at every point of K.
  - `def-uniformly-equicontinuous`: ∀ε > 0 ∃δ > 0 ∀f ∈ ℱ ∀x, y ∈ K (d(x, y) < δ ⇒ |f(x) − f(y)| < ε).
  - `def-pointwise-bounded`: ∀x ∈ K, sup_{f∈ℱ} |f(x)| < ∞; and *bounded* (uniformly bounded) means sup_{f∈ℱ} ‖f‖∞ < ∞.
  - Give each in prose and then in symbols (STYLE: quantifiers shown).
- **Quick check (quantifier swap, required by STYLE).** Compare "∀f ∀ε ∃δ ∀x, y" with "∀ε ∃δ ∀f ∀x, y". *Answer:* the first says each f is uniformly continuous; the second is uniform equicontinuity. δ may depend on f in the first and may not in the second.
- **Sources of equicontinuity** (`prp-equicontinuity-sources`), each with a one-line proof:
  - (a) Lipschitz families {f : Lip(f) ≤ L}: take δ = ε/L.
  - (b) For K = [a, b], differentiable families with sup_f ‖f′‖∞ ≤ L: reduce to (a) by `cor-mvt-lipschitz`†.
  - (c) Hölder families |f(x) − f(y)| ≤ C d(x, y)^α with 0 < α ≤ 1: take δ = (ε/C)^{1/α}.
  - (d) **Integral images** (`exm-integral-operator-compact`, first half): for k ∈ C([a, b]²), the family {Tg : ‖g‖∞ ≤ 1}, where (Tg)(x) = ∫_a^b k(x, y) g(y) dy, is uniformly equicontinuous, because |Tg(x) − Tg(x′)| ≤ (b − a) max_y |k(x, y) − k(x′, y)|, and k is uniformly continuous on the compact square (AN2.3). It is also bounded, by (b − a)‖k‖∞. The second half is in §05.
- **Non-examples**, each with the negated definition written out:
  - sin(nx) on [0, π] fails at x₀ = 0: take ε = ½ and xₙ = π/(2n), so |fₙ(xₙ) − fₙ(0)| = 1.
  - xⁿ on [0, 1] is equicontinuous at every x₀ < 1 (on [0, r] with r < 1, |d/dx xⁿ| ≤ n r^{n−1} → 0, which is bounded in n) and fails at x₀ = 1: take ε = ½ and xₙ = 2^{−1/n} → 1.
  - The tents fail at 0.
- **`thm-equicontinuous-uniform`.** If K is compact and ℱ is equicontinuous at every point, then ℱ is uniformly equicontinuous.
  - Proof route: copy the proof of uniform continuity on compact sets (AN2.3), with "for all f" carried along. By contradiction: there are ε, xₖ, yₖ with d(xₖ, yₖ) < 1/k and fₖ ∈ ℱ with |fₖ(xₖ) − fₖ(yₖ)| ≥ ε. Extract xₖ → x₀ (sequential compactness via `thm-metric-compact-equiv`); then yₖ → x₀, and equicontinuity at x₀ gives a contradiction through the triangle inequality with ε/2.
  - Remark: from now on "equicontinuous" on a compact K means either one.
- **`thm-uniform-limit-equicontinuous`.** If fₙ ∈ C(K) and fₙ → f uniformly on compact K, then {fₙ} is equicontinuous.
  - Proof: choose N with ‖fₙ − f‖∞ < ε/3 for n ≥ N. Take δ₀ for f (uniform continuity, AN2.3), and δ₁, …, δ_{N−1} for the finitely many early terms; let δ = min. For n ≥ N use the 3ε estimate through f.
  - The move "finitely many plus a tail" is named in one clause; it is reused in §04.
- **`lem-equicontinuous-bounded`.** On compact K, pointwise bounded + equicontinuous ⇒ uniformly bounded.
  - Proof: take δ for ε = 1 and a finite δ-net x₁, …, x_m (total boundedness, `thm-metric-compact-equiv`). Then |f(x)| ≤ 1 + maxᵢ sup_{f∈ℱ} |f(xᵢ)|.
- **Weird example** (`exm-equicontinuous-steep`). fₙ(x) = sin(n²x)/n on [0, 1]. Here ‖fₙ′‖∞ = n is unbounded, yet fₙ → 0 uniformly, so {fₙ} is equicontinuous by `thm-uniform-limit-equicontinuous`. Moral: (b) is sufficient, not necessary. This example goes into `tab-equicontinuity-weird`.
- **Warning.** "Each fₙ is uniformly continuous" is **not** equicontinuity. Witness: sin(nx).
- **Quick check.** Is {x ↦ x + c : c ∈ ℝ} equicontinuous on [0, 1]? Is it bounded? *Answer:* equicontinuous (Lip = 1) and not bounded. The two properties are independent.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Decide equicontinuity on the stated set: (i) x/n on [0,1]; (ii) nx on [0,1]; (iii) sin(x + n) on [0, 1]; (iv) arctan(nx) on [0,1]; (v) arctan(nx) on [1,2]. *Answers:* (i) yes; (ii) no; (iii) yes (Lip 1); (iv) no, fails at 0; (v) yes, since the derivative n/(1 + n²x²) ≤ n/(1 + n²) ≤ ½.
  - **B1.** Prove `thm-uniform-limit-equicontinuous` in full. *Answer:* as in the route.
  - **B2.** Write "ℱ is not equicontinuous at x₀" in symbols and verify it for {sin(nx)} at every x₀ ∈ [0, π]. *Answer:* ∃ε ∀δ ∃f ∈ ℱ ∃x (d(x, x₀) < δ and |f(x) − f(x₀)| ≥ ε). Take ε = 1 and n with 2π/n < δ. An interval of length 2π/n beside x₀ inside [0, π] is a full period of sin(n·), so sin(n·) takes every value of [−1, 1] there; pick x in it with |sin(nx) − sin(nx₀)| ≥ 1.
  - **C1.** Show: if ℱ ⊆ C(K) is equicontinuous, so is its closure in (C(K), ‖·‖∞), and every pointwise limit of a sequence from ℱ is continuous. *Answer:* the inequality |f(x) − f(y)| < ε, with δ uniform in f, passes to pointwise limits as ≤ ε.
  - **C2.** Show that a pointwise bounded equicontinuous family on a **non-compact** space can fail to be bounded. *Answer:* fₙ(x) = min(x, n) on [0, ∞): each is 1-Lipschitz, sup_n fₙ(x) = x < ∞, and ‖fₙ‖∞ = n.

## 03-diagonal-argument.md — The diagonal argument (target 3,000 words)

- **Lead-in.** On a single point, Bolzano–Weierstrass gives a subsequence converging there. On two points, extract twice. On a countable set, the extractions never end, so what do we take?
- **`lem-compact-separable`.** A compact metric space has a countable dense subset. Proof: the union over m ≥ 1 of finite 1/m-nets (total boundedness, `thm-metric-compact-equiv`). Example: ℚ ∩ [a, b].
- **Scratch Work** (this is the first appearance of the technique, so it is kept in AN2; it is set apart from the proof, as STYLE §11 requires).
  - Draw the rows: σ₁ is a subsequence along which fₙ(d₁) converges, σ₂ ⊆ σ₁ is one along which fₙ(d₂) also converges, and so on.
  - First wrong idea: "take the intersection of all rows". It can be empty. Witness: row k = {n ≥ k}.
  - Second wrong idea: "take the first term of each row". The result need not be increasing, and need not lie in later rows.
  - The fix: take the k-th term of row k. From index j on, this sequence lies in row j.
- **`lem-diagonal-extraction`.** Let D = {d₁, d₂, …} be countable and (fₙ) functions D → ℝⁿ with sup_n |fₙ(d_j)| < ∞ for each j. Then there are indices n₁ < n₂ < ⋯ such that (f_{n_k}(d_j))_k converges for every j.
  - Proof: inductive rows by Bolzano–Weierstrass in ℝⁿ (AN1.4); set n_k = σ_k(k).
  - **Strictly increasing:** σ_{k+1}(k+1) ≥ σ_k(k+1) > σ_k(k), because σ_{k+1} is a subsequence of σ_k.
  - **Eventually in row j:** for k ≥ j, n_k = σ_k(k) is a term of σ_k ⊆ σ_j, and at position ≥ k. So (n_k)_{k≥j} is a subsequence of σ_j.
  - Uses "for each k choose" (one clause, as STYLE §10 requires).
- **Remark (prose only).** Cantor's diagonal argument (F2.3) uses the same picture to *escape* a list. Here the picture is used to *stay in* every row. No `@ref`.
- **Faded triple** (STYLE "Faded proofs"; the only one in this chapter). (1) The lemma's proof, worked in full. (2) B2 below, with the last step blank. (3) C1 below, with the last half blank. Each blank carries "*Name the move, then complete it.*"
- **Quick check.** Rows: S_k = the positive multiples of 2^{k−1} (so S₁ = ℕ_{≥1}). List the first four diagonal indices. *Answer:* the k-th element of S_k is k·2^{k−1}, giving 1, 4, 12, 32.
- **Warning.** The diagonal subsequence converges at each d_j, but **at different rates**: there is no claim of uniformity over j. Uniformity needs equicontinuity (§04).
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Rows S_k = {n : n ≥ k² }. Is the diagonal n_k = k-th term of S_k equal to k² + k − 1? *Answer:* yes, since S_k = {k², k² + 1, …}.
  - **A2.** Compute the intersection of all rows in A1. *Answer:* it is empty (n < k² for k large), which is the Scratch Work's point: the diagonal, not the intersection, is the sequence to take.
  - **B1.** Let fₙ(x) = sin(nx). Show that there is a subsequence converging at every rational point of [0, π]. *Answer:* the lemma applies, since |fₙ| ≤ 1. (A contrast to remember: by §02 this subsequence does **not** converge uniformly, and §04 explains why.)
  - **B2 (faded, last step blank).** A sequence of real sequences x^{(n)} = (x^{(n)}_j)_j with |x^{(n)}_j| ≤ C_j has a subsequence converging in every coordinate. Rows are given; the blank is "n_k := ___ and why it lies in row j". *Answer:* σ_k(k); see the lemma.
  - **C1 (faded, last half blank; Helly's selection theorem).** Let φₙ : [0, 1] → [0, 1] be increasing. (a) Extract a subsequence converging at every rational (given). (b) *Blank:* define φ(x) = sup over rationals r ≤ x of lim φ_{n_k}(r); show φ_{n_k}(x) → φ(x) at every continuity point x of φ; the discontinuities are countable (AN2.9, `thm-monotone-discontinuities`†); diagonalize once more. *Answer:* at a continuity point x, squeeze φ_{n_k}(x) between the limits at rationals r < x < r′ whose limit values are within ε of φ(x). On the countable discontinuity set, apply `lem-diagonal-extraction` again. Remark: this is used in MT/PR (prose only).

## 04-arzela-ascoli.md — The Arzelà–Ascoli theorem (target 4,400 words)

- **Lead-in.** The diagonal argument gives convergence on a countable dense set. Equicontinuity spreads it to all of K, and does so uniformly.
- **`thm-propagation`.** Let K be compact, (fₙ) ⊆ C(K, ℝⁿ) equicontinuous, and D ⊆ K dense with (fₙ(d)) convergent for every d ∈ D. Then (fₙ) converges uniformly on K.
  - **Idea:** a finite net from D plus one δ for all n.
  - **Proof:** given ε, take δ from uniform equicontinuity (`thm-equicontinuous-uniform`) for ε/3. Cover K by finitely many balls B(dᵢ, δ) with dᵢ ∈ D; this uses compactness and density, since the balls B(d, δ), d ∈ D, form an open cover. Take N with |f_m(dᵢ) − fₙ(dᵢ)| < ε/3 for m, n ≥ N and all i. Then ‖f_m − fₙ‖∞ < ε, and completeness of C(K, ℝⁿ) (AN1.9, `thm-ck-complete`†) gives the uniform limit.
  - Named move 2 is stated here.
- **`thm-arzela-ascoli`** (registry label; the sequential form). Let K be a compact metric space. Every pointwise bounded, equicontinuous sequence (fₙ) in C(K, ℝⁿ) has a uniformly convergent subsequence.
  - Proof route: `lem-compact-separable` → `lem-diagonal-extraction` (applicable by pointwise boundedness) → `thm-propagation` applied to the subsequence (a subsequence of an equicontinuous sequence is equicontinuous).
  - "Pointwise bounded" can be replaced by "bounded" by `lem-equicontinuous-bounded`; say this.
  - This is one of the chapter's 2–3 key proofs (STYLE §11.2). **Try-it-first** box before the proof: "You have a tool for countable sets and a tool for spreading control. Assemble them."
- **`thm-arzela-ascoli-compact`** (Heine–Borel in C(K)). For E ⊆ C(K, ℝⁿ) with K compact: E is compact ⇔ E is closed, bounded and equicontinuous.
  - **(⇐)** A sequence in E has a uniformly convergent subsequence by `thm-arzela-ascoli`, with its limit in E because E is closed. Hence E is sequentially compact, hence compact (`thm-metric-compact-equiv`).
  - **(⇒)** Compact ⇒ closed and bounded (AN2.3). Equicontinuous: E is totally bounded (`thm-metric-compact-equiv`). Take a finite ε/3-net g₁, …, g_m in E, a δ that works for each gᵢ (finitely many uniformly continuous functions), and the 3ε estimate. This is the "finitely many plus a tail" move again, with a net in place of a tail.
  - This proof is where the registry edge `thm-arzela-ascoli uses thm-metric-compact-equiv` is realized.
- **`cor-relatively-compact`.** E has compact closure in C(K, ℝⁿ) ⇔ E is bounded and equicontinuous. Proof: by C1 of §02 the closure is still equicontinuous and still bounded.
- **`cor-bounded-derivatives`.** On [a, b], a sequence of differentiable fₙ with |fₙ(a)| ≤ C and ‖fₙ′‖∞ ≤ L has a uniformly convergent subsequence. Proof: `prp-equicontinuity-sources`(b) plus |fₙ(x)| ≤ C + L(b − a).
- **Worked example.** For any sequence hₙ ∈ C[0,1] with ‖hₙ‖∞ ≤ 1, the primitives Hₙ(x) = ∫₀ˣ hₙ have a uniformly convergent subsequence, because they are 1-Lipschitz with Hₙ(0) = 0. The hₙ themselves need not: take hₙ = sin(2πnx). Moral: integration smooths, and smoothing produces compactness.
- **"Alternatively" paragraph.** A second proof goes through total boundedness: approximate members of E by functions taking values in a finite grid on a finite net. It is set as C2; mention it in one paragraph.
- **Warning 1.** Arzelà–Ascoli promises a convergent **subsequence**, not convergence of the sequence. Witness: fₙ = (−1)ⁿ (constants), which is equicontinuous and bounded but does not converge.
- **Warning 2 (name clash).** Apostol's "Arzelà theorem" (9.12, the bounded convergence theorem for Riemann integrals) is a different result. MT proves it as a consequence of dominated convergence (prose pointer only).
- **Quick check.** Is {f ∈ C[0,1] : ‖f‖∞ ≤ 1, Lip(f) ≤ 5} compact? *Answer:* yes. It is bounded, equicontinuous by (a), and closed, because |f(x) − f(y)| ≤ 5|x − y| passes to uniform limits.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Which are compact in C[0,1]? (i) {‖f‖∞ ≤ 1, Lip ≤ 1}; (ii) {Lip ≤ 1}; (iii) {f ∈ C¹ : ‖f‖∞ ≤ 1, ‖f′‖∞ ≤ 1}; (iv) {xⁿ : n ≥ 1}. *Answers:* (i) yes. (ii) No: it is unbounded (the constants). (iii) No: it is not closed, since smooth approximations of ½|x − ½| converge uniformly to a non-C¹ function; but it is relatively compact. (iv) No: it is not equicontinuous at 1.
  - **B1.** Prove the (⇒) half of `thm-arzela-ascoli-compact`. *Answer:* as in the route.
  - **B2.** Show that `thm-propagation` fails without equicontinuity. *Answer:* xⁿ converges at every point of the dense set [0, 1) ∩ ℚ (to 0), but not uniformly on [0, 1].
  - **C1.** Prove: if (fₙ) ⊆ C(K) is equicontinuous and converges pointwise on all of K, then it converges uniformly. *Answer:* a special case of `thm-propagation`, with D = K.
  - **C2 (the ε-net proof).** Prove (⇐) of `thm-arzela-ascoli-compact` by showing directly that a closed, bounded, equicontinuous E is totally bounded. ## 05-hypotheses-and-applications.md — Each hypothesis is needed; first applications (target 3,200 words)

- **Lead-in.** Arzelà–Ascoli has three hypotheses: boundedness, equicontinuity and compactness of K. Drop any one and it fails.
- **`tab-equicontinuity-weird`** (the weird-examples table; its rows go to the pathological table that closes in AN2.15). Columns: sequence | bounded | equicontinuous | K compact | uniformly convergent subsequence?
  - sin(nx) on [0, π] | yes | no | yes | no (by `thm-uniform-limit-equicontinuous`: a uniformly convergent subsequence would be equicontinuous)
  - fₙ ≡ n on [0, 1] | no | yes | yes | no
  - xⁿ on [0, 1] | yes | no (only at 1) | yes | no
  - bₙ(x) = max(0, 1 − |x − n|) on ℝ | yes | yes (Lip 1) | **no** | no: the pointwise limit is 0 and ‖bₙ‖∞ = 1
  - sin(n²x)/n on [0, 1] | yes | yes (despite ‖fₙ′‖∞ = n) | yes | yes, the whole sequence → 0
  - (−1)ⁿ constants | yes | yes | yes | yes, but the whole sequence does not converge
- **`lem-subsequence-principle`.** In a metric space, xₙ → x iff every subsequence has a further subsequence converging to x. Proof of (⇐) by contrapositive: if xₙ ↛ x, some ε and some subsequence stay ε away, and no further subsequence of it converges to x.
  - **`cor-aa-unique-limit`:** a bounded equicontinuous sequence in C(K) whose uniformly convergent subsequences all have the same limit f converges uniformly to f.
- **Application 1** (`cor-lipschitz-ball-compact`). For x₀ ∈ K, C ≥ 0 and L ≥ 0, the set {f ∈ C(K) : |f(x₀)| ≤ C, Lip(f) ≤ L} is compact. Proof: bounded by C + L·diam K; closed; equicontinuous.
- **Application 2: minimizers exist** (`exm-minimizer-exists`).
  - Failure first. Over {f ∈ C[0,1] : f(0) = 1}, the infimum of ∫₀¹ f² is 0 and is not attained: f_k(x) = max(0, 1 − kx) gives ∫ f_k² = 1/(3k) → 0, and ∫f² = 0 forces f ≡ 0, contradicting f(0) = 1.
  - With the constraint Lip(f) ≤ L added, the set is compact by Application 1, Φ(f) = ∫₀¹ f² is continuous for ‖·‖∞ (AN1.9), so a minimizer exists (EVT on a compact metric space, AN2.3, `thm-metric-evt`†).
  - The computation for L = 1: the minimizer is f(x) = max(0, 1 − x), because any f with f(0) = 1 and Lip ≤ 1 has f(x) ≥ 1 − x, so f² ≥ (max(0, 1 − x))² pointwise.
  - Moral: compactness turns "the infimum" into "the minimum". This is the direct method, named in one prose sentence, no pointer.
- **Application 3** (`exm-integral-operator-compact`, second half). With T as in §02(d), T maps the closed unit ball of C[a, b] into a set with compact closure (`cor-relatively-compact`). Hook, one sentence: such operators are *compact operators*, and FA.7 builds a spectral theory on exactly this property.
- **Quick check.** In the bₙ row, which hypothesis fails, and does a subsequence converge uniformly on [−5, 5]? *Answer:* K = ℝ is not compact. Yes: on [−5, 5], bₙ = 0 for n ≥ 6.
- **Warning.** The compactness of K cannot be traded for a uniform bound. The bₙ row has both a uniform bound and a uniform Lipschitz constant.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** For each sequence, name the hypothesis that fails: (i) n sin x on [0, π]; (ii) arctan(nx) on [−1, 1]; (iii) a bump of height 1 sliding to +∞ on [0, ∞); (iv) cos(nx)/n on [0, 1]. *Answers:* (i) bounded; (ii) equicontinuous (at 0); (iii) compactness of K; (iv) none fails, and the sequence → 0 uniformly.
  - **B1.** Prove `cor-lipschitz-ball-compact`. *Answer:* as in the route; closedness by passing |f(x) − f(y)| ≤ L d(x, y) and |f(x₀)| ≤ C to the limit.
  - **B2.** Prove `lem-subsequence-principle`. *Answer:* as in the route.
  - **C1 (Arzelà–Ascoli on ℝ, which re-uses the diagonal).** Let (fₙ) be bounded and equicontinuous on ℝ. Show that a subsequence converges uniformly on every compact interval. *Answer:* apply `thm-arzela-ascoli` on [−1, 1], then on [−2, 2] to the resulting subsequence, and so on; the diagonal subsequence converges uniformly on each [−m, m]. Name the move.
  - **C2.** Show that the minimizer in Application 2 is unique for every L > 0. *Answer:* f(x) ≥ max(0, 1 − Lx) pointwise for every admissible f, and that function is admissible, so equality in ∫f² ≥ ∫ max(0, 1 − Lx)² forces f = max(0, 1 − Lx) (continuity and non-negativity of the difference of squares).

## 06-peano.md — Peano's existence theorem (target 4,300 words)

- **Setting.** f : R → ℝⁿ continuous on R = [t₀, t₀ + a] × B̄(y₀, b) ⊆ ℝ × ℝⁿ, with a, b > 0. Let M = max_R |f|, which exists by EVT since R is compact, and h = min(a, b/M), with h = a if M = 0.
- **`def-euler-polygon`.** For N ≥ 1, set t_k = t₀ + kh/N. Define y_N(t₀) = y₀ and, on [t_k, t_{k+1}], y_N(t) = y_N(t_k) + (t − t_k) f(t_k, y_N(t_k)). Write π_N(s) = t_k for s ∈ [t_k, t_{k+1}).
- **`lem-euler-polygon-properties`.** For every N:
  - (i) |y_N(t) − y₀| ≤ M(t − t₀) ≤ b on [t₀, t₀ + h], so the graph stays in R and every step is defined;
  - (ii) Lip(y_N) ≤ M;
  - (iii) y_N(t) = y₀ + ∫_{t₀}^{t} f(π_N(s), y_N(π_N(s))) ds.
  - Proof: induction on k for (i). Note that (i) is what makes the next node lie in R, which is where h ≤ b/M is used. (ii) and (iii) hold because y_N is piecewise linear with slopes f(t_k, y_N(t_k)).
- **Scratch Work** (the limit passage is the one new technique here; kept, set apart). The plan is to compare f(π_N(s), y_N(π_N(s))) with f(s, y(s)). Two errors appear:
  - the grid error |π_N(s) − s| ≤ h/N together with |y_N(π_N s) − y_N(s)| ≤ Mh/N;
  - the subsequence error ‖y_{N_k} − y‖∞.
  - Both are small *uniformly in s*. Uniform continuity of f on compact R converts small arguments into small values. Only now write the proof.
- **`thm-peano`.** Under the setting above, the IVP y′ = f(t, y), y(t₀) = y₀ has a C¹ solution on [t₀, t₀ + h]. The same holds on [t₀ − h, t₀], so on [t₀ − h, t₀ + h] for a two-sided rectangle.
  - **Proof (key proof):**
    - By the lemma, {y_N} ⊆ C([t₀, t₀ + h], ℝⁿ) is bounded (by |y₀| + b) and M-Lipschitz, so `thm-arzela-ascoli` gives y_{N_k} → y uniformly. Then |y(t) − y₀| ≤ b and Lip(y) ≤ M.
    - Given ε, take η from uniform continuity of f on R (AN2.3) and k₀ with h/N_k + Mh/N_k + ‖y_{N_k} − y‖∞ < η for k ≥ k₀. Then |f(π(s), y_{N_k}(π s)) − f(s, y(s))| < ε for all s.
    - Pass to the limit in (iii) (AN1.9, `thm-uniform-limit-integral`†) to get the integral equation.
    - Conclude by `thm-ivp-integral-equation`† (AN2.4).
  - Named move 4 is stated here.
- **Non-uniqueness, the hook's other half** (`exm-euler-picks-one`). y′ = 2√|y|, y(0) = 0.
  - For every c ≥ 0, y_c(t) = 0 for t ≤ c and y_c(t) = (t − c)² for t ≥ c is a solution, and so is y ≡ 0. On [0, ∞) these are all the solutions (B1).
  - The Euler polygons from y₀ = 0 are identically 0, so the construction finds one solution out of a continuum. Peano asserts existence, not a selection rule.
  - Contrast with AN2.4's y^{1/3}, with one sentence: there too f is continuous and not Lipschitz at 0.
- **`cor-euler-unique-converges`.** If, in addition, the IVP has at most one solution on [t₀, t₀ + h] (for example, f Lipschitz in y, AN2.4), then the whole sequence y_N converges uniformly to it.
  - Proof: every subsequence of (y_N) is bounded and equicontinuous; by `thm-arzela-ascoli` it has a further subsequence converging uniformly, and the proof of `thm-peano` shows that limit is a solution, hence *the* solution. Conclude by `lem-subsequence-principle`.
  - This gives back the convergence of Euler's method seen for y′ = y in §01.
- **Remarks** (prose, each one or two sentences, no proof):
  - (a) maximal solutions exist and the blow-up alternative of AN2.4 (`thm-blowup-alternative`†) holds with Peano in place of Picard. This is C2.
  - (b) Kneser: for each t, the set of values y(t) over all solutions is connected. Stated only.
  - (c) In an infinite-dimensional Banach space, continuity of f does not suffice. Dieudonné's example in c₀ is named, not worked; the reason is that bounded sets are not relatively compact (`prp-unit-ball-not-compact`). Hook: FA.
  - (d) The same scheme (approximate, extract, pass to the limit) proves Montel's theorem (CA.5) and existence results in PDE.5.
- **Quick check.** For f(t, y) = 1 + y² on [0, 1] × [−1, 1] with y₀ = 0, find M and h. Does the true solution exist beyond h? *Answer:* M = 2 and h = ½. The solution is tan t, which exists on [0, π/2), so it goes beyond h, but Peano's h is only a guarantee.
- **Warning 1.** Peano is local. y′ = 1 + y² blows up at π/2.
- **Warning 2.** The proof gives a subsequence of the polygons. Without uniqueness, different subsequences or different schemes may converge to different solutions.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Compute h for: (i) f = t + y on [0, 2] × B̄(1, 1); (ii) f = sin(ty) on [0, 3] × B̄(0, 2); (iii) f ≡ 0. *Answers:* (i) M = max(t + y) = 2 + 2 = 4, h = min(2, ¼) = ¼; (ii) M ≤ 1, h = min(3, 2) = 2 (with M = 1, attained); (iii) h = a.
  - **B1.** Show that every solution of y′ = 2√|y|, y(0) = 0 on [0, ∞) is y ≡ 0 or some y_c. *Answer:* y′ ≥ 0 and y(0) = 0 give y ≥ 0. Let c = sup{t : y(t) = 0}, so y = 0 on [0, c] by monotonicity. If c = ∞, y ≡ 0. If c < ∞, then on (c, ∞) we have y > 0 and (√y)′ = 1, so √y = t − c by continuity at c, and y = (t − c)².
  - **B2.** Prove (i) of `lem-euler-polygon-properties` by induction on the node index. *Answer:* if |y_N(t_k) − y₀| ≤ M(t_k − t₀) ≤ b, then (t_k, y_N(t_k)) ∈ R, so |slope| ≤ M on [t_k, t_{k+1}], which gives the bound at t ∈ [t_k, t_{k+1}]. Here M(t − t₀) ≤ Mh ≤ b.
  - **C1 (exit check).** Prove Peano's theorem for n = 1 unaided from `thm-arzela-ascoli`, stating where compactness of R and continuity of f are each used. *Marking scheme outline:* polygons stay in R (2); equicontinuous and bounded (2); extraction (1); uniform convergence of the integrands, using uniform continuity of f on R (3); limit in the integral and the IVP equivalence (2).
  - **C2.** Show that a solution from `thm-peano` extends to a maximal interval [t₀, ω), and that if ω < ∞ the solution leaves every compact subset of the open domain. *Answer:* the argument of AN2.4's blow-up alternative, with Peano re-applied at points near ω in place of Picard; uniqueness is never used.
  - **C3 (find the error; required once per chapter).** The "proof": "The Euler polygons y_N are bounded and equicontinuous, so by Arzelà–Ascoli y_N → y uniformly, and y solves the IVP. Hence Euler's method converges for every continuous f." *Error:* Arzelà–Ascoli gives a convergent subsequence only. The conclusion holds under uniqueness (`cor-euler-unique-converges`), and the example y′ = 2√|y| shows that subsequences can matter for other schemes.
