**Part II, Metric Spaces and Several Variables.** Lifts convergence, completeness, compactness and continuity from ℝ to metric spaces and C(K), forced by concrete problems (ODE existence and uniqueness, polynomial approximation, typical continuous functions), then does calculus in ℝⁿ (inverse and implicit functions, the Riemann integral on ℝⁿ) and ends by abstracting open sets into topological spaces.

## Planned sections

1. The question: are polynomials dense in C[a,b]? Weierstrass's theorem by Bernstein polynomials, and by convolution with an approximate identity
2. The lattice form: a lattice of functions that interpolates at pairs of points is dense
3. The algebra form of Stone–Weierstrass: closed subalgebras are lattices (approximate |t| by polynomials); separating points and vanishing nowhere
4. Complex Stone–Weierstrass (self-adjoint algebras); trigonometric polynomials are dense in C(𝕋); separability of C(K) for compact K ⊂ ℝⁿ; necessity of each hypothesis
5. Nowhere dense, meager and comeager sets; the Baire category theorem for complete metric spaces
6. First Baire arguments: ℚ is not a G_δ; no function is continuous exactly on ℚ; complete metric spaces without isolated points are uncountable
7. Nowhere-differentiable functions: an explicit Weierstrass-type example, and the comeager set of nowhere-differentiable functions in C[0,1]; preview of the uniform boundedness principle (Part VI)

## Key examples and counterexamples

- even polynomials not dense in C[−1,1]; dense in C[0,1]
- polynomials in z not dense in C(closed unit disc) (hook to Part V)
- functions vanishing at a point: algebra that fails to vanish nowhere
- Thomae function: continuous exactly on the irrationals
- Weierstrass/van der Waerden nowhere-differentiable function
- a lattice that is not an algebra and an algebra that is not a lattice (GO.13)
