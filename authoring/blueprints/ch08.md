**Purpose.**

- **State the axiom of choice** (`ax-choice`) on the main line, at its first genuine use (countable unions), and show exactly which step needs it and which look-alike steps do not.
- Diagonal arguments: \( \{0, 1\}^{\nN} \), \( \powerset{X} \) (Cantor), ℝ via decimals (the school facts about decimals stated, the \( 0.4999\ldots = 0.5 \) issue handled by the choice of digits).
- Compare sizes with injections; prove Schröder–Bernstein; show every infinite set contains a countably infinite one (choice again) and is equinumerous with a proper subset.
- Close Part 0 with **Gate 0**: a two-hour, closed-book, six-problem examination with a marking scheme per problem, a pass line and a remediation map.
- The reader leaves able to (i) prove a set countable by a surjection from ℕ or an injection into ℕ, choosing the cheaper; (ii) run a diagonal argument and say what makes the diagonal element new; (iii) point to the line of a proof that uses choice, and say when choice is avoidable.

**Signature moves** (`index.md`, "Named moves"):
- **List it.** To show \( A \) countable, give a surjection \( \nN \to A \) (or an injection \( A \to \nN \)); repetitions are free.

- **Injections both ways.** To show \( \card{A} = \card{B} \), find an injection each way and invoke Schröder–Bernstein; do not hunt for the bijection.
- **Name the choice.** When a proof picks one object for each of infinitely many indices with no rule, say that `ax-choice` is used, at that line.

## index.md

- **Title:** Cardinality: Countable and Uncountable Sets.
- **Story.** Are there more rational numbers than natural numbers? The rationals are dense and the naturals are spread out, and yet the answer is no: they can be listed. Are there more reals than rationals? Yes, and no list, however clever, contains them all. This chapter makes "more" exact, proves both answers, and shows that there is no largest size at all (Cantor). On the way it meets a principle that ordinary logic does not supply: making infinitely many arbitrary choices at once. The chapter states it as the axiom of choice and shows the one step that needs it. ## 01-finite-sets.md — Equinumerous Sets and Finite Sets

- **Hook (an everyday word made exact).** Two piles of coins have the same number when we can pair them off, before we can count. Pairing is a bijection.
- **Definitions.** `def-equinumerous` (major for Part 0: full template; slogan *same size means there is a pairing*): \( \card{A} = \card{B} \) iff there is a bijection \( A \to B \). `prp-equinumerous-properties`: reflexive (\( \id \)), symmetric (`@thm-bijection-iff-inverse`), transitive (`@thm-composition-injective-surjective`). **Say why this is not called an equivalence relation**: there is no set of all sets for it to live on (`@thm-russell`, pointing back); it behaves like one. `def-finite`: \( A \) is finite with \( n \) elements if \( \card{A} = \card{\{1, \dots, n\}} \), and \( \emptyset \) is finite with \( 0 \) elements. `def-infinite`: not finite.
- **`thm-pigeonhole`** (self-explanation prompt): for \( m, n \in \nN \) with \( m > n \) there is no injection \( \{1, \dots, m\} \to \{1, \dots, n\} \). Induction on \( n \) (`@ax-induction`, pointed back to): \( n = 1 \): two points share the value \( 1 \). Step: given an injection \( f \colon \{1, \dots, m\} \to \{1, \dots, n + 1\} \) with \( m > n + 1 \), if \( n + 1 \) is not a value, \( f \) maps into \( \{1, \dots, n\} \); otherwise \( f(j) = n + 1 \) for exactly one \( j \), and \( g \) on \( \{1, \dots, m - 1\} \) defined by \( g(i) = f(i) \) for \( i \ne j \) and \( g(j) = f(m) \) (if \( j \ne m \)) is an injection into \( \{1, \dots, n\} \) with \( m - 1 > n \) (`@lem-naturals-arithmetic` (c)). The construction of \( g \) is the whole proof: write both cases.
- **Corollaries.** `cor-finite-size-unique`: if \( A \) has \( m \) elements and \( n \) elements, then \( m = n \) — so "the number of elements" is well defined (this is the well-definedness step of the explain template). `prp-subset-finite`: a subset of a set with \( n \) elements is finite with at most \( n \) elements, and fewer if it is proper (induction on \( n \)). `prp-disjoint-union-count`: disjoint finite sets with \( m \) and \( n \) elements have a union with \( m + n \) elements (shift \( \{1, \dots, n\} \) to \( \{m + 1, \dots, m + n\} \)). `prp-power-set-count` (**pays** Chapter 6's pointer): a set with \( n \) elements has \( 2^n \) subsets (induction: \( \powerset{X \cup \{a\}} \) is the disjoint union of \( \powerset{X} \) and \( \{ S \cup \{a\} : S \subseteq X \} \)). `thm-finite-no-proper-copy`: a finite set is not equinumerous with any proper subset (from `prp-subset-finite` and `cor-finite-size-unique`).
- **`exm-hilbert-hotel`:** \( n \mapsto 2n \) is a bijection \( \nN \to 2\nN \), and \( 2\nN \subsetneq \nN \); so ℕ is infinite (`thm-finite-no-proper-copy`). The finite intuition "a proper part is smaller" fails exactly for infinite sets; §06 proves it fails for **every** infinite set.
- **Remark.** With `def-finite`, a non-empty finite set is \( \{x_1, \dots, x_n\} \), so it has a maximum when its elements are real (`@prp-finite-max`): the form Chapter 11 §02 uses (prose pointer).
- **Warning.** Equinumerous is not "one is a subset of the other", and \( A \subsetneq B \) does not give \( \card{A} < \card{B} \) for infinite sets (Hilbert's hotel). Also: "finite" means "in bijection with some \( \{1, \dots, n\} \)", not "bounded" (\( \{ 1/n : n \in \nN \} \) is bounded and infinite; to prove it infinite, use the proper-subset criterion with \( 1/n \mapsto 1/(n+1) \)).
- **Quick check.** Is \( n \mapsto n + 1 \) a bijection \( \nN \to \nN \setminus \{1\} \)? What does that show? (Yes; ℕ is infinite, again.)
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1:** (a) define "\( A \) is finite" and negate it in symbols; (b) true or false with reason: a subset of an infinite set is infinite; a superset of an infinite set is infinite; (c) how many injections \( \{1, 2, 3\} \to \{1, 2\} \) are there? (\( 0 \), by `thm-pigeonhole`); (d) the pigeonhole principle for a function \( f \colon A \to B \) between finite sets with more elements in \( A \): what does it guarantee?
  - **B1:** give explicit bijections \( \nN \to \nN \setminus \{1, 2, 3\} \), \( \nN \to \{ n \in \nN : n \ge 5 \} \) and \( \nN \to \{ 2n + 1 : n \in \nN \} \) (the odd naturals), and check that each is a bijection (inverse maps written out). **B2:** among any \( 6 \) integers, two have the same remainder on division by \( 5 \) (`@thm-division-algorithm`, `thm-pigeonhole`); among any \( 5 \) points of \( \{1, \dots, 8\} \), two sum to \( 9 \). **B3:** prove that a finite union of finite sets is finite (induction on the number of sets, from `prp-disjoint-union-count` and `prp-subset-finite`).
  - **C1:** prove that \( A \) is infinite iff for every \( n \in \nN \) there is an injection \( \{1, \dots, n\} \to A \). (\( \Leftarrow \) uses `thm-pigeonhole`; \( \Rightarrow \) is an induction on \( n \) that extends an injection by one point of \( A \) outside its finite image — one \( \exists \)E per step, **no** choice: say why — each step makes one choice, and the statement is about each \( n \) separately.) **C2:** if \( A \) has \( m \) elements and \( B \) has \( n \), then \( A \times B \) has \( mn \) elements and the set of functions \( B^A \) has \( n^m \) elements.

## 02-countable-sets.md — Countable Sets

- **Hook.** Can we list the rationals as \( q_1, q_2, q_3, \dots \) so that every rational appears? Density makes it look impossible: between any two there is a third. And yet.
- **`def-countable`** (major for Part 0: full template; slogan *countable means listable, repetitions allowed*): \( A \) is **countable** if \( A = \emptyset \) or there is a surjection \( \nN \to A \) — that is, \( A = \{ f(1), f(2), \dots \} \) for some \( f \colon \nN \to A \). This is the definition Chapter 11 already quoted: match its wording (the promise). `def-countably-infinite`: \( \card{A} = \card{\nN} \). **Uncountable** = not countable. Why repetitions are allowed: finite sets are then countable with no special case, and closure proofs are shorter.
- **`lem-subsets-of-naturals`:** every subset \( S \subseteq \nN \) is finite or countably infinite. If \( S \) is infinite, list it in increasing order: \( s_1 = \min S \), \( s_{n+1} = \min\{ s \in S : s > s_n \} \) (`@thm-well-ordering`; the set is non-empty because \( S \cap \{1, \dots, s_n\} \) is finite by `prp-subset-finite` while \( S \) is not) — a definition by `@thm-recursion`. Then \( n \mapsto s_n \) is strictly increasing, hence injective, and onto \( S \): for \( t \in S \), take the least \( n \) with \( s_n \ge t \) (exists since \( s_n \ge n \), by induction) and show \( s_n = t \).
- **`thm-countable-characterizations`:** for any set \( A \), TFAE: (i) \( A \) is countable; (ii) there is an injection \( A \to \nN \); (iii) \( A \) is finite or countably infinite. (i) \( \Rightarrow \) (ii): for a surjection \( g \colon \nN \to A \), \( h(a) \coloneqq \min g^{-1}(\{a\}) \) (`@thm-well-ordering`; the preimage is non-empty since \( g \) is onto) — **no choice is needed, because ℕ is well-ordered**: say so, and contrast with §03. (ii) \( \Rightarrow \) (iii): \( A \) is equinumerous with \( h(A) \subseteq \nN \); apply `lem-subsets-of-naturals`. (iii) \( \Rightarrow \) (i): a bijection \( \{1, \dots, n\} \to A \) extended by a constant, or a bijection \( \nN \to A \). The empty set passes all three (the empty function is an injection).
- **Closure.** `prp-countable-subset` (from (ii): restrict the injection). `prp-countable-image` (compose surjections). `prp-union-two-countable` (interleave: \( k(2n - 1) = g(n) \), \( k(2n) = h(n) \), using `@thm-division-algorithm` with divisor \( 2 \); the empty cases first).
- **`thm-product-countable`** (*Try it first*): \( \nN \times \nN \) is countable. Proof: \( (m, n) \mapsto 2^m 3^n \) is injective (the seven lines of the §02 B2 ordering item: parity, and strict monotonicity of \( 3^k \), both by induction; **no unique factorization**), then `thm-countable-characterizations`. Aftermath: the picture of the diagonal-by-diagonal listing, in words, as an alternative that the reader may prefer, with one "Alternatively," paragraph and no formula. `cor-product-countable`: \( A \times B \) is countable if \( A \) and \( B \) are (compose \( \nN \to \nN \times \nN \to A \times B \)); and \( A_1 \times \dots \times A_k \) by induction on \( k \).
- **`thm-integers-rationals-countable`** (**pays** the Chapter 11 promise): ℤ is countable (the bijection \( 1 \mapsto 0 \), \( 2k \mapsto k \), \( 2k + 1 \mapsto -k \), via `@thm-division-algorithm`); ℚ is countable, as the image of \( \nZ \times \nN \) under \( (m, n) \mapsto m/n \) (ℚ is defined as that set of quotients, Chapter 7 §01's standing description).
- **Warning `exm-rows-first-listing` (false conclusion).** "List \( \nN \times \nN \) as \( (1, 1), (1, 2), (1, 3), \dots \), then \( (2, 1), (2, 2), \dots \)": this is not a function on ℕ onto \( \nN \times \nN \); \( (2, 1) \) is never given an index (every \( n \in \nN \) is used up by the first row). A list must give every entry a **finite** position.

## 03-countable-unions-and-choice.md — Countable Unions and the Axiom of Choice

- **Hook (a goal theorem first).** \( A_1, A_2, A_3, \dots \) are countable. Is \( \bigcup_n A_n \) countable? The natural proof: take a list of each \( A_n \) and interleave them as in \( \nN \times \nN \). Where does "take a list of each" come from?
- **The obstacle, in rules.** "\( A_n \) is countable" is an \( \exists \) (a surjection exists); \( \exists \)E names **one** surjection for **one** \( n \) (`@def-rules-existential`, pointed back to). For finitely many \( n \), finitely many \( \exists \)E's; for all \( n \in \nN \) at once there is no derivation rule that does it, and recursion does not help, because the \( n \)-th list is not determined by the earlier ones. So we add a principle.
- **`ax-choice`** (full template for a major notion: situate, hook above, slogan *from any family of non-empty sets one may pick one element from each set at once*, statement, paraphrase, why an axiom, examples, non-example, warning). **Statement:** for every set \( I \) and every family \( (A_i)_{i \in I} \) of **non-empty** sets there is a function \( c \colon I \to \bigcup_{i \in I} A_i \) with \( c(i) \in A_i \) for every \( i \in I \) (a **choice function**). Status, in one paragraph: it is independent of the other rules of set formation (Gödel 1938, Cohen 1963; names and dates only), its equivalents (Zorn's lemma, the well-ordering theorem) are in the optional Chapter 9 (prose pointer), and the main line uses it openly: here; for right inverses; for the Vitali set (Chapter 43), Hahn–Banach (Chapter 67, via Zorn), Tychonoff (Chapter 69) (prose pointers). Remark: Chapter 11's approximation of a supremum by a sequence (its `cor-sup-approximation`, prose pointer) is an instance with \( I = \nN \). **When choice is not needed:** \( I \) finite (finitely many \( \exists \)E: C1 makes this an induction); a **rule** that picks (the least element of a set of naturals; the non-negative square root; the first interval of a bisection). This list is the point of the section: a reader must be able to tell the cases apart.
- **`thm-surjection-right-inverse`** (**pays** Chapter 6 §05's promise): every surjection \( f \colon X \to Y \) has a right inverse. Proof: the family \( (f^{-1}(\{y\}))_{y \in Y} \) consists of non-empty sets; a choice function \( c \) satisfies \( f(c(y)) = y \). With `@prp-right-inverse-surjective`: \( f \) is surjective iff it has a right inverse. Remark (prose): this statement is in fact equivalent to `ax-choice` (optional Chapter 9).
- **`thm-countable-union`** (key proof): if \( A_n \) is countable for every \( n \in \nN \), then \( \bigcup_{n \in \nN} A_n \) is countable; likewise for a family indexed by any countable set. Proof: let \( J = \{ n : A_n \ne \emptyset \} \); if \( J = \emptyset \) the union is empty. For \( n \in J \), the set \( S_n \) of surjections \( \nN \to A_n \) is non-empty; **by `ax-choice`** there is \( c \) with \( c(n) \in S_n \) for every \( n \in J \) — the one line that uses choice, tagged. Then \( (n, m) \mapsto c(n)(m) \) maps \( J \times \nN \) onto the union, and \( J \times \nN \) is countable and non-empty (`prp-countable-subset`, `cor-product-countable`), so compose with a surjection \( \nN \to J \times \nN \). *Before reading on* prompt. **Idea:** the \( \nN \times \nN \) listing of §02, with the lists supplied by choice.
- **`exm-choice-free-unions`:** \( \nQ = \bigcup_{n \in \nN} \{ m/n : m \in \nZ \} \) and \( \nZ \times \nN \): the lists are given by formulas, and no choice is used; the ℚ of §02 needed none. Say, as a checkable meta-claim, that `thm-integers-rationals-countable`'s proof has no line invoking `ax-choice`.
- **`thm-algebraic-countable`:** the set of **algebraic** numbers (real roots of non-zero polynomials with integer coefficients) is countable. Polynomials of degree at most \( d \) with integer coefficients are the image of \( \nZ^{d+1} \) (countable by `cor-product-countable`); each non-zero one has at most \( d \) real roots (school fact, ledgered: the factor theorem); so the algebraic numbers are a countable union (over polynomials, a countable index set) of finite sets: `thm-countable-union`. Remark: here choice can be avoided, since a finite set of reals can be listed in increasing order by a rule (`@prp-finite-max` repeatedly); C2 asks for it. The transcendental numbers exist: §04 (`cor-transcendental-exist`, after ℝ is shown uncountable).
- **Warning.** Choice is not "picking an element": a single \( \exists \)E does that. It is picking **one element from each of infinitely many sets with no rule**. Second warning: the choice in `thm-countable-union` is of the **lists**, not of preimages: a surjection from ℕ gives least preimages by a rule (§02).
- **Quick check.** Does `thm-surjection-right-inverse` need choice for \( f \colon \nZ \to \nN \), \( f(k) = \abs{k} \)? (No: take the non-negative preimage, a rule.)

- **Decimals.** `def-decimal-expansion`: a sequence of digits \( d_1, d_2, \dots \in \{0, \dots, 9\} \) is a **decimal expansion** of \( x \in [0, 1] \), written \( x = 0.d_1 d_2 d_3 \ldots \), if for every \( n \), \( t_n \le x \le t_n + 10^{-n} \), where \( t_n \coloneqq \sum_{k=1}^n d_k 10^{-k} \) (`@def-sum-product`: a finite sum, so no series is needed).   - **(D2)** every digit sequence is a decimal expansion of some \( x \in [0, 1] \);

## 05-comparing-cardinalities.md — Comparing Sizes: the Schröder–Bernstein Theorem

- **Hook.** \( (0, 1) \subseteq [0, 1] \subseteq \nR \): are they the same size? Writing down bijections is fiddly; injections are easy.
- **`def-card-le`:** \( \card{A} \le \card{B} \) iff there is an injection \( A \to B \); \( \card{A} < \card{B} \) iff \( \card{A} \le \card{B} \) and not \( \card{A} = \card{B} \). `prp-card-le-preorder`: reflexive and transitive. `prp-card-le-surjection`: for \( A \ne \emptyset \), \( \card{A} \le \card{B} \) iff there is a surjection \( B \to A \) — \( (\Rightarrow) \) by `@thm-left-inverse-iff-injective` (a left inverse is onto), \( (\Leftarrow) \) by `thm-surjection-right-inverse`, **using choice** (flag it). Examples: \( \card{\nN} < \card{\powerset{\nN}} \) (`thm-cantor`); countable means \( \card{A} \le \card{\nN} \) (`thm-countable-characterizations`).
- **`thm-schroder-bernstein`** (key proof): if \( \card{A} \le \card{B} \) and \( \card{B} \le \card{A} \), then \( \card{A} = \card{B} \). Idea with a **Step roadmap** and a picture of chains described in words: points of \( A \) not hit by \( g \) must be sent by \( f \); so must everything reached from them by \( g \circ f \); everything else can be sent back by \( g^{-1} \). ① \( C_1 \coloneqq A \setminus g(B) \), \( C_{n+1} \coloneqq g(f(C_n)) \) (`@thm-recursion` with values in \( \powerset{A} \)), \( C \coloneqq \bigcup_n C_n \); ② \( h(a) \coloneqq f(a) \) for \( a \in C \), and \( h(a) \coloneqq \) the unique \( b \) with \( g(b) = a \) for \( a \notin C \) (it exists: \( a \notin C_1 \) means \( a \in g(B) \); unique by injectivity of \( g \)); ③ **Claim:** \( h \) injective (the mixed case: \( f(a) = b \), \( g(b) = a' \), \( a \in C_n \) gives \( a' \in C_{n+1} \), a contradiction); ④ **Claim:** \( h \) onto (for \( b \in B \): if \( g(b) \notin C \), \( h(g(b)) = b \); if \( g(b) \in C \), it lies in some \( C_{n+1} \) since \( g(b) \notin C_1 \), so \( g(b) = g(f(a)) \) with \( a \in C_n \), and \( b = f(a) = h(a) \)). *Before reading on* prompt. Remark: no choice is used (every value is given by a rule); the case split "\( a \in C \) or not" is excluded middle (Chapter 5 §07, prose pointer), one clause.
- **Examples.** `exm-closed-open-interval`: \( [0, 1] \) and \( (0, 1) \) are equinumerous, twice: by `thm-schroder-bernstein` (the inclusion and \( x \mapsto x/2 + 1/4 \)), and by the explicit map \( \varphi(0) = \frac12 \), \( \varphi(1/n) = 1/(n + 2) \) for \( n \in \nN \), \( \varphi(x) = x \) otherwise — a Hilbert's hotel inside \( [0, 1] \) (check it is a bijection: the points \( 0, 1, \frac12, \frac13, \dots \) go onto \( \frac12, \frac13, \frac14, \dots \), everything else is fixed). `exm-reals-open-interval`: \( x \mapsto x/(1 + \abs{x}) \) is a bijection \( \nR \to (-1, 1) \) with inverse \( y \mapsto y/(1 - \abs{y}) \) (verified inline; Chapter 6 §05 B3 did the same computation, not cited), then \( y \mapsto (y + 1)/2 \); so every interval with more than one point is equinumerous with ℝ (combine with S–B for closed and half-open ones).
- **Warning.** Injections both ways do not make either injection a bijection: \( n \mapsto 2n \) both ways on ℕ. The theorem builds a **new** map.
- **Remark (prose only).** Are any two sets comparable? That needs Zorn's lemma (optional Chapter 9). The book never needs it.
- **Quick check.** Give injections both ways between \( \nN \) and \( \nN \times \nN \). (\( n \mapsto (n, 1) \); \( (m, n) \mapsto 2^m 3^n \).)
- **Exercises.**

## 06-infinite-sets.md — Infinite Sets

- **Hook.** ℕ is the smallest infinite set? Every infinite set should contain a copy of ℕ: keep picking new elements. That "keep picking" is choice again, in a stronger form.
- **`thm-infinite-countable-subset`:** every infinite set \( X \) has a countably infinite subset. Proof: by `ax-choice` applied to the family \( (S)_{S \in \powerset{X} \setminus \{\emptyset\}} \), fix a choice function \( c \) (**choice**, flagged); define \( F_0 \coloneqq \emptyset \), \( F_{n} \coloneqq F_{n-1} \cup \{ c(X \setminus F_{n-1}) \} \) by `@thm-recursion` (with \( g(n, F) = F \cup \{ c(X \setminus F) \} \) if \( F \ne X \), and \( F \) otherwise); by induction \( F_n \) has exactly \( n \) elements, so \( F_n \ne X \) (\( X \) is infinite) and the new element is new; \( n \mapsto \) the element of \( F_n \setminus F_{n-1} \) is an injection \( \nN \to X \). **Contrast** (a remark): Chapter 11 §07's intervals are chosen by an explicit rule, and needed no choice; here no rule is available for an arbitrary \( X \), so the choice function **is** the rule.
- **`thm-infinite-iff-proper-subset`:** a set is infinite iff it is equinumerous with a proper subset of itself. \( (\Leftarrow) \) is `thm-finite-no-proper-copy`. \( (\Rightarrow) \): take \( \{x_1, x_2, \dots\} \) from `thm-infinite-countable-subset` (distinct), send \( x_n \mapsto x_{n+1} \) and fix everything else: a bijection \( X \to X \setminus \{x_1\} \) (the hotel of `exm-hilbert-hotel` inside \( X \)). Aftermath: the curriculum's Stage 0 example (d), done.
- **Consequences.** For every infinite \( X \), \( \card{\nN} \le \card{X} \): ℕ is the smallest infinite size. Countable sets are exactly those of size at most \( \card{\nN} \).

- **`exr-gate-0-p1`** [P1: core] (20 min) — *formalize and negate a three-quantifier statement.* Say that \( f \colon \nR \to \nR \) has **property L** if for every \( x \in \nR \) there is \( \delta > 0 \) such that for every \( y \in \nR \) with \( \abs{y - x} < \delta \), \( f(y) \ge f(x) \). (a) Write property L in symbols with bounded quantifiers. (b) Write its negation with no \( \neg \) in front of a quantifier, a connective or a parenthesis. (c) Prove that \( f(x) = x^2 \) does not have property L. (d) Prove that \( \ind_{(0, \infty)} \) has property L, and that it does **not** have the property obtained by moving "there is \( \delta > 0 \)" to the front.
  - *Intended answer.* (b) \( \exists x \in \nR\ \forall \delta > 0\ \exists y \in \nR\ (\abs{y - x} < \delta \wedge f(y) < f(x)) \). (c) \( x = 1 \); for \( \delta > 0 \), \( y = 1 - \min\{\delta, 1\}/2 \in [\frac12, 1) \), so \( \abs{y - 1} < \delta \) and \( y^2 < 1 \). (d) For \( x > 0 \), \( \delta = x \); for \( x \le 0 \), any \( \delta \) (as \( f(x) = 0 \le f(y) \)); \( \delta \) **depends on \( x \)**. The swapped property fails: given \( \delta \), take \( x = \delta/4 \), \( y = -\delta/4 \).
  - *Marking scheme.* (a) 2; (b) 3 (bounds kept: \( \forall \delta > 0 \), not \( \exists \delta \le 0 \); the implication negated to a conjunction); (c) 2 (a witness \( x \) fixed **before** \( \delta \), and \( y \) given as a function of \( \delta \)); (d) 3 (the case split for L; the swap refuted with \( x \) depending on \( \delta \)). Fatal: a negation that keeps "\( \to \)"; \( y \) chosen before \( \delta \); a single \( \delta \) for all \( x \) in (d)'s first half.
- **`exr-gate-0-p2`** [P2: core] (20 min) — *turn a Fitch derivation into prose.* Given a printed derivation (≤ 18 lines, fitch.sty) of \( \forall z\, \exists y\, G(y, z),\ \forall y\, \exists x\, F(x, y),\ \forall x \forall y \forall z\, ((F(x, y) \wedge G(y, z)) \to H(x, z)) \vdash \forall z\, \exists x\, H(x, z) \), with the justifications of four lines left blank (a \( \forall \)E, the inner \( \exists \)E, the \( \exists \)I, the final \( \forall \)I). (a) Fill in the four justifications. (b) Explain why the \( \exists \)I line sits inside both \( \exists \)E boxes, and why each \( \exists \)E box may close (the side condition: its last line contains neither \( x_0 \) nor \( y_0 \)). (c) With \( F(x, y) \): "\( f(x) = y \)", \( G(y, z) \): "\( g(y) = z \)", \( H(x, z) \): "\( g(f(x)) = z \)", write the plain-prose proof that the composite of surjections is surjective that this derivation encodes, one sentence per box.
  - *Marking scheme.* (a) 4 (one per blank: the \( \forall \)E with its instance term, the inner \( \exists \)E with its cited line and box, the \( \exists \)I, the final \( \forall \)I with its box); (b) 2 (the fresh-name condition of \( \exists \)E named, and why \( x_0, y_0 \) may not leave their boxes); (c) 4 ("let \( z \in Z \)", "choose \( y \) with \( g(y) = z \)", "choose \( x \) with \( f(x) = y \)", "then \( g(f(x)) = z \)", in that order, each carrying its box). Fatal: "let \( x \) be arbitrary" for an \( \exists \)E; prose in which \( y \) is chosen before \( z \).
- **`exr-gate-0-p3`** [P3] (20 min) — *find the error and name the rule.* Two short proofs; for each: is the conclusion true? does the argument prove it? if not, the exact line, the rule broken (with its entry in `@exm-not-allowed-catalogue`), and a counterexample to the illegal step.
  - (a) (**true conclusion**) "Claim: \( n^2 + n \) is even for every \( n \in \nN \). Proof: \( 1^2 + 1 = 2 \) is even. Assume that \( n^2 + n \) is even for all \( n \in \nN \). Then \( (n + 1)^2 + (n + 1) = (n^2 + n) + 2(n + 1) \) is even. By induction, the claim holds." Intended: conclusion true (e.g. \( n(n + 1) \) has an even factor: `@thm-division-algorithm`); the line "assume … for all \( n \)" assumes the conclusion: (N9), circular; the step should be a \( \to \)I box for one arbitrary \( n \). Counterexample to the illegal inference "from \( P(1) \) and (\( \forall n\, P(n) \)) \( \to \) (\( \forall n\, P(n + 1) \)), conclude \( \forall n\, P(n) \)": \( P(n) \): "\( n \le 1 \)" satisfies both premises (the second vacuously) and \( \forall n\, P(n) \) is false.
  - (b) (**false conclusion**) "Claim: if \( A_n \subseteq \nR \) is bounded above for every \( n \in \nN \), then \( \bigcup_n A_n \) is bounded above. Proof: for each \( n \) let \( M_n \) be an upper bound of \( A_n \). Let \( M \) be a number larger than every \( M_n \). Then \( M \) bounds the union." Intended: false (\( A_n = \{n\} \)); the line "let \( M \) be larger than every \( M_n \)" turns \( \forall n\, \exists M \) into \( \exists M\, \forall n \): (N2), the quantifier swap refuted by `@prp-exists-forall-swap`'s converse failing; counterexample to the step: \( M_n = n \). (It is legitimate for finitely many \( n \): a maximum, `@prp-finite-max`.)

(b) 5: false, with the witness (1); the line (2); the quantifier swap named (1); the finite case noted or the counterexample to the step (1). Fatal: in (a), calling the argument correct; in (b), locating the error in the choice of the \( M_n \). The solution says that choosing an \( M_n \) for every \( n \) at once is a legitimate use of `ax-choice` (flagged), and that the flaw is the next line.
- **`exr-gate-0-p4`** [P4: core] (20 min) — *an induction proof.* Let \( a_1 = 1 \), \( a_2 = 3 \) and \( a_{n+2} = 3a_{n+1} - 2a_n \) for \( n \in \nN \). (a) Say which result guarantees that this defines a sequence. (b) Prove \( a_n = 2^n - 1 \) for every \( n \in \nN \). (c) If instead \( a_2 = 4 \), compute \( a_3 \) and say exactly which line of your proof in (b) fails.

- **`exr-gate-0-p5`** [P5: core] (20 min) — *a preimage identity.* Let \( f \colon X \to Y \).   - *Intended answer.* (a) A chain of "iff"s, or `@thm-preimage-operations` (complement and union) followed by `@thm-de-morgan-families`. - **`exr-gate-0-p6`** [P6] (20 min) — *a diagonal argument.* Let \( S \) be the set of sequences \( (a_n) \) of natural numbers with \( a_n \le n \) for every \( n \). (a) Prove that \( S \) is uncountable. (b) Let \( E \subseteq S \) consist of the sequences in \( S \) that are eventually constant. Prove that \( E \) is countable, and say why running your construction from (a) on a list of \( E \) produces no contradiction.
  - *Intended answer.* (a) The naive diagonal fails at \( n = 1 \) (\( a_1 = 1 \) is forced); shift it: given \( f_1, f_2, \dots \in S \), let \( g(1) = 1 \) and \( g(n + 1) = 1 \) if \( f_n(n + 1) \ne 1 \), \( g(n + 1) = 2 \) otherwise; \( g \in S \) and \( g \ne f_n \) at place \( n + 1 \). (b) \( E = \bigcup_{N} E_N \), \( E_N \) = sequences in \( S \) constant from \( N \) on, each finite (determined by \( a_1, \dots, a_N \), at most \( N! \) choices); a countable union of finite sets (`thm-countable-union`; choice avoidable by listing each \( E_N \) in lexicographic order). The diagonal of a list of \( E \) lies in \( S \) and not on the list, hence **not in \( E \)**: no contradiction.
  - *Marking scheme.* (a) 6: an arbitrary list (1); the diagonal element defined so that it lies in \( S \) — the \( n = 1 \) issue handled (2); differs from every \( f_n \) (2); conclusion, including the "empty or image of ℕ" form of countable (1). Fatal: a diagonal element not in \( S \) (e.g. \( g(n) = f_n(n) + 1 \), which can exceed \( n \)); proving (a) for one particular list.
