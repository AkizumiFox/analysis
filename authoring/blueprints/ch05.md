# Chapter 5: Sets, Functions, Images and Preimages

Directory: `src/ch05-sets-and-functions/`. State: not started.

**Part 0, Foundations: Logic, Sets, Numbers.** Teaches the grammar of proof (natural deduction for connectives, its truth-table semantics with soundness and completeness, the quantifier and equality rules, and the passage from derivations to mathematical prose), then sets, functions, relations, induction and cardinality as the main-line bridge, and, beside the main line, builds ℕ, ℤ, ℚ and ℝ and proves the complete ordered field unique; forced by a reader who has never written a proof and by Part I's decision to start from the least upper bound axiom.

*Naive but careful set theory; the ZF axioms themselves are in the optional chapter zf-and-natural-numbers. Relations are introduced only as far as functions need (Velleman §4.1–4.2, Hammack §11.1); equivalence relations and orders come next chapter. ℕ, ℤ, ℚ, ℝ are used as familiar sets in examples; no main-line proof depends on their construction (decisions 4).*

**Page budget:** about 50 source-book pages.

**Prerequisites (from the DAG):** Chapter 1 (Propositional Logic and Natural Deduction), Chapter 3 (Predicate Logic and the Quantifier Rules), Chapter 4 (From Derivations to Mathematical Prose).

## Planned sections

1. Sets, membership, extensionality; proving A ⊆ B and A = B as ∀ and ↔ proofs; Russell's paradox as the reason set formation is restricted
2. Operations: union, intersection, difference, complement, power set; indexed families and De Morgan's laws
3. Ordered pairs, Cartesian products, relations as subsets of a product
4. Functions as relations with the ∀∃! property; composition; injective, surjective, bijective
5. Inverses: left inverses and injectivity, right inverses and surjectivity (choice flagged), two-sided inverses and bijections
6. Images and preimages: preimages respect all set operations, images only unions

## Must prove

- De Morgan's laws for arbitrary families
- f⁻¹ preserves arbitrary unions, intersections and complements
- f(⋃Aᵢ) = ⋃f(Aᵢ), f(A∩B) ⊆ f(A)∩f(B), with equality for all A, B iff f is injective
- A ⊆ f⁻¹(f(A)) and f(f⁻¹(B)) ⊆ B, with equality conditions
- composites of injections (surjections) are injections (surjections)
- f has a left inverse iff injective (nonempty domain); f has a right inverse iff surjective (flag the use of choice)
- Russell's paradox: there is no set of all sets

## Key examples and counterexamples

- f(x) = x², A = [−1,0], B = [0,1]: f(A∩B) = {0} ⊊ [0,1] = f(A)∩f(B)
- the empty function; P(∅) and P({∅})
- x ↦ x² on ℝ, [0,∞) → ℝ, [0,∞) → [0,∞): same rule, different injectivity/surjectivity

## Exit checks (C-exercises)

- Prove f⁻¹(⋂Aᵢ) = ⋂f⁻¹(Aᵢ), and give a function for which f(A∩B) ≠ f(A)∩f(B)
- Prove that g∘f injective implies f injective, and find an example where g is not
