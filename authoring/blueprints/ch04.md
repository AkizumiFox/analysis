# Chapter 4: Equivalence Relations, Orders, and Induction

Directory: `src/ch04-relations-and-induction/`. State: not started.

**Part 0, Foundations: Logic, Sets, Numbers.** Teaches the grammar of proof (natural deduction for connectives, quantifiers and equality), then sets, functions, relations, induction and cardinality as the main-line bridge, and, beside the main line, builds ℕ, ℤ, ℚ and ℝ and proves the complete ordered field unique; forced by a reader who has never written a proof and by Part I's decision to start from the least upper bound axiom.

*ℕ with induction and the well-ordering principle is taken as given here; its construction is optional (zf-and-natural-numbers). The orders section plants upper bounds and greatest elements so that Part I ch 1 (sup, LUB axiom) can recall them.*

**Page budget:** about 48 source-book pages.

**Prerequisites (from the DAG):** Chapter 1 (The Grammar of Proof I: Connectives and Natural Deduction), Chapter 2 (The Grammar of Proof II: Quantifiers, Equality, and What Is Not Allowed), Chapter 3 (Sets, Functions, Images and Preimages).

## Planned sections

1. Properties of relations: reflexive, symmetric, antisymmetric, transitive
2. Equivalence relations, equivalence classes, partitions, and the correspondence between them; quotient sets (ℤ/n)
3. Partial and total orders; upper bounds, maximal and greatest elements (a first look at sup)
4. The well-ordering principle of ℕ, induction and strong induction, and why they are equivalent
5. Recursive definitions: sequences, finite sums and products, n!
6. Induction as a proof skeleton, including smallest counterexample, and how induction proofs go wrong

## Must prove

- the equivalence classes of an equivalence relation partition the set, and every partition comes from exactly one equivalence relation
- well-ordering ⇒ induction ⇒ strong induction (and back)
- Bernoulli's inequality (1 + x)ⁿ ≥ 1 + nx for x ≥ −1
- every integer n ≥ 2 has a prime factor (strong induction)
- 1 + 2 + ⋯ + n = n(n+1)/2 and the geometric sum formula

## Key examples and counterexamples

- congruence mod n and ℤ/n
- ⊆ on P(X) is a partial but not total order; a set with a maximal element that is not greatest
- the 'all horses are the same colour' induction, error located at n = 1 → 2

## Exit checks (C-exercises)

- Prove that equivalence classes partition the set
- Find the error in a flawed induction proof and name the step that fails
