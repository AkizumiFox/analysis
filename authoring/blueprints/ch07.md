## 01-naturals-and-well-ordering.md — The Natural Numbers and Well-Ordering

- **Opening frame.** Point back to `@ax-induction` and `@thm-strong-induction` of Chapter 3 in one sentence each. State the standing description (N1), (N2) and the status of ℝ's arithmetic. Then what the section does: derive from these the facts Chapter 3 took from school, then well-ordering.
- **`prp-induction-set-form`:** if \( S \subseteq \nN \), \( 1 \in S \), and \( n \in S \Rightarrow n + 1 \in S \), then \( S = \nN \). Two lines: `@ax-induction` for the property "\( n \in S \)". Remark: this is the form "ℕ is contained in every inductive set" that Chapter 11 uses (prose pointer).
- **`lem-naturals-arithmetic`.** For all \( m, n \in \nN \): (a) \( n \ge 1 \); (b) \( m + n \in \nN \) and \( mn \in \nN \); (c) if \( n \ne 1 \), then \( n - 1 \in \nN \); (d) if \( m < n \), then \( n - m \in \nN \); (e) there is no \( k \in \nN \) with \( n < k < n + 1 \); equivalently, \( m < n \) implies \( m + 1 \le n \). Each part is an induction whose property is **written out** before the base case:
  - (a): property \( n \ge 1 \); the step uses (N2) and \( n + 1 > n \).
  - (b): fix \( m \) and induct on \( n \): \( m + 1 \in \nN \) by (N2), and \( m + (n + 1) = (m + n) + 1 \). Products: \( m \cdot 1 = m \) and \( m(n + 1) = mn + m \), by the sum half.
  - (c): property "\( n = 1 \) or \( n - 1 \in \nN \)"; the step holds because \( (n + 1) - 1 = n \in \nN \), **without** the induction hypothesis. `exm-predecessor-no-hypothesis` makes this a remark: an induction whose step ignores its hypothesis is legitimate; the base case and the form of the step are what the principle needs.
  - (d): induct on \( m \) with the property "for every \( n \in \nN \), \( m < n \Rightarrow n - m \in \nN \)". Base \( m = 1 \): \( n > 1 \), so \( n \ne 1 \), and (c) applies. Step: if \( m + 1 < n \), then \( m < n \), so \( n - m \in \nN \) by the hypothesis; also \( n - m > 1 \), so \( n - (m + 1) = (n - m) - 1 \in \nN \) by (c).
  - (e): if \( n < k < n + 1 \), then \( k - n \in \nN \) by (d), and \( 0 < k - n < 1 \), contradicting (a).
  - **Remark (a checkable meta-claim).** Chapter 3's proof of `@thm-strong-induction` uses (e) as a school fact, in the line "\( k < n + 1 \) implies \( k \le n \)" (quote it). (e) is now proved from `@ax-induction` by ordinary induction, so strong induction rests on `@ax-induction`, (N1), (N2) and the arithmetic of ℝ alone.
- **`prp-integers-closed`:** sums, differences and products of integers are integers; a positive integer is a natural number. Proof by cases on signs, from `lem-naturals-arithmetic` (b), (d) and trichotomy. Write out the case \( m + (-n) \) with \( m, n \in \nN \) in full (three subcases: \( m > n \), \( m = n \), \( m < n \)); the rest as a table.
- **`thm-well-ordering`** (key proof; *Try it first*): every non-empty subset of ℕ has a least element. Idea: suppose \( S \) has none; show by strong induction that no \( n \) lies in \( S \). Proof: property \( P(n) \): \( n \notin S \). Assume \( k \notin S \) for every \( k < n \). If \( n \in S \), then every \( s \in S \) satisfies \( s \ge n \) (trichotomy: \( s < n \) would give \( s \notin S \)), so \( n \) is least, a contradiction; hence \( n \notin S \). By `@thm-strong-induction`, \( S = \emptyset \). *Before reading on* prompt. Rule-level remark: the proof is an RAA on "\( S \) has a least element": the least element is shown to exist, not found. Point to Chapter 5 §07 (prose) for why that distinction has been argued over.
- **`thm-induction-iff-well-ordering`.** Let \( N \subseteq \nR \) satisfy \( 1 \in N \), \( n \in N \Rightarrow n + 1 \in N \), and the **predecessor property**: every \( n \in N \) with \( n \ne 1 \) has \( n - 1 \in N \). Then \( N \) has the **induction property** (every \( S \subseteq N \) containing \( 1 \) and closed under \( n \mapsto n + 1 \) is all of \( N \)) **if and only if** every non-empty subset of \( N \) has a least element.
  - \( (\Rightarrow) \): by `@ax-induction` for the property "\( n \in N \)", \( \nN \subseteq N \); \( \nN \) is a subset of \( N \) that contains \( 1 \) and is closed, so the induction property of \( N \) gives \( \nN = N \); then `thm-well-ordering`.
  - \( (\Leftarrow) \): the seven lines of §01 B2 (the text prints the proof and B2 shuffles it; STYLE allows ordering a printed proof, as Chapter 2 §03 B2 does).
  - **Each hypothesis has a job** (STYLE §10). Drop the predecessor property and \( (\Leftarrow) \) fails: \( N = \nN \cup \{ n + \frac12 : n \in \nN \} \) is well-ordered (for non-empty \( T \subseteq N \), compare the least elements of \( T \cap \nN \) and of \( \{ t - \frac12 : t \in T \setminus \nN \} \), shifted back) and is closed under \( +1 \); yet \( S = \nN \) contains \( 1 \), is closed, and \( S \ne N \). The witness breaks line (v) of the proof, at \( m = 3/2 \).
  - Aftermath: "induction and well-ordering are the same principle" means exactly this theorem; given (N1), (N2) and the predecessor property, either could have been Chapter 3's axiom. The optional Chapter 9 builds ℕ from sets and proves both (prose pointer).
- **`cor-well-ordering-integers`:** a non-empty set of integers with an **integer** lower bound has a least element; with an integer upper bound, a greatest element. Proof: shift into ℕ by \( s \mapsto s - b + 1 \) (`prp-integers-closed`). Say why the bound must be an integer: for a real bound we would first need an integer below it, which is Chapter 11's Archimedean property (prose pointer; Chapter 11's `@lem-integer-part` does exactly this).
- **Warning.** Well-ordering is about subsets of ℕ: the positive rationals have no least element (\( q/2 < q \)), and neither has ℤ. (Chapter 11 §06 has a find-the-error item on this; do not reuse its wording.)

## 02-induction-in-practice.md — Induction, Strong Induction and the Smallest Counterexample

- **Opening frame.** Chapter 3 gave the principle and one worked proof; Chapter 5 gave its rhetoric (`@exm-rhetoric-induction`, pointed back to). This section is the craft: choosing the form, and recognizing the ways it fails.
- **`prp-induction-from-base`:** if \( P(n_0) \) holds for an integer \( n_0 \) and \( P(n) \Rightarrow P(n + 1) \) for every integer \( n \ge n_0 \), then \( P(n) \) holds for every integer \( n \ge n_0 \); likewise for strong induction. Proof: shift to ℕ with \( k = n - n_0 + 1 \) (`prp-integers-closed`).
- **The smallest counterexample** (named move; an `::: {.algorithm}` block): suppose the claim fails; by `thm-well-ordering` there is a least failure \( m \); use the claim for every \( k < m \) to produce a contradiction. One paragraph: it is strong induction with an RAA box drawn around it; the two forms are interchangeable by `thm-well-ordering` and `@thm-strong-induction`.
- **Results.**
  - `def-divides` (\( a \mid b \iff \exists k \in \nZ\ b = ka \)); `def-prime` (an integer \( p \ge 2 \) whose only positive divisors are \( 1 \) and \( p \)).
  - `thm-division-algorithm` (*Try it first*): for \( a \in \nZ \) and \( n \in \nN \) there are **unique** \( q, r \in \nZ \) with \( a = qn + r \) and \( 0 \le r < n \). Existence: \( R \coloneqq \{ a - qn : q \in \nZ \} \cap \{ x \in \nZ : x \ge 0 \} \) is non-empty (\( q = -\abs{a} \) gives \( a + \abs{a} n \ge a + \abs{a} \ge 0 \)), so it has a least element \( r \) (`cor-well-ordering-integers`, lower bound \( 0 \)); if \( r \ge n \), then \( r - n \in R \) is smaller. Uniqueness: \( n \mid r - r' \) with \( \abs{r - r'} < n \) forces \( r = r' \) (a non-zero multiple of \( n \) has absolute value at least \( n \), by `lem-naturals-arithmetic` (a)). Aftermath: with \( n = 2 \), every integer is even or odd, and not both — the school fact of Chapters 1–2, now proved.
  - `lem-lowest-terms` (faded triple, full version; **pays (L)**): every rational number can be written \( p/q \) with \( p \in \nZ \), \( q \in \nN \), and \( p, q \) not both even. Proof in smallest-counterexample form: among all representations \( r = p/q \) with \( q \in \nN \) (there is one, by the definition of ℚ), take one with \( q \) least (`thm-well-ordering`); if \( p = 2p' \) and \( q = 2q' \), then \( q' \) is a positive integer, hence in ℕ (`prp-integers-closed`), and \( r = p'/q' \) with \( q' < q \). Remark, pointing back: this is the fact (L) that Chapter 2 took on trust in the proof of `@thm-sqrt2-irrational`. Full lowest terms (no common divisor \( d \ge 2 \)) goes the same way; that is C2.
  - `thm-prime-factor` (**pays** the prime-divisor promise): every integer \( n \ge 2 \) has a prime factor. Strong induction from base \( 2 \) (`prp-induction-from-base`): if \( n \) is not prime, it has a divisor \( a \) with \( 1 < a < n \); \( a \) has a prime factor by hypothesis, and it divides \( n \) (transitivity of \( \mid \), one line inline).
- **How induction proofs go wrong** (subsection; each failure located at a step, with its catalogue entry where one fits):

## 03-recursion.md — Definition by Recursion

- **Hook (a goal theorem first).** "Let \( u^1 = u \) and \( u^{n+1} = u^n u \)": such definitions are familiar from school. But a definition must define **something**. Induction proves facts about a function that already exists; here the function is what we want to obtain. Chapter 3 met the same issue for formulas (`@thm-structural-recursion`, pointed back to); this is its version for ℕ.
- **`thm-recursion`** (key proof). Let \( X \) be a set, \( a \in X \), and \( g \colon \nN \times X \to X \). Then there is **exactly one** function \( f \colon \nN \to X \) with \( f(1) = a \) and \( f(n + 1) = g(n, f(n)) \) for every \( n \in \nN \). Idea with a **Step roadmap**: ① uniqueness, by induction; ② **Claim**: for every \( n \) there is exactly one \( f_n \colon \{1, \dots, n\} \to X \) satisfying both equations wherever they make sense (induction on \( n \): \( f_{n+1} = f_n \cup \{ (n + 1, g(n, f_n(n))) \} \); uniqueness because the restriction of any such function to \( \{1, \dots, n\} \) is \( f_n \)); ③ \( f \coloneqq \bigcup_n f_n \) is a function because the \( f_n \) agree where they overlap (\( f_m = f_n|_{\{1, \dots, m\}} \) for \( m \le n \)), and it satisfies the equations. Chapter 6 supplies functions as sets of pairs (`@def-function`), restriction, unions of families (`@def-family-union-intersection`) and `@prp-function-equality`. *Before reading on* prompt. Remark: the step may depend on \( n \); Chapter 11 builds nested intervals \( I_{n+1} \) from \( I_n \) and the \( (n{+}1) \)-st listed number in exactly this way, with no choice involved (prose pointer: the promise).
- **`cor-two-step-recursion`:** given \( a, b \in X \) and \( h \colon \nN \times X \times X \to X \), there is exactly one \( f \) with \( f(1) = a \), \( f(2) = b \), \( f(n + 2) = h(n, f(n), f(n + 1)) \). Proof: `thm-recursion` applied to the pairs \( (f(n), f(n + 1)) \in X \times X \).
- **Definitions by recursion.** `def-sum-product`: \( \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k \), \( \prod_{k=1}^{n} a_k \), \( n! \) with \( 0! \coloneqq 1 \). `def-power`: \( u^0 \coloneqq 1 \), \( u^{n+1} \coloneqq u^n u \) for integers \( n \ge 0 \) (recursion on ℕ shifted by one; say so). Examples: \( a_1 = 2 \), \( a_{n+1} = (a_n + 2/a_n)/2 \), approximants to \( \sqrt2 \) (only the definition and the values \( 2, 3/2, 17/12 \); that \( a_n > 0 \), so that \( 2/a_n \) is defined, is an induction to do on the spot; convergence is Chapter 12, prose pointer); a two-step example.
- **Results.** `prp-power-laws` (\( u^{m+n} = u^m u^n \), \( (u^m)^n = u^{mn} \), \( (uv)^n = u^n v^n \); induction on \( n \), the first law in full). `prp-sum-first-n` (\( \sum_{k=1}^n k = n(n+1)/2 \)). `prp-geometric-sum` (\( \sum_{k=1}^{n} r^{k-1} = (1 - r^n)/(1 - r) \) for \( r \ne 1 \)). `thm-bernoulli` (\( (1 + x)^n \ge 1 + nx \) for real \( x \ge -1 \) and \( n \in \nN \)): the step multiplies by \( 1 + x \ge 0 \), and **this is where \( x \ge -1 \) is used**; drop it and the statement fails (\( x = -4 \), \( n = 3 \): \( -27 < -11 \)). `prp-finite-max` (**pays** Chapter 11's promise): for every \( n \in \nN \) and reals \( x_1, \dots, x_n \), the set \( \{x_1, \dots, x_n\} \) has a maximum and a minimum; induction on \( n \), with step \( \max\{M, x_{n+1}\} \).
- **`exm-strengthen-hypothesis`:** \( \sum_{k=1}^n 1/k^2 < 2 \) does not carry its own step; \( \sum_{k=1}^n 1/k^2 \le 2 - 1/n \) does (the step reduces to \( 1/(n+1)^2 \le 1/(n(n+1)) \)). The move: prove more.
- **Warning.** A recursion needs its starting values: \( a_{n+2} = a_{n+1} + a_n \) alone defines nothing unique. A recursive "definition" whose step leaves \( X \) is not one: \( a_1 = 2 \), \( a_{n+1} = 1/(a_n - 1) \) gives \( a_2 = 1 \) and then division by \( 0 \) (the hypothesis \( g \colon \nN \times X \to X \) fails).
- **Quick check.** Compute \( \prod_{k=1}^{4} (1 + 1/k) \) (\( = 5 \)) and say why the product telescopes.

## 04-relations.md — Properties of Relations

- **Hook (the standard example, generalized).** \( = \), \( \le \), \( \subseteq \), \( \mid \), "same remainder on division by \( 3 \)": what do they share, and where do they differ?

## 06-orders.md — Partial Orders, Maximal and Greatest Elements

- **Hook.** "Largest" is clear for numbers. For sets under \( \subseteq \) or numbers under divisibility, two things can be incomparable, and "largest" splits into two notions. This section plants the vocabulary Chapter 11 needs for the supremum.
- **Definitions.** `def-partial-order` (reflexive, antisymmetric, transitive; **total** if any two elements are comparable; the strict part \( \prec \)). `def-order-bounds` (upper and lower bounds of \( A \subseteq X \); bounded above and below). `def-greatest-element` (\( g \in A \) with \( a \preceq g \) for every \( a \in A \)) and `def-maximal-element` (\( m \in A \) with no \( a \in A \) such that \( m \prec a \)), both in symbols: the difference is **where the \( \neg \) sits** — greatest is \( \forall a \in A\ (a \preceq g) \), maximal is \( \neg\exists a \in A\ (m \prec a) \). `def-order-supremum`: a least upper bound of \( A \) in \( X \) (an upper bound that is \( \preceq \) every upper bound). `def-well-ordered-set`: a total order in which every non-empty subset has a least element; \( (\nN, \le) \) is one by `thm-well-ordering`, and \( (\nZ, \le) \) is not.
- **Results.** `prp-greatest-unique` (by antisymmetry; so "**the** greatest element"). `prp-greatest-maximal`: a greatest element is maximal, and in a **total** order a maximal element is greatest. The converse fails in general: `exm-maximal-not-greatest`: \( \{2, 3, 4, 5, 6\} \) under \( \mid \) has maximal elements \( 4, 5, 6 \) and no greatest; \( \{\{1\}, \{2\}\} \) under \( \subseteq \).
- **Least upper bounds, a first look.** In \( (\powerset{X}, \subseteq) \) the supremum of \( \{A, B\} \) is \( A \cup B \) (prove both halves: an upper bound, and below every upper bound), and the supremum of any family is its union. `exm-divisibility-lcm`: in \( (\nN, \mid) \), \( \sup\{a, b\} \) is the least common multiple: the least positive common multiple exists by `thm-well-ordering`, and it divides every common multiple by `thm-division-algorithm` (the remainder of a common multiple on division by it is a smaller non-negative common multiple, hence \( 0 \)). In \( (\nQ, \le) \): \( \{ x \in \nQ : x^2 < 2 \} \) is bounded above and has **no** least upper bound in ℚ — stated only, and sent to Chapter 11 (prose pointer: Chapter 11 proves it and makes the existence of suprema its axiom). A supremum need not belong to the set (\( A \cup B \notin \{A, B\} \) unless one contains the other).
- **Warning.** "Maximal" is not "greatest" (the witnesses above); and a set may have no maximal element at all (\( \nN \) under \( \le \)) or many.
- **Remark (prose pointers only).** Whether every partially ordered set in which every chain is bounded above has a maximal element is Zorn's lemma, equivalent to the axiom of choice: Chapter 8 states the axiom, Chapter 67 proves Zorn's lemma from it, the optional Chapter 9 lists the equivalents.
- **Quick check.** In \( \powerset{\{1, 2, 3\}} \setminus \{\{1, 2, 3\}\} \) under \( \subseteq \), name the maximal elements; is there a greatest? (The three two-element sets; no.)
