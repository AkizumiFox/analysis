# Blueprint: Chapter 1 — Propositional Logic and Natural Deduction

**Directory:** `src/ch01-propositional-natural-deduction/` (slug `propositional-natural-deduction`). **Part 0, the first of four logic chapters**, main line. It replaces the pilot `src/ch01-logic-connectives/`, whose material is redistributed over Chapters 1, 2 and 4 (migration notes per section below; the map for the whole block is `authoring/blueprints/part0-logic-overview.md`). Written to the full standard of `authoring/STYLE.md`, including §10–§11.

**Level reference.** Huth & Ryan, *Logic in Computer Science* (2nd ed.), §1.1–1.2, for **depth and coverage only** (source policy, `STYLE.md` hard rules): none of its prose, examples, sequent lists or exercises is reused. Where a standard sequent is unavoidable (De Morgan, contrapositive, excluded middle), the derivation's layout, its surrounding example and its exercises are ours.

**Purpose.**
- Teach natural deduction for the connectives as a **real calculus**, not as a metaphor: statements, the primitive rules, derivations with boxes and scope, derivability \( \Gamma \vdash P \), derived rules, provable equivalence, and above all **how to find a derivation**.
- The reader leaves able to (i) check any Fitch derivation line by line, naming the rule and the cited lines; (ii) find a derivation of a short propositional sequent by goal-directed search; (iii) say which results need RAA and why.
- Every rule still comes with **one** example from mathematics (integers, inequalities, absolute value), as decision 1 requires, but the chapter's centre of gravity is now the calculus. The rhetoric of each rule is only **sketched** here, in a short "What the rules sound like" paragraph per section (kept from the pilot); the full dictionary is Chapter 4.
- Truth tables are **not** in this chapter. They move to Chapter 2, which gives them a proper semantics and proves the rules sound and complete. This chapter ends on the question Chapter 2 answers: *we show that something is derivable by exhibiting a derivation; how could we ever show that something is not?*

**Signature moves** (`index.md`, "Named moves"):
- **Read the skeleton off the goal.** The goal's main connective chooses the introduction rule, and so the box to open.
- **Use a premise by its main connective.** Its elimination rule says what you may do with it.
- **Open a box, close a box.** An assumption lives exactly as long as its box.
- **Work from both ends.** Premises at the top, the goal at the bottom; fill the gap from both sides.
- **When stuck, assume the negation of the goal** (RAA), and hunt for a clash among the available lines.
- **Use a derived rule as one line.** A proved sequent is a new rule.

**Rule system (binding for Chapters 1–4; matches `latex/fitch-book.sty`).**
- **Primitive rules:** \( \wedge \)I, \( \wedge \)E, \( \to \)I, \( \to \)E, \( \vee \)I, \( \vee \)E, \( \neg \)I, \( \neg \)E, \( \bot \)E, **RAA**, \( \leftrightarrow \)I, \( \leftrightarrow \)E, plus the structural clause R (repetition, (D3) of `def-derivation`). Chapter 3 adds \( \forall \)I, \( \forall \)E, \( \exists \)I, \( \exists \)E, =I, =E.
- **RAA is the one classical rule:** a box \( \neg P \dots \bot \) gives \( P \). Some books call exactly this rule PBC and take \( \neg\neg \)E as primitive instead; §06 says so in one remark, naming no book. The book uses one name, **RAA**, for the rule and "proof by contradiction" for its rhetoric. There is **no separate PBC rule**.
- **Derived rules**, each proved once as a `prp-` and then usable as one line citing it (per `NOTATION.md`): \( \neg\neg \)I and \( \neg\neg \)E (`prp-double-negation`), MT (`prp-modus-tollens`), contrapositive (`prp-contrapositive`) and its converse (`prp-contrapositive-converse`), DS (`prp-disjunctive-syllogism`), `prp-ex-falso`, `prp-vacuous-implication`, LEM (`prp-excluded-middle`), De Morgan (`prp-de-morgan`).
- **\( \bot \)E stays primitive** although RAA derives it (a B exercise in §06), so that "every primitive rule except RAA" is exactly the intuitionistic system of Chapter 4 §07. §06 gives this as the reason for keeping a redundant rule.
- **\( \leftrightarrow \) stays primitive**, not an abbreviation, so every connective has its own I/E pair; `prp-iff-two-implications` shows it could have been an abbreviation.

**Allowed prerequisites.** None: this is the first chapter. School algebra about integers and reals may be used in examples as facts (\( x^2 \ge 0 \); sums of even integers are even; every integer is even or odd and not both). "\( n \) is even" means \( n = 2k \) for some integer \( k \); say in one clause that the rules for "some" come in **Chapter 3** (prose pointer, no `@ref`). No `@ref` outside this chapter.

## 01-statements-and-connectives.md — Statements, Connectives and the Form of an Argument

*Migrates:* pilot §01 almost whole: the hook, `def-statement`, `exm-statements`, the connectives, precedence and main connective, "Each connective, two rules"; its exercises A1, B1, B2, C1, C2, lightly edited.

- **Hook (kept): two arguments about squares.** Argument A is correct; Argument B proves the converse. The section ends on two promises: by §02 we can name B's error as a misuse of \( \rulename{{\to}I} \), and by Chapter 2 we can prove that B's pattern (from \( P \to Q \) and \( Q \), infer \( P \)) is not a rule at all.
- **Definitions.** `def-statement` (kept). **Atomic** and **compound** statements; the symbols \( \wedge, \vee, \to, \leftrightarrow, \neg, \bot \) with their readings. `def-main-connective` (new, minor): the connective applied last. Precedence: \( \neg \) binds tightest, then \( \wedge \) and \( \vee \), then \( \to \) and \( \leftrightarrow \); \( \to \) groups to the right; the book always writes the parentheses in a mixture of \( \wedge \) and \( \vee \). One sentence: Chapter 2 makes "statement built from letters" a precise definition; here we parse by eye.
- **New subsection: the form of an argument.** The same form carries different content: "if \( n \) is odd then \( n^2 \) is odd; \( n \) is odd; so \( n^2 \) is odd" and "if \( x > 2 \) then \( x^2 > 4 \); \( x > 2 \); so \( x^2 > 4 \)" share one form. Logic studies forms. `def-sequent` introduces \( P_1, \dots, P_n \vdash Q \) as the *claim* "\( Q \) can be derived from \( P_1, \dots, P_n \)"; `def-derivable` in §02 makes it exact.
- **Slogans (kept):** *each connective comes with a way to prove it and a way to use it*; *a proof is a chain in which every line is an assumption or one application of a rule*.
- **Preview table for \( \wedge \)** (kept).
- **Warnings.** Inclusive "or" (kept: \( x \ge 0 \) or \( x \le 0 \) at \( x = 0 \)). New: "if" in a theorem is one direction, but in a definition it is "iff"; pointer to §05.
- **Quick checks.** Main connective of three statements (kept). New: which of two arguments has the same form as a given one.
- **Exercises.** A1 (kept, plus a part "write the form of this argument as a sequent"). B1 translating analysis, B2 parsing (kept). C1 "only if" and "unless", C2 necessary and sufficient (kept).

## 07-finding-derivations.md — How to Find a Derivation

*Migrates:* pilot §07's "The skeletons" (`exm-skeleton-odd-product`) and its one-table summary, which becomes the chapter's reference sheet **without** the full phrase column (Chapter 4 owns that). The rest of pilot §07 (annotating, writing, checking) → Chapter 4.

- **Hook.** Checking a derivation is mechanical; finding one is not. For propositional logic there is a method that is enough for every derivation this chapter asks for, and it is the one a mathematician uses at the top of a page: write what you have, write what you want, look at the main connectives.
- **The method** (an `::: {.algorithm}` block, our own formulation):
  1. Premises at the top, the goal at the bottom, a gap between.
  2. **Goal-directed step.** By the goal's main connective, choose its introduction rule and set up its boxes or subgoals: \( \to \), a box with the antecedent; \( \wedge \), two subgoals; \( \leftrightarrow \), two boxes; \( \neg \), a box with the unnegated statement ending in \( \bot \); \( \vee \), **postpone**, since choosing a side too early is the commonest dead end.
  3. **Premise-directed step.** By the main connective of an available line, apply its elimination rule: \( \wedge \)E freely; \( \to \)E when the antecedent is available or can become a subgoal; \( \vee \)E by opening two boxes whose last line is the **current** goal.
  4. **Goal \( \bot \).** Look for a pair \( S \), \( \neg S \) among the available lines, or make \( S \) a subgoal for some \( \neg S \) you have.
  5. **Stuck.** RAA on the goal (assume its negation), or LEM on a well-chosen letter; return to 3.
- **Worked searches**, each shown as the gap shrinking (two or three snapshots of the partial derivation, then the finished one):
  - `exm-search-distribute`: \( P \to \neg Q \vdash \neg(P \wedge Q) \wedge (Q \to \neg P) \): three goal-directed steps (\( \wedge \)I into two subgoals; a \( \neg \)I box; a \( \to \)I box with a \( \neg \)I box inside), each gap then closed from the premise by \( \to \)E and \( \neg \)E (14 lines). **Faded triple, full version.** (Replaces \( P \to (Q \wedge R) \vdash (P \to Q) \wedge (P \to R) \), which is Huth & Ryan exercise 1.2.1(r), and whose search was not "goal-directed steps only": it needs \( \to \)E and \( \wedge \)E on the premise.   - `exm-search-cases`: \( P \vee Q, P \to R, Q \to R \vdash R \) (three premises; the one-premise form needed parentheses around two \( \wedge \)s): why the \( \vee \)E boxes must end in the current goal.
  - `exm-search-postpone-or`: \( P \to (Q \vee R) \vdash (P \to Q) \vee (P \to R) \): the dead end of choosing a side, then LEM on \( Q \), with DS in the \( \neg Q \) case (14 lines).
