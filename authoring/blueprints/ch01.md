# Blueprint: Chapter 1 — The Grammar of Proof I: Connectives and Natural Deduction

**Directory:** `src/ch01-logic-connectives/`. **Part 0**, main line. **Pilot chapter**: once the author approves it, it becomes the exemplar every later chapter imitates (`authoring/STATUS.md`). It is written to the full standard of `authoring/STYLE.md`, including §10.

**Purpose.**
- Readers find logic strange because the first thing they usually meet is a truth table: "if P is false, then P → Q is true", which feels like an arbitrary convention. This chapter reverses the order.
- Each connective is defined by two rules:
  - **introduction**: how you *prove* a statement of this shape;
  - **elimination**: what you may *do* with one once you have it.
- These rules are exactly the moves mathematicians already make in prose ("Assume P. … Hence Q. Therefore P implies Q."). So the answer to "what is allowed?" becomes concrete: **every sentence of a proof is one use of one rule.**
- Truth tables come at the end, as a summary of the rules.
- Chapter 2 does the same for ∀, ∃ and =, catalogues what is not allowed, and ends with the dispute over excluded middle.

**Signature moves** (list them in `index.md` under "Named moves"):
- read the proof's skeleton off the goal's main connective;
- open a box for an assumption, and close it to discharge the assumption;
- prove "not P" by assuming P and reaching a contradiction (¬I);
- prove by cases (∨E);
- use the contrapositive.

**Allowed prerequisites.** None: this is the first chapter. School algebra about integers and reals may be used in examples, as facts (x² ≥ 0; the sum of two even integers is even).
- **Evenness.** Everyday usage involves ∃: "n is even" means n = 2k for some integer k. Take evenness and oddness as understood. Also take as given that every integer is even or odd and not both. Chapter 2 makes the quantifier rules precise.
- **Signposting.** Where an example leans on a quantifier, say in one clause that the quantifier rules come in Chapter 2. Do not `@ref` anything. No other chapter exists yet.

**Notation.** See `authoring/NOTATION.md`, "Logic (Part 0)".
- **Symbols.**
  - Connectives: ∧, ∨, →, ↔, ¬, and ⊥ ("contradiction").
  - Derivability: Γ ⊢ P.
- **Derivations** are display arrays with columns for line number, formula and justification.
  - `\fsc` marks each open box and `\fhyp{…}` marks an assumption (the full pattern is in `latex/macros.tex`).
  - Justifications cite line numbers and a rule name, e.g. `\rulename{\to E}\ 1, 3`.
  - Keep every derivation to **at most 12 lines** and inside the text column.
- **Prose vs symbols.** Prose says "implies" and "if and only if". Theorem statements outside derivations use ⇒ and ⇔ as usual.
- **Lean** appears only in the rule/phrase/Lean tables and in at most one short remark per section, as `code`. The reader never needs it.

**Labels.**
- Rules are **definitions**, one per connective:
  - `def-rules-conjunction`
  - `def-rules-implication`
  - `def-rules-disjunction`
  - `def-rules-negation` (¬I, ¬E, ⊥E)
  - `def-rules-biconditional`
  - `def-rule-raa`
- Other definitions:
  - `def-statement`
  - `def-derivation` (a finite list of lines, each an assumption or a rule application, with boxes)
  - `def-derivable` (Γ ⊢ P)
- Derived results are `prp-…` (listed per section).
- Theorems about numbers are `thm-…`: `thm-square-even-implies-even`, `thm-sqrt2-irrational`.
  - **`thm-sqrt2-irrational` is owned by this chapter.** Chapter 2 and Chapter 8 (the gap in ℚ) cite it.

## index.md

- **Story.** A proof is an argument in which every step is licensed. This chapter makes "licensed" precise for the connectives: and, or, implies, not, iff. It shows that the license is what careful mathematicians already follow. The chapter ends able to write, read and check proofs whose logic is propositional. Chapter 2 adds the quantifiers, which are where analysis really lives.
- **What you need:** nothing beyond school algebra.
- **Roadmap:** one line per section.
- **Named moves:** as above.

## 01-statements-and-proofs.md — What a proof is made of

- **Hook: two proofs of the same claim, one wrong.** "If n² is even, then n is even" is proved correctly. Beside it, a plausible-looking argument proves the converse instead ("suppose n is even, then n² = 4k² is even…"). Ask: what exactly went wrong? The answer by the end of the chapter: the →I rule was used with the assumption and conclusion swapped.
- **Statements** (`def-statement`): sentences that are true or false. Examples and non-examples: a question; "x > 2" with x unspecified, which is an open sentence, left for Chapter 2.
  - The connectives and their symbols, with the ordinary-English readings.
  - Precedence and parentheses.
  - The main connective of a statement, found by parsing, e.g. ¬(P ∧ Q) → R.
- **The central idea, stated as a slogan:**
  - *Each connective comes with a way to prove it and a way to use it.*
  - *A proof is a chain in which every line is an assumption or one application of a rule.*
- **A preview table.** For ∧ only, show the two rules side by side with the English sentence that uses each. Promise the other connectives in the next sections.
- **Warning.** The English "or" is sometimes exclusive; the mathematical ∨ is inclusive. Give a concrete case: "x ≥ 0 or x ≤ 0" is true at x = 0.
- **Check.** Identify the main connective of three statements.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A:** translate between English and symbols; find main connectives.
  - **B:** translate four real-analysis sentences, with the atomic statements given. Examples:
    - "f is continuous at a but not differentiable there";
    - "if a sequence converges, it is bounded";
    - "x is rational or irrational";
    - "a set is closed iff its complement is open".
  - **C:** show that "unless" and "only if" are both →, in opposite directions. Include a decide-and-justify item on "P only if Q" versus "P if Q".

## 02-conjunction-and-implication.md — And, implies, and the box

- **Rules** (`def-rules-conjunction`, `def-rules-implication`):
  - **∧I:** from P and Q, infer P ∧ Q.
  - **∧E:** from P ∧ Q, infer P, and likewise Q.
  - **→I:** open a box with assumption P, reach Q inside, close the box, infer P → Q.
  - **→E (modus ponens):** from P → Q and P, infer Q.
- **The box and its scope** (`def-derivation`, `def-derivable`).
  - An assumption is available only inside its box.
  - Once the box closes, its lines are gone, and only the discharged implication remains.
  - Draw the first Fitch derivation in full: P → Q, Q → R ⊢ P → R.
  - Then write the same argument as mathematical prose. Take a transitivity-flavoured example with real numbers: "if a < b and b < c then a < c" used as the Q → R step. The point is that the prose and the derivation line up sentence for sentence.
- **Rule / phrase / Lean table (first rows).**

  | Rule | You write | Lean |
  |---|---|---|
  | ∧I | "We show P and we show Q." | `constructor` |
  | ∧E | "In particular, P." | `h.1` |
  | →I | "Assume P. … Hence Q." | `intro hp` |
  | →E | "Since P → Q and P holds, Q." | `exact h hp` |

- **Derived results** (`prp-implication-transitive`, `prp-and-commutes`, `prp-curry`): (P ∧ Q) → R ⊣⊢ P → (Q → R). Each gets a short derivation, and an Idea only where it helps.
- **Warning: the assumption of a closed box is not available afterwards.** Use a concrete case. A student assumes "x > 1" to prove "x > 1 → x² > 1", then later in the same proof uses x > 1 again to conclude something about x³. Name it: the assumption left its scope. Point out that this is the last row of Chapter 2's catalogue.
- **Check.** Which lines of a given derivation may cite line 2? The derivation has a box closing at line 5.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A:** fill in the justifications of a derivation.
  - **B:** derive (P ∧ Q) → (Q ∧ P) and P → (Q → P), each with a prose version.
  - **C:** a flawed derivation that cites a line from a closed box. Find the line, name the rule broken, and repair the derivation if possible. Then say whether the conclusion is still derivable, with a reason.

## 03-disjunction-negation-contradiction.md — Or, not, and contradiction

- **Rules** (`def-rules-disjunction`, `def-rules-negation`):
  - **∨I:** from P, infer P ∨ Q.
  - **∨E (proof by cases):** from P ∨ Q, a box P ⊢ R, and a box Q ⊢ R, infer R.
  - **¬I:** a box with assumption P reaching ⊥ gives ¬P.
  - **¬E:** from P and ¬P, infer ⊥.
  - **⊥E (ex falso):** from ⊥, infer anything.
- **Cases in analysis: |xy| = |x||y|.** Prove it by cases on the signs, and prove the triangle inequality |x + y| ≤ |x| + |y| by cases too.
  - Treat the facts about ≤ and absolute value as school algebra. Chapter 8 re-proves them from the ordered-field axioms; say so in prose, without an `@ref`.
  - The point is the shape of ∨E: every case must reach the same conclusion.
- **¬I done right: √2 is irrational** (`thm-sqrt2-irrational`).
  - The structure is ¬I. Assume √2 = p/q in lowest terms, and reach ⊥.
  - It first needs `thm-square-even-implies-even`, which is proved here by contraposition from "n odd ⇒ n² odd".
  - The contraposition step is P → Q ⊢ ¬Q → ¬P (`prp-contrapositive`). It is derived with →I and ¬I only: **no RAA**. State this explicitly.
  - Recovering "n² even ⇒ n even" from "n odd ⇒ n² odd" uses the school fact that every integer is even or odd, with ∨E on the two cases. **No RAA is needed here either.**
- **Vacuous truth** (`prp-ex-falso`, `prp-vacuous-implication`): ¬P ⊢ P → Q. Derive it with →I, ¬E and ⊥E.
  - Then discuss "if x² < 0 then x = 7" (true for every real x): *the rule ⊥E is where the strange row of the truth table comes from.*
  - Also give an everyday example: a promise never tested is never broken.
- **Rule/phrase/Lean rows** for ∨I (`left`/`right`), ∨E (`rcases h with hp | hq`), ¬I (`intro hp` … ⊢ `False`), ¬E (`exact hn hp`) and ⊥E (`exfalso` / `absurd`).
- **Warning: a missing case.** Give a proof of |x + y| ≤ |x| + |y| that treats x, y ≥ 0 and x, y < 0 and forgets the mixed-sign cases. Name ∨E as the rule broken.
- **Check.** Is "¬(P ∨ Q) ⊢ ¬P" derivable? Say which rules.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A:** match each proof sentence to its rule.
  - **B:**
    - disjunctive syllogism P ∨ Q, ¬P ⊢ Q (`prp-disjunctive-syllogism`);
    - distributivity P ∧ (Q ∨ R) ⊢ (P ∧ Q) ∨ (P ∧ R);
    - max(a, b) ≥ a by cases;
    - √3 is irrational, with the lemma about divisibility by 3 stated and proved.
  - **C:**
    - prove that √6 is irrational;
    - find the error in a "proof" that √4 is irrational, which uses the same template. Name the step that fails: the lemma "n² divisible by 4 ⇒ n divisible by 4" is false, with the witness n = 2.

## 04-biconditional-and-definitions.md — Iff, and what a definition says

- **Rules** (`def-rules-biconditional`):
  - **↔I:** two boxes, P ⊢ Q and Q ⊢ P.
  - **↔E:** from P ↔ Q and P, infer Q, and conversely.
- **Proving an iff takes two proofs**, labelled (⇒) and (⇐) as in `STYLE.md`.
  - Example: for integers, n is odd iff n² is odd.
  - Example: for real x ≥ 0 and y ≥ 0, x ≤ y iff x² ≤ y².
- **The "if" of a definition means "iff".** This is a convention of mathematical language, not a rule of logic.
  - Example: "we say f is increasing if x < y implies f(x) < f(y)". It is used in both directions: to check that a function is increasing, and to use that it is.
  - A **warning** in the other direction: in a *theorem*, "if" means only "if". Show the converse failing: "if f is differentiable at a then f is continuous at a" (school calculus), with |x| at 0 as the witness.
- **Chains of equivalences:** (a) ⇔ (b) ⇔ (c), proved as a cycle (a) ⇒ (b) ⇒ (c) ⇒ (a). Why the cycle suffices, as a derivation sketch.
- **Check.** Which direction of a given iff proof is missing?
- **Exercises.**
  - **A:** identify the two directions.
  - **B:** prove P ↔ Q ⊢ ¬P ↔ ¬Q, and prove a small integer iff.
  - **C:** a TFAE with three conditions on an integer (for example, 6 | n, 2 | n and 3 | n, 6 | n²). Prove it by a cycle, and explain why a two-arrow "cycle" would not suffice.

## 05-reductio.md — Proof by contradiction is a different rule

- **RAA** (`def-rule-raa`): a box with assumption ¬P reaching ⊥ gives P.
  - Contrast it with ¬I, a box with assumption P reaching ⊥ that gives ¬P. They look alike. They are not.
  - ¬I *introduces* a negation. RAA *removes* one, and nothing else in the rules so far can.
- **What only RAA gives:**
  - double-negation elimination, ¬¬P ⊢ P (`prp-double-negation`), against P ⊢ ¬¬P, which needs no RAA;
  - the reverse contrapositive, ¬Q → ¬P ⊢ P → Q (`prp-contrapositive-converse`);
  - the De Morgan law ¬(P ∧ Q) ⊢ ¬P ∨ ¬Q (`prp-de-morgan`). Derive the other three De Morgan directions without RAA, and point out which one needs it.
  - excluded middle, ⊢ P ∨ ¬P (`prp-excluded-middle`). It is derived here with RAA. Promise the full story, including the people who refused this rule, at the end of Chapter 2, without an `@ref`.
- **Style advice.** Say when a "proof by contradiction" is really a ¬I or a contrapositive in disguise, and should be rewritten as one.
  - Example: a "contradiction" proof of "if n² is even then n is even" that never uses the assumption except to contradict its own conclusion. That is a contrapositive in costume.
  - Name the smell: *the contradiction reached is with the negated conclusion itself.*
- **Remark** (at most 3 sentences): Lean separates `by_contra`, which is RAA, from `intro`, which is ¬I, for exactly this reason.
- **Check.** For each of three proofs, is it ¬I or RAA?
- **Exercises.**
  - **A:** classify rule uses.
  - **B:** derive ¬¬¬P ⊢ ¬P without RAA; derive (¬P → P) ⊢ P with RAA.
  - **C:** a "proof" of the infinitude of primes written as a contradiction. Rewrite it as a direct construction (Euclid's product plus one), and say what the rewrite gains.

## 06-truth-tables.md — Truth tables summarize the rules

- **Truth tables as a summary of the rules.** For each connective, read its truth table off its rules:
  - the rows where ∧I applies;
  - the →-row from ⊥E, recalling `prp-vacuous-implication`.
- **Tautologies and equivalence.**
  - Truth-table equivalence of the standard pairs: contrapositive, De Morgan, distributivity, P → Q ≡ ¬P ∨ Q.
  - The **informal soundness statement**: every rule preserves truth, so whatever is derivable is true in every row. Give an intuitive explanation, not a proof.
  - Completeness is only mentioned: every tautology is derivable, with RAA among the rules.
- **Using tables to *refute*.**
  - One row makes an argument invalid.
  - Example: "P → Q, Q ⊢ P" (affirming the consequent) fails in the row P false, Q true. Tie this back to the opening hook of §01.
- **Warning.** A truth table checks propositional form only. It cannot check a quantifier argument: "every ε has an N" is not a row. This leads into Chapter 2.
- **Check.** Find the refuting row for "P → Q, ¬P ⊢ ¬Q".
- **Exercises.**
  - **A:** fill in tables.
  - **B:** decide validity of four argument forms, derived if valid, with a row if not; show P → Q ≡ ¬P ∨ Q both by table and by derivation, noting which direction needs RAA.
  - **C:** show {¬, ∧} can express every truth function of two variables.

## 07-proof-skeletons.md — Reading the proof off the goal

- **The skeletons.** The goal's main connective chooses the skeleton:
  - direct proof of P → Q: →I;
  - contrapositive: →I plus ¬I/¬E, or RAA;
  - contradiction: RAA;
  - cases: ∨E;
  - iff: ↔I;
  - conjunction: ∧I.
- **The full propositional rule / phrase / Lean table**, collected in one place as the chapter's reference table.
- **Two directions of practice:**
  - **Annotate.** Take a short real proof (n² odd ⇒ n odd; or: if |x − 1| < 1 then x > 0, written with cases) and label every sentence with its rule.
  - **Write.** From a given derivation skeleton, write the fluent paragraph a mathematician would write. Show both.
- **Proof verification** (`STYLE.md` §10): four short "student proofs" of propositional-level claims (with integer and real-number content).
  - Two are correct.
  - One affirms the consequent: proving the converse, which settles the hook of §01 explicitly.
  - One uses an assumption outside its box.
  - Model the two-column check for each: *is each line true?* / *does it follow, and by which rule?*
- **Check.** Name the skeleton for three goals.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A:** skeleton identification.
  - **B:** prove three integer or real statements, each with the skeleton named first.
  - **C (exit checks):**
    - write a Fitch derivation of (P → Q) ∧ (Q → R) ⊢ P → R, then the same argument as prose;
    - label each line of a given proof with its rule;
    - verify four proofs: judge each, and locate and name the error.
