### 4.0 `index.md`

- **Title** `# Open, Closed, and Compact Subsets of \(\nR^n\)`.
- **Story (~350 words).** Start with [0, 1]. Remove the open middle third (1/3, 2/3). From each of the two closed intervals left, remove its open middle third; repeat, forever. After k steps, 2ᵏ closed intervals of length 3⁻ᵏ are left, so the removed pieces have total length 1 − (2/3)ᵏ, which exceeds any number below 1 once k is large. Something is left: the endpoints 0, 1, 1/3, 2/3, 1/9, … are never removed. Questions:
  - **(Q1)** Is anything left besides the endpoints of removed intervals (which form a countable set)?
  - **(Q2)** How many points are left: countably many, or as many as in ℝ?
  - **(Q3)** Does what is left contain an interval?
  - and the length question: is "total length 0" a property of the leftover set itself, and does every set built by removing middle pieces have it?
  "To answer them we need words for sets in which every point has room around it (open), sets that keep the points they crowd towards (closed), and sets that are 'finite up to small errors' (compact). This chapter builds those words, first on the line and in the plane, then in ℝⁿ, and answers all four questions in its last section." No answers here.

### 4.1 Section 2.1: Open Sets (`01-open-sets.md`)

**Budget** ≤ 5,000 (expected ~4,600). Five Quick checks, two warnings.

1. **Opening (~200).** One paragraph: Chapter 1 worked with intervals; analysis needs sets that are not intervals (the story's leftover set, ℚ, domains in the plane). Recall from F4.8 in one paragraph, no proofs: ℝⁿ as n-tuples, the norm ‖x‖ = (x₁² + ⋯ + xₙ²)^{1/2} (the root by @thm-nth-root), the distance ‖x − y‖, the triangle inequality ‖x − z‖ ≤ ‖x − y‖ + ‖y − z‖, its reverse form | ‖x‖ − ‖y‖ | ≤ ‖x − y‖, and |xᵢ − yᵢ| ≤ ‖x − y‖ ≤ … For n = 1, ‖x‖ = |x| (@def-absolute-value). Points are plain italic (NOTATION).
2. **`def-open-ball`** `[Open Ball]`: B(a, r) ≔ {x ∈ ℝⁿ : ‖x − a‖ < r} for a ∈ ℝⁿ, r > 0. For n = 1 it is (a − r, a + r) by @eq-abs-interval; for n = 2 an open disc. **`def-interior-point`** `[Interior Point, Interior]`: x is an interior point of S if some B(x, r) ⊆ S; \( \interior{S} \) is the set of interior points. Symbols: x ∈ \( \interior{S} \) ⇔ ∃r > 0: B(x, r) ⊆ S.
3. **Naive attempts (~250), then `def-open-set`.** Paragraph `**What "open" should mean.**` (a) "No endpoints": fine for intervals, meaningless for ℚ or for {x ∈ ℝ² : x₁ > 0}. (b) "Does not contain its edge": needs "edge" defined first, and for ℚ every real number is on the edge. (c) Chapter 4 will offer a definition by sequences; each use then costs a sequence and a limit. What every attempt reaches for: around each point of the set there is room in every direction. **`def-open-set`**: S ⊆ ℝⁿ is open if every point of S is an interior point, i.e. S = \( \interior{S} \). Symbols: ∀x ∈ S ∃r > 0: B(x, r) ⊆ S. Slogan: *every point has room*.
4. **Quick check (weird examples), straight after the definition.** "Open or not, in the space named: (a) ∅ in ℝ; (b) ℤ in ℝ; (c) {(x, y) ∈ ℝ² : y > 0}; (d) the line {(x, 0) : x ∈ ℝ} in ℝ²; (e) (0, 1) in ℝ." *Solution:* (a) yes, vacuously (there is no point to check); (b) no: B(0, r) contains min(r, 1)/2 ∉ ℤ; (c) yes, with radius y at (x, y) (if ‖(u, v) − (x, y)‖ < y then |v − y| < y so v > 0); (d) no, B((x, 0), r) contains (x, r/2); (e) yes. Aftermath: (d) and (e) together: whether a set is open depends on the space it sits in; Chapter 3 makes this precise (one forward pointer).
5. **`prp-open-ball-open`** `[Open Balls Are Open]`. **`[Scratch work]`** (technique 1, ≤ 18 lines): Givens/Goal table. Givens: x ∈ B(a, r), so d ≔ ‖x − a‖ < r. Goal: some s > 0 with B(x, s) ⊆ B(a, r), i.e. ‖y − x‖ < s ⇒ ‖y − a‖ < r. Gap: ‖y − a‖ ≤ ‖y − x‖ + d < s + d, so s + d ≤ r suffices: s = r − d. Picture sentence: the room left is the distance to the rim. **Proof** forwards, three lines. **Named move**: Leave room.
6. **`exm-open-sets-gallery`** `[Open Sets, and Sets That Are Not]` (~400). (a) ℝⁿ and ∅. (b) Open intervals, including (a, ∞) and (−∞, b). (c) {x ∈ ℝⁿ : ‖x − a‖ > r}, with radius ‖x − a‖ − r (reverse leave room). (d) Not open: [0, 1) at 0; ℚ (every ball about a rational contains an irrational, @cor-irrationals-dense); the line of 4 (d); a finite nonempty set.
7. **`def-box`** `[Boxes]`: open box (a₁, b₁) × ⋯ × (aₙ, bₙ), closed box [a₁, b₁] × ⋯ × [aₙ, bₙ] (aᵢ < bᵢ, resp. ≤), cube when all sides bᵢ − aᵢ are equal; side lengths. **`lem-ball-box`** `[Balls and Boxes]`: (a) the open cube with centre a and half-side r/n lies in B(a, r); (b) B(a, r) lies in the open cube with centre a and half-side r; (c) any two points of a closed cube of side ℓ are at distance ≤ nℓ. *Proof:* (a) Σ(xᵢ − aᵢ)² < n(r/n)² = r²/n ≤ r². (b) |xᵢ − aᵢ| ≤ ‖x − a‖ < r. (c) Σ(xᵢ − yᵢ)² ≤ nℓ² ≤ n²ℓ². Remark: so "open" could be defined with boxes instead of balls, and nothing would change (Quick check: why does (a) and (b) give that? *Solution:* a box around x inside S gives a ball around x inside S by (a) and conversely by (b)). The lemma is used in §03 (cubes in bisection), §04 (rational balls), §06.
8. **`thm-open-union-intersection`** `[Unions and Finite Intersections of Open Sets]`: (a) the union of any family of open sets is open; (b) the intersection of finitely many open sets is open. *Idea* (2 lines, back-reference to leave room): for (b), each set gives its own radius; take the smallest (a finite set of positive numbers has a minimum, @fd2:prp-finite-max). *Proof.* Family notation from @fd2:def-family-union-intersection.
9. **`exm-infinite-intersection`** + **Warning** `**Infinitely many radii can shrink to nothing.**`: ⋂_{k≥1} (−1/k, 1/k) = {0}, not open; proof that no x ≠ 0 lies in all by @cor-archimedean-epsilon. The proof of (b) breaks exactly where it takes a minimum: the radii 1/k have infimum 0. (This is the false proof of C2, so the warning names the failing step without writing the false proof out.)

### 4.4 Section 2.4: Open Covers and the Heine–Borel Theorem (`04-heine-borel.md`)

**Budget** ≤ 5,000 (expected ~4,700). Five Quick checks, two warnings.

1. **Hook: bounded near every point, but not bounded (~350).** f(x) = 1/x on (0, 1]. Near each point x it is bounded: on (x/2, 1] ∩ (0, 1] by 2/x. Yet f is unbounded. The local bounds 2/x get worse and worse as x → 0, and no finite number of them suffices. On [0, 1], with any value at 0, the same f is not even bounded near 0. 2. **`def-open-cover`** `[Cover, Open Cover, Subcover]`: a family 𝓕 of sets covers S if S ⊆ ⋃𝓕; an open cover if its members are open; a subcover is a subfamily that still covers; finite if it has finitely many members. **`exm-cover-no-finite-subcover`**: (a) (0, 1] and {(1/k, 2) : k ≥ 1}: a finite subfamily has a largest k, its union is (1/k, 2), which misses 1/k; (b) ℝ and {(−k, k) : k ≥ 1}; (c) ℤ and {B(m, 1/2) : m ∈ ℤ}: no proper subfamily covers at all. ### 4.5 Section 2.5: Compact Sets (`05-compact-sets.md`)

**Budget** ≤ 5,000 (expected ~4,300). Four Quick checks, two warnings.

1. **Why a new word (~350; failure-motivated).** Heine–Borel says: closed and bounded ⇒ every open cover has a finite subcover. Which of the two descriptions should be the definition? "Closed and bounded" is a description of shape, two checks on S alone; but every proof in §04 used only the cover property, and the hook's argument (local to global) needs nothing else. So the definition is the cover property.

3. **`thm-compact-rn`** `[Compactness in ℝⁿ]`: for S ⊆ ℝⁿ the following are equivalent: (a) S is compact; (b) S is closed and bounded; (c) every infinite subset of S has an accumulation point that belongs to S. Proof plan stated first: (a) ⇒ (b) ⇒ (c) ⇒ (b) ⇒ (a).
   - (b) ⇒ (a): @thm-heine-borel.
   - (a) ⇒ (b), bounded: the balls B(0, k) cover ℝⁿ (Archimedes), finitely many cover S, and they are nested.    - (b) ⇒ (c): an infinite T ⊆ S is bounded, has an accumulation point x (@thm-bolzano-weierstrass-sets), and x ∈ T′ ⊆ S′ ⊆ S.

Uncountable by @fd2:thm-binary-sequences-uncountable. (One clause: the level-k interval containing x is unique by (c) of `prp-cantor-levels`.)
- **C2** `[Perfect sets in the plane]` (exit check). Show that C × C ⊆ ℝ² is compact and perfect, and that it contains no open box. *Solution:* closed: a point (x, y) ∉ C × C has x ∉ C or y ∉ C; say x ∉ C; an open interval (x − r, x + r) misses C (C closed), and the strip (x − r, x + r) × ℝ is an open set missing C × C. Bounded. Perfect: given (x, y) ∈ C × C and r > 0, x ∈ C′ gives x′ ∈ C, x′ ≠ x, |x′ − x| < r; (x′, y) ∈ C × C is within r. No open box: a box (a, b) × (c, d) inside C × C would put (a, b) inside C. *Marking scheme* (10): closed 3 (the strip argument); bounded 1; perfect 3 (the point ≠ (x, y) named); no box 3. Fatal: "a product of closed sets is closed" asserted without argument.
- **C3** `[Find the error]` (false conclusion; extra, since the chapter's quota is met: drafter may drop it if §06 runs over budget). "Claim: every closed subset of ℝ with no isolated points is uncountable. 'Proof': such a set is perfect, so `thm-perfect-uncountable` applies." *Solution:* false for ∅; the theorem needs nonempty. Counterexample to the step "applies": P = ∅.
- **R1** (method from Chapter 1). Let A ⊆ ℝ be nonempty and bounded above, with sup A ∉ A. Show sup A ∈ A′. *Solution:* for r > 0 @thm-sup-epsilon gives a ∈ A with sup A − r < a ≤ sup A, and a ≠ sup A. (Solution cites Chapter 1 by `@ref`; the problem text names no chapter.)
- **R2** (reach back within the chapter and to F2.3, STYLE §11.5 "early chapters"). Show that the set of all open intervals with rational endpoints is countable, and that every open U ⊆ ℝ is the union of those of them that lie in U. *Solution:* ℚ × ℚ countable; for x ∈ U, (x − r, x + r) ⊆ U and rationals x − r < p < x < q < x + r.
- **R3** (spiral: nested intervals → nested boxes). Stem: "State and prove a version of the nested interval theorem for nested closed boxes in ℝⁿ, including a condition under which the common point is unique." *Solution:* nested closed boxes have a common point (@thm-nested-intervals in each coordinate); if for every ε > 0 some box has all sides < ε, the point is unique (@lem-ball-box (c): two common points are at distance ≤ n·ε for every ε). The largest side, or the bound nℓ on distances, replaces the length.
- **R4** (no hint of method). "Is the set S = {x ∈ [0, 1] : x ∈ C or 1/3 ≤ x ≤ 2/3} compact? perfect? countable?" *Solution:* S = C ∪ [1/3, 2/3]: compact (union of two compact sets); perfect (union of two perfect sets, B3 (a)); uncountable. Tools: closedness by finite unions; the look-alike "remove middle thirds again" does not apply because [1/3, 2/3] is put back whole.
