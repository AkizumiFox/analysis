## 1. Purpose, hook and devices

**Purpose.**
- Chapter 2 built the topology of ℝⁿ (open, closed, accumulation point, closure, Bolzano–Weierstrass, Heine–Borel) from the Euclidean distance. F4.11 abstracted the distance to a metric. This chapter asks **which of Chapter 2's facts were about distance alone** and proves those in every metric space, with labels the rest of the series cites.
- It finds the facts that **fail**: the closure of an open ball need not be the closed ball, a ball's radius need not be determined by the set, a closed bounded infinite set may have no accumulation point (Bolzano–Weierstrass fails), and a space may have no countable dense subset.
- It adds the notions every later chapter uses: interior, boundary, dense, separable, relative topology of a subspace, equivalent metrics, product metrics.
- The reader leaves able to (i) prove a set open by finding a radius, or closed by passing to complements; (ii) compute interior, closure and boundary in ℝⁿ, a discrete space, B(S) and the railway plane; (iii) decide whether a set is open *in a subspace*; (iv) decide whether two metrics have the same open sets; (v) refute a plausible "metric" claim with one of the chapter's witnesses.

**Chapter hook: "Every line runs through the capital"** (`index.md` story and §1 opening). A country's railway lines all run straight out of the capital, placed at 0 ∈ ℝ². To travel between two towns on the same line you ride along it; otherwise you ride in to the capital and out again. This is the **railway metric** (`exm-railway-metric`), d_R(x, y) = |x − y| when one of x, y is a nonnegative multiple of the other, and |x| + |y| otherwise (|·| the Euclidean norm of F4.8). Three questions are posed, unanswered:

- **(Q1)** What does a small ball around a town look like? Around the capital?
- **(Q2)** The towns at distance 1 from the capital form the unit circle. It is bounded and uncountable. In ℝ², Chapter 2's Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem gives such a set an accumulation point. Does the circle have one for d_R?
- **(Q3)** Is there a countable set of towns that comes within any given distance of every town?

**Where the hook is paid.** Q1 in §1 (`exm-railway-balls`); Q2 in §2 (`exm-railway-circle`: no, and Bolzano–Weierstrass fails) and fully in AN2.3 (what ℝⁿ has that the railway plane lacks: total boundedness; prose pointer "Chapter 8"); Q3 in §3 (`exm-railway-not-separable`). §5 closes with "Back to the capital", which fills in the survive/fail table opened in §1.

**Named moves** (`index.md`; each one line, linked to its first worked use):
1. **Find a radius.** To show x is interior to S, name r from the data (often r − d(x, y), or the gap to a boundary value) and check B(x, r) ⊆ S with one triangle inequality. First: `thm-ball-open`.
2. **Take the minimum of finitely many radii.** Finitely many conditions, each with its own radius; their minimum is positive because there are finitely many. First: `thm-open-set-operations` (c).
3. **Pass to complements.** Turn a statement about closed sets into one about open sets by De Morgan (F2.1), prove it there, and turn it back. First: `thm-closed-set-operations`.
4. **Smallest closed superset.** To identify a closure, check two properties: the candidate is closed and contains S, and it lies inside every closed superset of S (the closure analogue of "split the supremum", @thm-sup-epsilon). First: `thm-closure-smallest-closed`.
5. **A separated family.** Uncountably many points pairwise at distance ≥ δ > 0 give disjoint balls of radius δ/2, each of which a dense set must meet; so no countable dense set exists. First: `prp-separated-family`.
6. **Intersect with the subspace.** A ball of Y is a ball of X intersected with Y; build an open set of X as a union of such balls. First: `thm-relatively-open`.
7. **Balls inside balls.** To compare two metrics, put a ball of one inside a ball of the other around the same point. First: `thm-ball-criterion`.

### 5.1 `01-metric-open-sets.md` — Open Sets in a Metric Space (Apostol 3.13, 3.14 first half)

**Budget:** ≤ 5,000 (expected ~4,300). Two Quick checks, one warning, one non-example.

**Opening frame (~120).** Recall F4.11: a metric space (X, d), the ball B(x, r) ≔ {y ∈ X : d(x, y) < r}, the closed ball, bounded sets. Recall Chapter 2's open subsets of ℝⁿ in one sentence. Say what this section does: define open sets in any metric space and test which of Chapter 2's facts about them survive.

**`## All lines run through the capital` (~650).**
1. **`exm-railway-metric`** `[The railway metric]`. State d_R as in §1. *Solution:* (M1), (M2) are immediate (both formulas are symmetric and vanish only at x = y; in the second case x, y are not both 0, so |x| + |y| > 0). Two inequalities used throughout: d_R(x, y) ≥ |x − y| (in the second case by the Euclidean triangle inequality) and d_R(x, y) ≤ |x| + |y|. (M3) d_R(x, z) ≤ d_R(x, y) + d_R(y, z), by cases. *Case x, z on a common ray:* d_R(x, z) = |x − z| ≤ |x − y| + |y − z| ≤ d_R(x, y) + d_R(y, z). *Case not:* d_R(x, z) = |x| + |z|. If y = 0, the right side is |x| + |z|. If y ≠ 0 and y were on a common ray with both x and z, then x and z would both be nonnegative multiples of y, hence one a nonnegative multiple of the other, against the case. So, say, x and y are not on a common ray (the other subcase is symmetric): d_R(x, y) = |x| + |y| and d_R(y, z) ≥ |y − z| ≥ |z| − |y| (F4.8), so the sum is ≥ |x| + |z|. Ledger: "common ray" means one point is a nonnegative multiple of the other; 0 is on every ray.

**`## Interior points and open sets` (~900).**
1. **Failure-motivated definition.** Lead: Chapter 2 called a subset of ℝⁿ open when every point has a ball around it inside the set. Why not ask for one radius serving every point? Try it on (0, 1) ⊆ ℝ: a radius r would have to satisfy B(x, r) ⊆ (0, 1) for x = r/2, but B(r/2, r) contains −r/4. So the uniform version makes (0, 1) "not open", and with it every bounded open interval; the radius must be allowed to depend on the point (≤ 120 words).
2. **`def-interior-point-metric`** `[Interior Point]`: x ∈ S is an interior point of S ⊆ X if B(x, r) ⊆ S for some r > 0. **`def-open`** `[Open Set]`: S is open if every point of S is an interior point of S. In symbols (STYLE §10): (∀x ∈ S)(∃r > 0)(∀y ∈ X)(d(x, y) < r ⇒ y ∈ S).
3. **Quick check (quantifier swap):** "Write the uniform version with the quantifiers (∃r > 0)(∀x ∈ S) and decide which of these sets it calls open in ℝ: (0, 1), (0, ∞), ℝ, ∅." *Solution:* (0, 1): no (as above). (0, ∞): no, the same witness x = r/2. ℝ: yes, any r. ∅: yes, vacuously. The two definitions agree on ℝ and ∅ and differ on (0, 1) and (0, ∞).
4. **`thm-ball-open`** `[Open Balls Are Open]`: in a metric space, for x ∈ X and r > 0, B(x, r) is open.
   - **`[Scratch work]`** (first appearance of "find a radius", ≤ 18 lines). Givens: y ∈ B(x, r), i.e. d(x, y) < r. Goal: some s > 0 with B(y, s) ⊆ B(x, r), i.e. for z with d(y, z) < s, show d(x, z) < r. The only tool that relates d(x, z) to d(x, y) and d(y, z) is (M3): d(x, z) ≤ d(x, y) + d(y, z) < d(x, y) + s. This is < r if s ≤ r − d(x, y). Table, last row "s ≔ r − d(x, y) > 0: check". One sentence: in ℝ² the picture is a small disk at y whose radius is y's distance to the rim.
   - **Proof** (forward): Let y ∈ B(x, r); put s ≔ r − d(x, y), positive since d(x, y) < r. If d(y, z) < s, then d(x, z) ≤ d(x, y) + d(y, z) < d(x, y) + s = r by (M3). So B(y, s) ⊆ B(x, r).
   - Aftermath: the proof used (M3) once and nothing about coordinates, so this row of the table survives.
5. **`prp-open-union-of-balls`** `[Open Sets Are Unions of Balls]`: S ⊆ X is open iff S is a union of open balls. Idea (2 lines, cites the move). Proof (choice-free): (⇒) let 𝓑 be the family of all open balls contained in S. Their union lies in S; and each x ∈ S lies in some ball B(x, r) ⊆ S, which belongs to 𝓑. So S = ⋃𝓑. (⇐) a union of open balls is open by `@thm-ball-open` and `thm-open-set-operations` (b) — **order:** state `thm-open-set-operations` before this proposition (the drafter puts this proposition at the end of "Unions and intersections"). 

**`## Unions and intersections` (~700).**
1. **`thm-open-set-operations`** `[Unions and Finite Intersections of Open Sets]`: (a) ∅ and X are open; (b) the union of any family of open sets is open; (c) the intersection of finitely many open sets is open.
   - **`[Scratch work]`** for (c) only (first appearance of "minimum of finitely many radii", ≤ 10 lines): x ∈ U₁ ∩ … ∩ U_k gives radii r₁, …, r_k, one per set; a ball inside all of them needs a radius ≤ each r_i; the minimum works and is positive *because there are finitely many*.

**`## A gallery of open sets` (~1,500).**
1. **`exm-railway-balls`** `[Balls in the railway metric]` (answers Q1). *Solution:* For p ≠ 0 and 0 < r ≤ |p|: B_R(p, r) = {tp : t > 0, |tp − p| < r}, an open segment of p's line not reaching 0 (a point q off the line has d_R(p, q) = |p| + |q| ≥ |p| ≥ r; q = 0 has distance |p| ≥ r). For r > |p|: the segment {tp : t ≥ 0, |tp − p| < r} together with the Euclidean disk {q : |q| < r − |p|}. Around 0: B_R(0, r) is the Euclidean disk of radius r (every point is on a common ray with 0). Consequence: a set U is d_R-open iff each point p ≠ 0 of U has a small segment of its line around it inside U, and, if 0 ∈ U, U contains a disk around 0. So the segment {(t, 0) : 1 < t < 2} is open for d_R but not for d₂. Every d₂-open set is d_R-open: d_R ≥ d₂ gives B_R(p, r) ⊆ B₂(p, r).
2. **`exm-discrete-open`** `[Every set is open in a discrete space]`. B(x, 1) = {x} ⊆ S for x ∈ S. Aftermath: so "open" says nothing about shape; it depends on d.
3. **`exm-positive-functions-open`** `[Positive bounded functions]`. In B([0, 1]) with the sup metric d(f, g) = sup |f − g| (F4.11): (a) U ≔ {f : inf f > 0} is open; (b) V ≔ {f : f(t) > 0 for every t} is not. *Solution:* (a) Let f ∈ U, m ≔ inf f > 0 (exists by `@thm-greatest-lower-bound`). If d(f, g) < m then for every t, g(t) ≥ f(t) − d(f, g) ≥ m − d(f, g); so m − d(f, g) is a positive lower bound of g, and inf g ≥ m − d(f, g) > 0. So B(f, m) ⊆ U (find a radius, with the infimum as the gap). (b) f(0) ≔ 1, f(t) ≔ t for 0 < t ≤ 1 lies in V. For any r > 0, g ≔ f − r/2 has d(f, g) = r/2 < r and g(t) = t − r/2 < 0 at t = min(r/4, 1) (with r/4 ≤ 1 the value is −r/4; if r > 4 use t = 1 where g(1) = 1 − r/2 < 0). So no ball around f lies in V. Aftermath: pointwise positivity can be destroyed by an arbitrarily small uniform push; uniform positivity cannot. (Weird-table row.) Note for the drafter: f is bounded (0 < f ≤ 1), so f ∈ B([0, 1]).
4. **`exm-radius-not-intrinsic`** `[A smaller radius, a larger ball]`. In X = [0, 1] with |x − y|: B(0, 0.9) = [0, 0.9) and B(1/2, 0.6) = [0, 1], so a ball of radius 0.6 strictly contains a ball of radius 0.9. Contrast (one sentence, proof in §1 B4 (d) solution): in ℝⁿ, B(x, r) ⊆ B(y, s) forces r ≤ s.
5. **Warning** `**Open depends on the metric.**` "The segment {(t, 0) : 1 < t < 2} of the plane is open for the railway metric and not for the Euclidean metric. 'Open' is a property of a set *in a metric space*, never of a set alone." (§4 refines this to the surrounding set.)
6. **Non-example**, inside the warning or as a sentence: [0, 1) is not open in ℝ: every ball around 0 contains negative numbers.

The open ray is: for p = (t, 0), t > 0, the ball of radius t is the segment of the ray from 0 (excluded) to 2p, inside the open ray.

### 5.2 `02-metric-closed-sets.md` — Accumulation Points, Closed Sets, and Closure (Apostol 3.14 second half)

**Opening frame (~100).** Recall Chapter 2's closed subsets of ℝⁿ and its two descriptions (complement open; contains its accumulation points). Both survive in metric spaces; one picture does not, and it answers Q2.

**`## Closed sets` (~700).**
1. **`def-closed`** `[Closed Set]`: S ⊆ X is closed if X ∖ S is open.
2. **`thm-closed-set-operations`** `[Intersections and Finite Unions of Closed Sets]`: ∅ and X are closed; any intersection of closed sets is closed; any finite union of closed sets is closed.
   - **`[Scratch work]`** (first appearance of "pass to complements", ≤ 8 lines): X ∖ ⋂F_i = ⋃(X ∖ F_i) and X ∖ (F₁ ∪ … ∪ F_k) = ⋂(X ∖ F_i) (De Morgan, F2.1); the open-set theorem does the rest.
   - Proof: three lines from `thm-open-set-operations`.
3. **`prp-closed-ball-closed-metric`** `[Closed Balls and Spheres Are Closed]`: for x ∈ X and r ≥ 0, B̄(x, r) and S(x, r) are closed. Proof: for y with d(x, y) > r, put s ≔ d(x, y) − r; if d(y, z) < s then d(x, z) ≥ d(x, y) − d(y, z) > r (M3 in the form d(x, y) ≤ d(x, z) + d(z, y)); so the complement of B̄(x, r) is open. S(x, r) = B̄(x, r) ∩ (X ∖ B(x, r)), closed by `thm-closed-set-operations` and `thm-ball-open`.
4. **Warning** `**Closed is not "not open".**` "[0, 1) ⊆ ℝ is neither open nor closed; in a discrete space every set is both. Proving that a set is not open proves nothing about whether it is closed."

**`## Adherent points, accumulation points, closure` (~1,300).**
1. **Failure-motivated lead (~120).** In ℝⁿ, the closure of the open ball B(x, r) is the closed ball (Chapter 2). So one might *define* closure through closed balls, or expect the closed ball to be the smallest closed set containing the open ball. Test it in a discrete space with two or more points: B(x, 1) = {x} is already closed (every set is), while B̄(x, 1) = X. Closure must be defined by what the set itself approaches, not by a formula in the radius.
2. **`def-adherent-point-metric`** `[Adherent Point]`: x ∈ X is adherent to S if every ball B(x, r) meets S. **`def-accumulation-point-metric`** `[Accumulation Point; Isolated Point]`: x is an accumulation point of S if every B(x, r) contains a point of S other than x; S′ is the set of accumulation points; a point of S that is not an accumulation point of S is an isolated point of S. Symbols for both (STYLE §10).
3. **`def-closure-metric`** `[Closure]`: \closure S is the set of points adherent to S.
4. **Quick check:** "S = {1/n : n ≥ 1} ⊆ ℝ. Find S′ and \closure S. Which points of S are isolated?" *Solution:* S′ = {0}; \closure S = S ∪ {0}; every point of S is isolated (1/n has the ball of radius 1/n − 1/(n + 1) meeting S only in 1/n; for 0: each ball (−r, r) contains 1/n for n > 1/r; for x ∉ S ∪ {0}, a small ball misses S — x < 0 radius |x|, x > 1 radius x − 1, 1/(n + 1) < x < 1/n radius the smaller gap).
5. **`thm-closed-iff-contains-accumulation-points`** `[Closed Sets Contain Their Accumulation Points]`: (a) \closure S = S ∪ S′; (b) S is closed iff S′ ⊆ S iff \closure S = S. Idea (2–3 lines): an adherent point outside S cannot use itself to meet S, so it is an accumulation point; (b) is "a point outside S is interior to the complement iff it is not adherent". Proof: (a) a point of S is adherent (it lies in every ball around it); an accumulation point is adherent; an adherent point not in S meets S in each ball at a point ≠ itself. (b) S closed ⇔ every x ∉ S has a ball missing S ⇔ no x ∉ S is adherent ⇔ \closure S ⊆ S ⇔ (by (a)) S′ ⊆ S. The (⇐) half is §2 B3's proof-ordering item.
6. **`thm-closure-smallest-closed`** `[The Closure Is the Smallest Closed Superset]` (**key proof**): For S ⊆ X: (a) \closure S is closed; (b) S ⊆ \closure S; (c) if F is closed and S ⊆ F, then \closure S ⊆ F. Consequently \closure S is the intersection of all closed supersets of S, \closure(\closure S) = \closure S, and A ⊆ B implies \closure A ⊆ \closure B.
   - **`[Scratch work]`** (first appearance of "smallest closed superset", ≤ 20 lines): compare with "split the supremum" (`@thm-sup-epsilon`): sup A is an upper bound below every upper bound; \closure S is a closed superset inside every closed superset. For (a), unwind: the goal is X ∖ \closure S open. Givens/Goal table: x ∉ \closure S → some B(x, r) misses S → want a ball around x missing \closure S → each y ∈ B(x, r) has B(y, r − d(x, y)) ⊆ B(x, r) (`thm-ball-open`), which misses S, so y is not adherent. Last row: "B(x, r) itself works". For (c): x ∉ F gives a ball in X ∖ F ⊆ X ∖ S.
   - *Before reading on: which earlier theorem makes the radius r − d(x, y) work?* (self-explanation prompt).
   - Proof: (a) as above, citing `thm-ball-open`. (b) `thm-closed-iff-contains-accumulation-points` (a). (c) X ∖ F is open, so x ∉ F has B(x, r) ⊆ X ∖ F, which misses S; x is not adherent. Consequences: the intersection C of all closed supersets is closed (`thm-closed-set-operations`) and contains S, so \closure S ⊆ C by (c), and C ⊆ \closure S since \closure S is one of them (a, b). Idempotence: apply `thm-closed-iff-contains-accumulation-points` (b) to the closed set \closure S. Monotone: \closure B is a closed superset of A.
   - Aftermath: this is the description later chapters use most; Chapter 4 adds a third, by sequences.
7. **`prp-ball-infinitely-many`** `[Accumulation Points Attract Infinitely Many Points]`: (a) if x ∈ S′, every ball B(x, r) contains infinitely many points of S; (b) hence a finite set has no accumulation points and is closed. Proof (a): if B(x, r) ∩ (S ∖ {x}) = {y₁, …, y_k} with k ≥ 1, then r′ ≔ min d(x, y_i) > 0 gives B(x, r′) ∩ (S ∖ {x}) = ∅, against x ∈ S′ (minimum of finitely many radii, back-reference). (b) from (a) and `thm-closed-iff-contains-accumulation-points`.

**`## When the closed ball is not the closure` (~1,500).**
1. **`exm-discrete-ball-closure`** `[The closed ball can be larger]`. X discrete, |X| ≥ 2: \closure B(x, 1) = {x} ⊊ X = B̄(x, 1). (Weird-table row.)
2. **`prp-normed-ball-closure`** `[Balls in a Normed Space]`: in a normed space (V, ‖·‖) (F4.11), for x ∈ V and r > 0, \closure B(x, r) = B̄(x, r).
   - *Try it first* is **not** used here (budget of two).
   - **`[Scratch work]`** (first appearance of "approach along a segment", ≤ 14 lines): ⊆ is free (`prp-closed-ball-closed-metric` and `thm-closure-smallest-closed` (c)). For ⊇, only the sphere is new: ‖y − x‖ = r. The segment from x to y stays in the ball until its end: z_t ≔ x + t(y − x) has ‖z_t − x‖ = tr < r for 0 ≤ t < 1 and ‖y − z_t‖ = (1 − t)r. Given ε > 0, need (1 − t)r < ε: t ≔ max(0, 1 − ε/(2r)).
   - *Before reading on: where does the proof use that V is a vector space, and why does the discrete metric have no such step?* (self-explanation prompt).
   - Proof: forward, with t placed first and justified last; the case ε ≥ 2r gives t = 0, z = x, ‖y − x‖ = r < ε.
   - Remark: if V = {0}, B(0, r) = {0} = B̄(0, r); the statement holds trivially. Contrast: the discrete metric is not the metric of any norm on a nonzero real vector space (follows from this proposition and `exm-discrete-ball-closure`; one sentence).
3. **`exm-railway-circle`** `[The unit circle of the railway plane]` (answers Q2). S¹ ≔ {p ∈ ℝ² : |p| = 1}. *Solution:* (i) **bounded**: d_R(p, q) ≤ |p| + |q| = 2; distinct p, q ∈ S¹ are not on a common ray (a nonnegative multiple of a unit vector with norm 1 is the vector itself), so d_R(p, q) = 2. (ii) **closed**: for q ∉ S¹, if q = 0 then B_R(0, 1/2) is a disk missing S¹; if q ≠ 0, r ≔ min(|q|, ||q| − 1|) > 0 and B_R(q, r) is a segment of q's ray on which ||p| − |q|| < r ≤ ||q| − 1|, so |p| ≠ 1. (iii) **every point isolated**: B_R(p, 1) = {tp : 0 < t < 2} meets S¹ only in p. (iv) Hence S¹′ ⊆ S¹ (closed) and no point of S¹ is an accumulation point: **S¹′ = ∅**. (v) **uncountable**: t ↦ (t, √(1 − t²)) is injective from (−1, 1) into S¹ (the square root exists by `@thm-nth-root`, since 1 − t² > 0), and (−1, 1) is uncountable (ledger line, §4). Conclusion: a closed, bounded, uncountable set with no accumulation point; Chapter 2's Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem fails in the railway plane. One sentence: Chapter 8 identifies the property of ℝⁿ that the proof of Bolzano–Weierstrass used and the railway plane lacks.
4. **`exm-sup-unit-vectors`** `[Unit vectors in ℓ^∞]`. e_k ∈ ℓ^∞ the sequence with 1 in place k and 0 elsewhere, E ≔ {e_k : k ∈ ℕ}. *Solution:* d_∞(e_j, e_k) = 1 for j ≠ k; E ⊆ B̄(0, 1); for any x ∈ ℓ^∞, B(x, 1/2) contains at most one e_k (two would be at distance < 1 by (M3)), so x is not an accumulation point: E′ = ∅, E closed, bounded, infinite. A countable companion to the railway circle, inside a normed space.
5. **Quick check:** "In the railway plane, find \closure B_R((1, 0), 1) and B̄_R((1, 0), 1). Are they equal?" *Solution:* B_R((1, 0), 1) = {(t, 0) : 0 < t < 2}; its closure is {(t, 0) : 0 ≤ t ≤ 2} (0 is adherent: d_R(0, (t, 0)) = t; (2, 0) likewise; other points have small balls missing it). ### 5.3 `03-interior-boundary-density.md` — Interior, Boundary, and Dense Sets (Apostol 3.16; Rudin, Pugh on density)

**Budget:** ≤ 5,000 (expected ~4,500). Two Quick checks, one warning, one non-example. Key proof: `prp-separated-family`.

**Opening frame (~90).** The interior is the closure's mirror image, and the boundary is where they disagree. Then a set can be "everywhere" without being "large": density, and the spaces where no countable set is dense, which answers Q3.

**`## Interior` (~700).**
1. **`def-interior`** `[Interior]`: \interior S is the set of interior points of S.
2. **`thm-interior-largest-open`** `[The Interior Is the Largest Open Subset]`: \interior S is open, \interior S ⊆ S, and every open U ⊆ S lies in \interior S; S is open iff \interior S = S. Idea (2 lines, back-reference to `thm-ball-open`; no Scratch Work: the technique is not new). Proof short.
3. **`prp-interior-closure-complement`** `[Interior and Closure Are Complementary]`: X ∖ \interior S = \closure(X ∖ S) and X ∖ \closure S = \interior(X ∖ S). Proof: x ∉ \interior S ⇔ every ball meets X ∖ S ⇔ x adherent to X ∖ S; the second from the first applied to X ∖ S. Aftermath: every closure fact has an interior twin by passing to complements (back-reference to the move).

**`## Boundary` (~900).**
1. **Failure-motivated lead (~100).** In ℝⁿ the boundary of B(x, r) is the sphere S(x, r) (Chapter 2 — if AN1.4 does not state it, "as a picture of the disk suggests"). In a discrete space with two or more points, S(x, 1) = X ∖ {x} ≠ ∅, while {x} is both open and closed and nothing should sit "on its edge". Define the boundary by closures, not by spheres.
2. **`def-boundary`** `[Boundary]`: \boundary S ≔ \closure S ∩ \closure(X ∖ S).
3. **`prp-boundary-properties`** `[Properties of the Boundary]`: (a) \boundary S is closed and \boundary S = \boundary(X ∖ S); (b) \boundary S = \closure S ∖ \interior S; (c) X is the disjoint union of \interior S, \boundary S and \interior(X ∖ S); (d) S is open iff S ∩ \boundary S = ∅, and S is closed iff \boundary S ⊆ S. Proof: (a) intersection of two closed sets, symmetric formula; (b) \closure(X ∖ S) = X ∖ \interior S (`prp-interior-closure-complement`); (c) from (b) and \closure S = X ∖ \interior(X ∖ S); (d) from (b) with `thm-interior-largest-open` and `thm-closure-smallest-closed`.
4. **Quick check:** "What is \boundary B(x, 1) in a discrete space? Is it S(x, 1)?" *Solution:* B(x, 1) = {x} is open and closed, so \boundary{x} = {x} ∖ {x} = ∅ by (b); S(x, 1) = X ∖ {x}, nonempty when |X| ≥ 2.
5. **`exm-rationals-interior-boundary`** `[ℚ inside ℝ]`. \interior ℚ = ∅ (`@cor-irrationals-dense`), \closure ℚ = ℝ (`@thm-rationals-dense`), \boundary ℚ = ℝ. Same for ℝ ∖ ℚ. (Weird-table row.)
6. **Warning** `**A boundary can be bigger than the set.**` "\boundary ℚ = ℝ, and the boundary of a set can be all of the space. The picture of a thin rim around a region holds for balls in ℝⁿ, not in general."

**`## Dense sets and separable spaces` (~2,200).**
1. Lead: ℚ is "everywhere" in ℝ, and Chapter 1 proved it (`@thm-rationals-dense`). Name the property.
2. **`def-dense`** `[Dense Set]`: D ⊆ X is dense in X if \closure D = X; equivalently (one-line proof in the block's follow-up sentence) every ball of X meets D, equivalently every nonempty open subset of X meets D. **`def-separable`** `[Separable Space]`: X is separable if it has a countable dense subset.
3. **`thm-rn-separable`** `[ℝⁿ Is Separable]` (recall Chapter 2's rational-balls lemma in the lead, prose pointer until labelled): ℚⁿ is dense in (ℝⁿ, d₂); ℚⁿ is countable (F2.3); so ℝⁿ is separable. Proof: given x and r > 0, choose q_i ∈ ℚ with |q_i − x_i| < r/n (`@thm-rationals-dense`); then d₂(q, x) ≤ d₁(q, x) < r (F4.11). (Use r/n with d₁ to avoid square roots.)
4. **`prp-separated-family`** `[A Separated Family Prevents Separability]` (**key proof**): if A ⊆ X is uncountable and d(a, b) ≥ δ for all distinct a, b ∈ A, for some δ > 0, then X is not separable.
   - *Try it first (ten minutes): suppose D is countable and dense; for each a ∈ A, what does density give you near a, and why can two different a's not share it?*
   - **`[Scratch work]`** (first appearance of "a separated family", ≤ 16 lines). Starts from the obstacles: density gives a point of D in each ball around a, but different a's might get the same point — unless the balls are disjoint. Balls B(a, δ/2) are pairwise disjoint: a point z in B(a, δ/2) ∩ B(b, δ/2) gives d(a, b) < δ by (M3). So "a ↦ some point of D in B(a, δ/2)" is injective from an uncountable set into a countable one. To make "some point" definite with no choice: enumerate D = {q₀, q₁, …} and take the least index.
   - *Before reading on: self-explain why the map is injective.*
   - Proof: forward. Suppose D dense and countable; enumerate D (if D is finite, the same with a finite list; or note a nonempty finite list can be padded). Define φ(a) ≔ q_{k(a)}, k(a) the least k with q_k ∈ B(a, δ/2) (exists by density and well-ordering of ℕ, F2.2 pointer). φ is injective by disjointness. An injection of the uncountable A into the countable D is impossible (F2.3). Remark one clause: no choice principle is used, the enumeration makes the choice.
   - Aftermath: this is the only general tool for non-separability in the book's first volumes.
5. **`exm-railway-not-separable`** `[The railway plane is not separable]` (answers Q3). S¹ with δ = 2 (`exm-railway-circle` (i), (v)). No countable set of towns comes within distance 1 of every town on the unit circle.
6. **`exm-linf-not-separable-preview`** `[ℓ^∞ is not separable]`. The 0–1 sequences form an uncountable subset of ℓ^∞ (F2.3, {0, 1}^ℕ uncountable) at mutual distance 1. Short solution here; the exit check §3 C1 asks for the full write-up and for B([0, 1]).
7. **`prp-separable-countable-base`** `[A Countable Base]`: if X has a countable dense set D, every open U ⊆ X is a union of balls B(q, 1/m) with q ∈ D, m ∈ ℤ_{>0}, B(q, 1/m) ⊆ U; there are countably many such balls. Idea (3 lines). Proof: x ∈ U, B(x, r) ⊆ U; m with 2/m < r (`@cor-archimedean-epsilon`); q ∈ D ∩ B(x, 1/m); then x ∈ B(q, 1/m) ⊆ B(x, 2/m) ⊆ U by (M3). Countability: D × ℤ_{>0} countable (F2.3). Aftermath: Chapter 8 uses this to show that open covers of a separable space have countable subcovers (prose pointer, S2a item 9 confirms the AN2.3 plan).
8. **Non-example and Quick check:** "Is the discrete space ℝ separable? Is the discrete space ℤ?" *Solution:* ℝ: no, `prp-separated-family` with δ = 1. ℤ: yes, ℤ is countable and dense in itself.

### 5.4 `04-metric-subspaces.md` — Subspaces and Relative Topology (Apostol 3.14, relatively open and closed sets)

**Budget:** ≤ 5,000 (expected ~3,800). Two Quick checks, one warning, one non-example. Key proof: `thm-relatively-open`.

**Opening frame (~90).** F4.11 made every subset Y of a metric space into a metric space with the restricted metric. So every word of §1–§3 applies to Y, and now "open" can mean two things.

**`## Is [0, 1) open?` (~600).**
1. **Failure-motivated lead.** Is [0, 1) open? In ℝ, no (§1 non-example). In the space Y = [0, 1] with the restricted metric, B_Y(0, 1/2) = [0, 1/2) ⊆ [0, 1), and every other point of [0, 1) has a small Y-ball inside it: yes. The question has no answer until the surrounding space is named.
2. **`def-relatively-open`** `[Open and Closed in a Subspace]`: for Y ⊆ X with the restricted metric, a set A ⊆ Y is *open in Y* (relatively open) if it is open in the metric space Y, and *closed in Y* if Y ∖ A is open in Y. B_Y(y, r) = B(y, r) ∩ Y; \closure_Y, \interior_Y denote closure and interior in Y.
3. **Warning** `**Always name the space.**` "(0, 1) is closed in the space (0, 1) and not closed in ℝ; [0, 1) is open in [0, 1] and not open in ℝ. Write 'open in Y' whenever more than one space is in play."
4. **Quick check:** "In Y = (0, 1) ∪ (2, 3) ⊆ ℝ, is (0, 1) open in Y? Closed in Y?" *Solution:* both, directly from the definition: each point of (0, 1) has a Y-ball inside (0, 1), and so does each point of Y ∖ (0, 1) = (2, 3) inside (2, 3); so (0, 1) and its complement in Y are both open in Y.

**`## Open sets of a subspace` (~1,200).**
1. **`thm-relatively-open`** `[Open Sets of a Subspace]` (**key proof**): Let Y ⊆ X. A ⊆ Y is open in Y iff A = V ∩ Y for some open V ⊆ X.
   - *Try it first (ten minutes): given A open in Y, build an open set of X whose trace on Y is A. What are you allowed to take unions of?*
   - **`[Scratch work]`** (first appearance of "intersect with the subspace", ≤ 18 lines): the obstacles first — A itself is usually not open in X ([0, 1/2) in [0, 1]); taking "the X-interior of A" loses points (0). The fix: the Y-balls inside A are traces of X-balls, B_Y(y, r) = B(y, r) ∩ Y, so take the union of those X-balls. Givens/Goal table for "V ∩ Y = A": (⊆) a point of V ∩ Y lies in some B(y, r_y) ∩ Y = B_Y(y, r_y) ⊆ A; (⊇) y ∈ B(y, r_y). For (⇐): y ∈ V ∩ Y gives B(y, r) ⊆ V, so B_Y(y, r) ⊆ V ∩ Y.
   - *Before reading on: self-explain the (⊆) inclusion.*
   - Proof: forward. (⇒) V ≔ the union of all balls B(y, r) with y ∈ A, r > 0 and B(y, r) ∩ Y ⊆ A (choice-free, as in `prp-open-union-of-balls`); V open (`thm-open-set-operations`, `thm-ball-open`); V ∩ Y = A by the table. (⇐) as in the Scratch Work.
2. **`cor-relatively-closed`** `[Closed Sets and Closures in a Subspace]`: (a) F ⊆ Y is closed in Y iff F = C ∩ Y for some closed C ⊆ X; (b) for A ⊆ Y, \closure_Y A = \closure A ∩ Y. Proof: (a) Y ∖ F = V ∩ Y ⇔ F = (X ∖ V) ∩ Y (F2.1 identity). (b) for y ∈ Y, every B_Y(y, r) = B(y, r) ∩ Y meets A iff every B(y, r) meets A, since A ⊆ Y.
3. **`prp-open-in-open-subspace`** `[Open Subsets of Open Subspaces]`: if Y is open in X, then A ⊆ Y is open in Y iff A is open in X; if Y is closed in X, then F ⊆ Y is closed in Y iff F is closed in X. Proof: from `thm-relatively-open` and `cor-relatively-closed` with `thm-open-set-operations`, `thm-closed-set-operations`.
4. **Remark (dropped hypothesis):** without "Y open", (0, 1] is open in Y = [0, 1] and not in ℝ; the interior does not behave like the closure: \interior_Y A ⊋ \interior A ∩ Y can happen (A = Y = [0, 1]). (§4 B3 (b).)

**`## Examples` (~1,300).**
1. **`exm-rationals-clopen`** `[A clopen piece of ℚ]`. In Y = ℚ, A ≔ {q ∈ ℚ : q² < 2} is open and closed in ℚ, nonempty, and not all of ℚ. *Solution:* let s = √2 (`@thm-sqrt2-exists`), irrational (`@fd1:thm-sqrt2-irrational`). For q ∈ ℚ, q² < 2 ⇔ −s < q < s ⇔ −s ≤ q ≤ s (equality impossible for rational q). So A = (−s, s) ∩ ℚ = [−s, s] ∩ ℚ: open in ℚ and closed in ℚ by `thm-relatively-open`, `cor-relatively-closed`. One sentence: in Chapter 8's language ℚ is not connected; ℝ has no such subset (pointer).
2. **`exm-harmonic-subspace`** `[The harmonic subspace]`. Y ≔ {1/n : n ≥ 1}, Z ≔ Y ∪ {0} ⊆ ℝ. Every subset of Y is open in Y (each 1/n has B_Y(1/n, 1/n − 1/(n + 1)) = {1/n}): Y has the same open sets as a discrete space. In Z, {0} is not open. (Full description of the open sets of Z is §4 B2.)
3. Short remark: ℤ ⊆ ℝ also has every subset open (balls of radius 1/2).
4. **Quick check:** "Is [0, 1/2) closed in Y = [0, 1/2) ∪ [1, 2]? Open?" *Solution:* both: [0, 1/2) = (−1, 1/2) ∩ Y (open in Y) and = [0, 1/2] ∩ Y (closed in Y), since 1/2 ∉ Y.

### 5.5 `05-equivalent-metrics-and-products.md` — Equivalent Metrics and Product Spaces (Pugh 2.1–2.3; Rudin exercises)

**Budget:** ≤ 5,000 (expected ~4,400). Two Quick checks, one warning, one non-example. Last section: R group.

**Opening frame (~100).** ℝⁿ carries d₁, d₂, d_∞ (F4.11), and Chapter 2 used one of them. Do the theorems depend on which? The answer is a comparison of balls, and it also settles what "open" means in a product X × Y.

**`## Same open sets, different balls` (~1,300).**
1. **Failure-motivated lead.** Call two metrics "the same" if they have the same balls? Then d₁ and d_∞ on ℝ² differ (a diamond is not a square), yet each d₁-ball contains a d_∞-ball around its centre and conversely, so they have the same open sets (by `prp-open-union-of-balls` thinking). Asking for equal balls is too strict; ask for equal open sets.
2. **`def-equivalent-metrics`** `[Topologically Equivalent Metrics]`: d and d′ on X are (topologically) equivalent if they have the same open sets. **`def-strongly-equivalent`** `[Strongly Equivalent Metrics]`: if there are a, b > 0 with a·d ≤ d′ ≤ b·d.
3. **`thm-ball-criterion`** `[Ball Criterion for Equivalence]`: d and d′ are equivalent iff for every x ∈ X and r > 0 there are s, s′ > 0 with B_{d′}(x, s) ⊆ B_d(x, r) and B_d(x, s′) ⊆ B_{d′}(x, r).
   - **`[Scratch work]`** (first appearance of "balls inside balls", ≤ 12 lines): (⇐) a d-open U and x ∈ U: a d-ball in U, a d′-ball inside it; so U is d′-open. (⇒) B_d(x, r) is d-open (`thm-ball-open`), hence d′-open, and x is in it.
   - Proof: forward, both halves, symmetric halves skipped by STYLE §4.
4. **`cor-strong-equivalence`** `[Strongly Equivalent Metrics Are Equivalent]`: B_{d′}(x, ar) ⊆ B_d(x, r) and B_d(x, r/b) ⊆ B_{d′}(x, r). Proof 2 lines.
5. **`cor-rn-metrics-equivalent`** `[The Metrics of ℝⁿ Are Equivalent]`: d₁, d₂, d_∞ on ℝⁿ are pairwise strongly equivalent (d_∞ ≤ d₂ ≤ d₁ ≤ n·d_∞, F4.11), so they have the same open, closed, dense sets, closures, interiors, boundaries. Aftermath: every statement of Chapter 2 is the same for all three; "open in ℝⁿ" is unambiguous.
6. **Quick check:** "Find s with B_∞(0, s) ⊆ B₁(0, 1) in ℝ³, and s′ with B₁(0, s′) ⊆ B_∞(0, 1)." *Solution:* s = 1/3 (d₁ ≤ 3d_∞); s′ = 1 (d_∞ ≤ d₁).

**`## Equivalent but not alike` (~1,100).**
1. **`exm-truncated-metric`** `[Truncating a metric]`. d′ ≔ min(d, 1) is a metric, equivalent to d, and every subset of X is d′-bounded. *Solution:* (M3): if d(x, y) ≥ 1 or d(y, z) ≥ 1 the right side is ≥ 1 ≥ d′(x, z); otherwise it is d(x, y) + d(y, z) ≥ d(x, z) ≥ d′(x, z). For r ≤ 1 the balls agree: B_{d′}(x, r) = B_d(x, r); `thm-ball-criterion` with s = s′ = min(r, 1). If d is unbounded (ℝ), d′ is not strongly equivalent to d: a·d ≤ d′ ≤ 1 fails for d large. Aftermath: boundedness is not determined by the open sets. (Weird-table row.)
2. **`exm-harmonic-discrete-metric`** `[Same open sets as a discrete space]`. On Y = {1/n : n ≥ 1}, |x − y| and the discrete metric are equivalent (both make every set open, `exm-harmonic-subspace`), but not strongly: d_disc(1/n, 1/(n + 1)) = 1 while |1/n − 1/(n + 1)| = 1/(n(n + 1)), so no a > 0 has a·d_disc ≤ |·|. One sentence pointer: Chapter 5 shows that 1, 1/2, 1/3, … behaves differently in the two metrics, though they have the same open sets.
3. **`exm-railway-not-equivalent`** `[The railway metric is finer]`. d_R and d₂ on ℝ² are not equivalent: the segment of `exm-railway-balls` is d_R-open, not d₂-open. In terms of `thm-ball-criterion`: every d₂-ball contains the d_R-ball of the same radius (d₂ ≤ d_R), so every d₂-open set is d_R-open; but B_R((1, 0), 1/2), the segment {(t, 0) : 1/2 < t < 3/2}, contains no d₂-ball around (1, 0).
4. **Warning** `**Equivalent metrics need not agree on size.**` "min(d, 1) and d have the same open sets, but ℝ is bounded for one and not for the other. Being bounded, and (Chapter 5) being Cauchy, are properties of the metric, not of the open sets."

**`## Products` (~1,200).**
1. Recall F4.11's product metric on X × Y. Define (if F4.11 has not) ρ₁ ≔ d_X + d_Y, ρ₂ ≔ (d_X² + d_Y²)^{1/2}, ρ_∞ ≔ max(d_X, d_Y), coordinatewise; they satisfy ρ_∞ ≤ ρ₂ ≤ ρ₁ ≤ 2ρ_∞ (the case n = 2 of F4.11's inequalities applied to the pair (d_X, d_Y)), so they are strongly equivalent (`cor-strong-equivalence`) and "open in X × Y" is unambiguous. Work with ρ_∞, whose balls are products: B((x, y), r) = B_X(x, r) × B_Y(y, r).
2. **`thm-product-open-sets`** `[Open and Closed Sets in a Product]`: (a) if U ⊆ X, V ⊆ Y are open, U × V is open; (b) W ⊆ X × Y is open iff for every (x, y) ∈ W there are open U ∋ x, V ∋ y with U × V ⊆ W; (c) \closure(A × B) = \closure A × \closure B; in particular A × B is closed when A and B are. Idea (2 lines: ρ_∞-balls are products of balls). Proof: (a) min of two radii; (b) (⇒) the ρ_∞-ball is such a U × V (`thm-ball-open`); (⇐) U × V contains a ρ_∞-ball around (x, y) by (a); (c) (x, y) adherent to A × B ⇔ for every r, B_X(x, r) × B_Y(y, r) meets A × B ⇔ for every r, B_X(x, r) meets A and B_Y(y, r) meets B.
3. Remark: with X = ℝ^k, Y = ℝ^m and d_∞ on each, ρ_∞ is d_∞ on ℝ^{k+m}; by `cor-rn-metrics-equivalent` the product topology of ℝ^k × ℝ^m is that of ℝ^{k+m}.
4. **`exm-open-disk-not-product`** `[An open set that is not a product]` (non-example). B₂(0, 1) ⊆ ℝ² is open but not of the form U × V: if B₂(0, 1) = U × V, then (0.9, 0) and (0, 0.9) in it force 0.9 ∈ U and 0.9 ∈ V, so (0.9, 0.9) ∈ U × V, but |(0.9, 0.9)| = 0.9√2 > 1. Aftermath: (b) is the correct statement: open sets are unions of open rectangles.
5. **Quick check:** "In ℝ², find \closure(ℚ × [0, 1)) and \interior(ℚ × [0, 1))." *Solution:* ℝ × [0, 1] by (c); ∅ (any ball contains points with an irrational first coordinate).

(So d_R is not the metric of a norm, a second proof after `prp-normed-ball-closure`'s remark.)
- **R4** `[R4]` (no hint of method). "Is ℚ² dense in the railway plane?" *Solution:* No. Take p = (1, √2) and r = 1 < |p| = √3: B_R(p, 1) = {tp : t > 0, |t − 1|·√3 < 1}. A point tp in ℚ² needs t ∈ ℚ and t√2 ∈ ℚ, impossible for t ≠ 0 (`@fd1:thm-sqrt2-irrational`). So B_R(p, 1) misses ℚ². Tools: `def-dense` (every ball meets D) and `exm-railway-balls`; the look-alike tool `thm-rn-separable` fails because d_R-balls are not d₂-balls (`exm-railway-not-equivalent`), and the railway plane is not separable at all (`exm-railway-not-separable`), which gives a second proof.
