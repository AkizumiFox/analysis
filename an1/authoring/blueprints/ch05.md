### 4.1 Section 5.1: Complete Metric Spaces (`01-complete-metric-spaces.md`)

**Budget:** ≤ 4,500 (expected ~3,900). Two Quick checks at least, two warnings, one remark.

**Opening frame (~180).** The spiral, written out: Chapter 1 took the least upper bound axiom (`@ax-least-upper-bound`) and called it the completeness axiom; Chapter 4 proved from it that every Cauchy sequence of reals converges (`@thm-cauchy-criterion`) and defined Cauchy sequences in any metric space (`@def-cauchy-sequence`), where convergent sequences are Cauchy (`@thm-convergent-implies-cauchy`). What changes: in a general metric space the converse can fail, and when it holds the space is called complete. What survives: everything proved for Cauchy sequences in Chapter 4.

**`## When Cauchy sequences fail to converge` (~600; failure-motivated definition).**
1. Two failures. (a) In ℚ: the hook's fractions, or (already proved) the sequence of decimal truncations of √2, Cauchy in ℚ with no rational limit (cite AN1.2's example by label). (b) In X = (0, 1] with |x − y|: xₙ = 1/n is Cauchy, and it converges in ℝ to 0 ∉ X; by uniqueness of limits (`@thm-limit-unique`) it has no limit in X.
2. The diagnosis, one paragraph: in both cases nothing is wrong with the sequence, which converges in ℝ. The defect is in the space, which lacks the point. Hence the definition is a property of the space.
3. **`def-complete-metric-space`** `[Complete Metric Space]`: "A metric space (X, d) is **complete** if every Cauchy sequence in X converges to a point of X." In symbols: for every sequence (xₙ) in X, if ∀ε > 0 ∃N ∀m, n ≥ N d(xₘ, xₙ) < ε, then ∃x ∈ X with xₙ → x.
4. **Quick check** (weird examples, motivation mode, straight after the definition): "Decide from the definition alone: (a) ℤ with |x − y|; (b) {1/n : n ≥ 1} with |x − y|; (c) any set with the discrete metric." *Solution:* (a) complete: with ε = 1/2 a Cauchy sequence of integers has |xₘ − xₙ| < 1/2, hence xₘ = xₙ, for m, n ≥ N, so it is eventually constant and converges; (b) not complete: (1/n) is Cauchy, its only candidate limit 0 is missing; (c) complete, as in (a) with ε = 1/2, since distinct points are at distance 1.
5. **Warning** `**Complete belongs to the metric.**` "Being Cauchy depends on the distance, not only on which sequences converge. The last subsection gives two metrics on ℝ with the same convergent sequences, one complete and one not."
6. One paragraph paying AN1.1's promise: "The word *complete* now has two meanings: an ordered field satisfying the least upper bound axiom, and a metric space in which Cauchy sequences converge. For ℝ both hold. Chapter 10 shows that for an ordered field the second, together with the Archimedean property, is equivalent to the first."

**`## Euclidean space is complete` (~800).**
1. **`thm-rn-complete`** `[ℝⁿ Is Complete]`: "ℝⁿ with the Euclidean metric d₂ is complete." Idea (3 lines): reduce to coordinates (|x_k − y_k| ≤ d₂(x, y) ≤ √n · max_k |x_k − y_k|), and use the Cauchy criterion in each coordinate. Proof: let (x⁽ᵐ⁾) be Cauchy in (ℝⁿ, d₂). For each k, |x⁽ᵐ⁾_k − x⁽ᵖ⁾_k| ≤ d₂(x⁽ᵐ⁾, x⁽ᵖ⁾), so the k-th coordinates form a Cauchy sequence in ℝ, converging to some a_k by `@thm-cauchy-criterion`. Let a = (a₁, …, aₙ); given ε > 0 choose N_k with |x⁽ᵐ⁾_k − a_k| < ε/√n for m ≥ N_k, N = max N_k (finitely many), then d₂(x⁽ᵐ⁾, a) < ε for m ≥ N. **Alternatively** paragraph (Apostol's route, ≤ 80 words): a Cauchy sequence is bounded (`@thm-cauchy-bounded`), so it has a convergent subsequence by Bolzano–Weierstrass in ℝⁿ (AN1.4), and a Cauchy sequence with a convergent subsequence converges (`@thm-cauchy-subsequence`).
2. **`prp-comparable-metrics-cauchy`** `[Comparable Metrics Have the Same Cauchy Sequences]`: "Let d and d′ be metrics on X with c·d ≤ d′ ≤ C·d for constants c, C > 0. Then d and d′ have the same Cauchy sequences and the same convergent sequences, with the same limits. In particular (X, d) is complete if and only if (X, d′) is." Proof ≤ 6 lines (ε/C and cε). Aftermath: with F4.11's d_∞ ≤ d₂ ≤ d₁ ≤ n·d_∞, ℝⁿ is complete under d₁ and d_∞ as well; ℂ, as ℝ² (F4.1), is complete.
3. **Quick check:** "Why does the proof of @thm-rn-complete need N = max N_k, and why would it fail for infinitely many coordinates?" *Solution:* each coordinate needs its own N_k; a maximum of finitely many exists, of infinitely many need not (N_k = k). Section 5.2 meets this.

**`## Closed subsets and complete subspaces` (~1,000).**
1. Lead: which subsets of a complete space are complete in the restricted metric?
2. **`thm-closed-subset-complete`** `[Complete Subspaces Are the Closed Ones]`: "Let (X, d) be a metric space and F ⊆ X, with the restricted metric. (a) If F is complete, then F is closed in X. (b) If X is complete and F is closed in X, then F is complete." 
3. **`[Scratch work]`** (≤ 8 lines, no table): for (b), a Cauchy sequence in F is Cauchy in X, so X supplies a limit; the only question is whether the limit is in F, and closedness is exactly the statement that limits of sequences in F stay in F (`@thm-closed-iff-sequentially-closed`). For (a), the reverse: take a sequence in F converging in X; it is Cauchy, F gives it a limit in F, and uniqueness of limits identifies the two.
4. **Proof.** (a) Let (xₙ) in F converge in X to x. Then (xₙ) is Cauchy (`@thm-convergent-implies-cauchy`), so converges in F to some y ∈ F; as a sequence in X it converges to both x and y, so x = y ∈ F (`@thm-limit-unique`). So F is closed (`@thm-closed-iff-sequentially-closed`). (b) Let (xₙ) be Cauchy in F. It is Cauchy in X, so xₙ → x ∈ X; F is closed, so x ∈ F; the distance is the same, so xₙ → x in F.
5. **Warning** `**Closed in what?**` "(b) needs X complete. The set (0, 1] is closed in the space (0, 2) (its complement (1, 2) is open there), yet it is not complete." 
6. **`exm-complete-gallery`** `[Complete and Incomplete Subsets of ℝⁿ]`: decide and justify: (a) [a, b] and [0, ∞) (complete, closed in ℝ); (b) every closed bounded subset of ℝⁿ, and every closed ball (complete); (c) ℕ (complete: closed, or as in the Quick check); (d) ℚ ∩ [0, 1] (incomplete: not closed in ℝ, `@thm-rationals-dense` gives rationals tending to 1/√2, and (a) of the theorem); (e) ℝ ∖ {0} (incomplete: 1/n). *Solution* for each in 1–3 lines.
7. **Quick check:** "Is {(x, y) ∈ ℝ² : y = 1/x, x > 0} complete?" *Solution:* yes. It is closed: if (xₖ, 1/xₖ) → (x, y) with xₖ > 0, then xₖ · (1/xₖ) = 1 → xy (limit laws), so xy = 1, hence x ≠ 0, and x ≥ 0 as a limit of positives, so x > 0 and y = 1/x. By (b) it is complete.

**`## Completeness is not a topological property` (~900).**
1. **`exm-completeness-not-topological`** `[Same Convergent Sequences, Different Completeness]` (weird row 1). Let φ(x) = x/(1 + |x|) and d_φ(x, y) = |φ(x) − φ(y)| on ℝ. Show: (a) d_φ is a metric; (b) a sequence converges to x for d_φ iff it converges to x for |·|; (c) (n)_{n≥1} is Cauchy for d_φ, so (ℝ, d_φ) is not complete, although (ℝ, |·|) is.
   *Solution.* (a) φ is strictly increasing (both pieces), so injective; the axioms transfer from |·| on φ(ℝ). (b) Two inequalities: |φ(x) − φ(y)| ≤ |x − y| for all x, y (same sign: φ(x) − φ(y) = (x − y)/((1+|x|)(1+|y|)); opposite signs: |φ(x)| + |φ(y)| ≤ |x| + |y| = |x − y|); and with ψ(u) = u/(1 − |u|) the inverse on (−1, 1), |ψ(u) − ψ(v)| ≤ |u − v|/(1 − r)² when |u|, |v| ≤ r < 1 (same sign: (u − v)/((1−|u|)(1−|v|)); opposite: |ψ(u)| + |ψ(v)| ≤ (|u| + |v|)/(1 − r)). If φ(xₙ) → φ(x), take r = (1 + |φ(x)|)/2; eventually |φ(xₙ)| ≤ r, so |xₙ − x| ≤ |φ(xₙ) − φ(x)|/(1 − r)² → 0. (c) d_φ(m, n) = |1/(1+n) − 1/(1+m)| ≤ 1/(1 + min(m, n)); it does not converge for d_φ, since by (b) it would converge for |·|, and it is unbounded.
2. Aftermath (2 sentences): same convergent sequences means the same closed sets (sequential characterization), hence the same open sets: the two metrics give ℝ the same topology (Chapter 3's word). Completeness is not decided by the topology. **Remark:** φ is an isometry of (ℝ, d_φ) onto (−1, 1) with |·|; the example is (−1, 1) in disguise, and §5.6 B2 finds its completion.
3. **Quick check:** "Why does @prp-comparable-metrics-cauchy not apply to |·| and d_φ?" *Solution:* no C with |x − y| ≤ C·d_φ(x, y) for all x, y: take x = n, y = n + 1, where d_φ → 0.

### 4.2 Section 5.2: Sequence Spaces, and Spaces That Are Not Complete (`02-sequence-spaces.md`)

**Budget:** ≤ 5,000 (expected ~4,500). Key proof: `thm-ell-one-complete`.

**Opening frame (~150).** In ℝⁿ completeness was proved coordinate by coordinate, and the proof needed a maximum of n thresholds (the Quick check of 5.1). Spaces of sequences have infinitely many coordinates. They are the first infinite-dimensional spaces of the book, and the place where proving completeness becomes a three-step method that the rest of analysis reuses for spaces of functions.

**`## Spaces of sequences` (~700).** Sums of nonnegative terms are defined as suprema of partial sums, so that no theory of series is needed (series are *Integration, Series, and Function Spaces*, Chapter 4; one sentence says that the definitions agree with the ones there, since increasing partial sums converge to their supremum).
- **`def-ell-one`**: ℓ¹ is the set of real sequences x = (x_k)_{k≥1} with ‖x‖₁ := sup_K Σ_{k≤K} |x_k| < ∞; d₁(x, y) = ‖x − y‖₁. The triangle inequality: for each K, Σ_{k≤K} |x_k − z_k| ≤ Σ_{k≤K} |x_k − y_k| + Σ_{k≤K} |y_k − z_k| ≤ d₁(x, y) + d₁(y, z); take the sup over K. (So x − y ∈ ℓ¹ when x, y ∈ ℓ¹.)
- **`def-ell-two`**: the same with Σ x_k² and ‖x‖₂ = (sup_K Σ_{k≤K} x_k²)^{1/2}. Triangle inequality from the finite Minkowski inequality (F4.8, Cauchy–Schwarz), then the sup over K.
- **`def-ell-infinity`**: bounded sequences, ‖x‖_∞ = sup_k |x_k|.
- One sentence: if AN2.1 introduced these spaces, this is a recall (see §0).
- **Quick check:** "Is (1/k)_{k≥1} in ℓ¹? in ℓ²? in ℓ^∞?" *Solution:* not in ℓ¹ (Σ_{k≤2ʲ} 1/k ≥ 1 + j/2, grouping into blocks (2^{i−1}, 2^i], each ≥ 1/2); in ℓ² (Σ_{k≤K} 1/k² ≤ 1 + Σ_{2≤k≤K} 1/(k(k−1)) = 2 − 1/K < 2, telescoping); in ℓ^∞.

**`## Coordinatewise limits are only candidates` (~400).**
- **`exm-coordinatewise-not-enough`** `[Unit Vectors in ℓ¹]`: e⁽ᵐ⁾ = (0, …, 0, 1, 0, …) (1 in place m). Each coordinate sequence is eventually 0, so the coordinatewise limit is 0, but d₁(e⁽ᵐ⁾, 0) = 1 for all m. Moreover d₁(e⁽ᵐ⁾, e⁽ᵖ⁾) = 2 for m ≠ p, so the sequence is not Cauchy; the example shows that the coordinatewise limit is a *candidate* that must be checked, not a limit.
- Lead into the theorem: for a Cauchy sequence the candidate is right, and the proof has three steps.

**`## ℓ¹ is complete` (~1,500).**
1. **`thm-ell-one-complete`** `[ℓ¹ Is Complete]`: "(ℓ¹, d₁) is complete."
2. **`[Scratch work]`** (≈ 20 lines), with the Givens/Goal table:

   | Givens | Goal |
   |---|---|
   | (x⁽ᵐ⁾) Cauchy in ℓ¹ | some x ∈ ℓ¹ with d₁(x⁽ᵐ⁾, x) → 0 |
   | for each k: \|x⁽ᵐ⁾_k − x⁽ᵖ⁾_k\| ≤ d₁(x⁽ᵐ⁾, x⁽ᵖ⁾) | a candidate x: x_k = lim_m x⁽ᵐ⁾_k (Step 1) |
   | d₁(x⁽ᵐ⁾, x⁽ᵖ⁾) < ε for m, p ≥ N | d₁(x⁽ᵐ⁾, x) ≤ ε for m ≥ N (Step 2) — but d₁(·, x) is a sup of infinite length, and "let p → ∞" inside it is not licensed |
   | finite sums: Σ_{k≤K} \|x⁽ᵐ⁾_k − x⁽ᵖ⁾_k\| < ε | let p → ∞ in a finite sum (limit laws), then sup over K |
   | x⁽ᴺ⁾ ∈ ℓ¹ and x⁽ᴺ⁾ − x has finite norm | x ∈ ℓ¹ (Step 3), by the triangle inequality |

   The gap is the middle row; truncation closes it. "The proof writes this as three steps."
3. **Proof**, as **Step 1. Candidate.** / **Step 2. Upgrade.** / **Step 3. Belong.** In Step 2 the limit p → ∞ of Σ_{k≤K} |x⁽ᵐ⁾_k − x⁽ᵖ⁾_k| < ε gives Σ_{k≤K} |x⁽ᵐ⁾_k − x_k| ≤ ε by `@thm-algebraic-limit` and `@thm-order-limit`; the sup over K is then ≤ ε. Step 3: x = x⁽ᴺ⁾ − (x⁽ᴺ⁾ − x) is in ℓ¹ by the triangle inequality of `def-ell-one`. Then Step 2 says d₁(x⁽ᵐ⁾, x) ≤ ε for m ≥ N, which is convergence. (Order note for the drafter: Step 2's bound on partial sums already shows x⁽ᵐ⁾ − x ∈ ℓ¹, which Step 3 uses; say so in one clause.)
4. **Aftermath:** the three steps are the chapter's main named move; *Integration, Series, and Function Spaces*, Chapter 6, runs them for spaces of functions.
5. **Quick check:** "In Step 2, the strict inequality < ε became ≤ ε. Why could it not stay strict?" *Solution:* limits preserve ≤, not <: 1/p > 0 for all p, and lim 1/p = 0.
6. **Warning** `**Do not pass to the limit inside an infinite sum.**` "'Let p → ∞ in d₁(x⁽ᵐ⁾, x⁽ᵖ⁾) < ε' looks shorter, but d₁ is a supremum of sums of unboundedly many terms, and the limit laws are about finitely many. Truncate first."

**`## ℓ² is complete` (~350).**
- **`thm-ell-two-complete`** `[ℓ² Is Complete]`. Idea (2 lines): as in `@thm-ell-one-complete`, with squares. Proof (≤ 8 lines): Step 1 uses |x⁽ᵐ⁾_k − x⁽ᵖ⁾_k| ≤ d₂; Step 2 truncates Σ_{k≤K} (x⁽ᵐ⁾_k − x⁽ᵖ⁾_k)² < ε²; Step 3 by the triangle inequality of `def-ell-two`. This is the faded (Idea-level) use of the move.

**`## Two spaces that are not complete` (~1,200).**
1. Lead: incomplete spaces are not exotic; two appear as soon as one restricts to "simple" sequences or functions.
2. **`exm-finite-sequences-incomplete`** `[Finitely Nonzero Sequences]` (weird row 2). c₀₀ = sequences with only finitely many nonzero terms, a subset of ℓ¹, with d₁. x⁽ᵐ⁾ = (1, 1/2, …, 1/2^{m−1}, 0, 0, …) (x⁽ᵐ⁾_k = 2^{1−k} for k ≤ m, else 0). Show (x⁽ᵐ⁾) is Cauchy and has no limit in c₀₀. *Solution:* for m < p, d₁(x⁽ᵐ⁾, x⁽ᵖ⁾) = Σ_{k=m+1}^{p} 2^{1−k} < 2^{1−m}. If x⁽ᵐ⁾ → y ∈ c₀₀, then |x⁽ᵐ⁾_k − y_k| ≤ d₁(x⁽ᵐ⁾, y) → 0 forces y_k = 2^{1−k} for every k (x⁽ᵐ⁾_k = 2^{1−k} once m ≥ k), so y has no zero term, contradicting y ∈ c₀₀. In ℓ¹ the sequence converges, to (2^{1−k}) (by `thm-ell-one-complete` or directly). Aftermath: c₀₀ is not closed in ℓ¹ (`thm-closed-subset-complete` (a)); it is "ℓ¹ with holes", and Section 5.6 shows that ℓ¹ is exactly what fills them.
3. **`exm-polynomials-incomplete`** `[Polynomials with the Sup Distance]` (weird row 3). P = real polynomials on [0, 1] with d(p, q) = sup_{0≤x≤1} |p(x) − q(x)| (the sup exists: |p(x)| ≤ Σ|c_j| on [0, 1]). sₙ(x) = Σ_{k=0}^{n} (x/2)^k. Show (sₙ) is Cauchy and has no limit in P. *Solution:* for m < n and x ∈ [0, 1], 0 ≤ sₙ(x) − sₘ(x) = Σ_{k=m+1}^{n} (x/2)^k ≤ Σ_{k=m+1}^{n} 2^{−k} < 2^{−m}. If d(sₙ, p) → 0 for some p ∈ P, then for each x ∈ [0, 1], sₙ(x) → p(x); but sₙ(x) = (1 − (x/2)^{n+1})/(1 − x/2) → 2/(2 − x) (F4.6 for the finite geometric sum, `@exm-geometric-null`). So (2 − x)p(x) = 2 for all x ∈ [0, 1]. The polynomial (2 − x)p(x) − 2 has infinitely many roots, so it is the zero polynomial; but if p has degree d ≥ 0 with leading coefficient c ≠ 0, (2 − x)p(x) − 2 has degree d + 1 and leading coefficient −c ≠ 0 (and p = 0 gives −2 ≠ 0). Contradiction. The root bound ("a nonzero polynomial of degree d has at most d roots") is cited from *Analytic Preliminaries* if F4.6/F4.7 states it; otherwise as a school fact with the factor theorem (S2a item 6).
4. **Remark** (outlook, 2 sentences): the completion of P in this distance is the space C[0, 1] of continuous functions: completeness of C[0, 1] is *Integration, Series, and Function Spaces*, Chapter 6, and density of polynomials (Weierstrass) is its Chapter 12. One more sentence: continuous functions with the integral distance are a third incomplete space, met there.
5. **Quick check:** "Is P with the sup distance on [0, 1] the same metric space as P with the sup distance on [0, 2]? Is (sₙ) Cauchy there?" *Solution:* different metrics on the same set; on [0, 2], sₙ(2) = n + 1, so d(sₙ, sₘ) ≥ |n − m|: not Cauchy. Cauchy-ness depends on the metric.

### 4.3 Section 5.3: Nested Closed Sets (`03-nested-closed-sets.md`)

**Budget:** ≤ 4,000 (expected ~3,300).

**Opening frame (~150).** Spiral: Chapter 1 proved that nested closed bounded intervals in ℝ share a point (`@thm-nested-intervals`), and no condition on their lengths was needed. In a metric space there are no intervals; closed sets replace them, and a size condition becomes necessary. This section finds the condition, proves the theorem, and shows that it characterizes completeness.

**`## Diameter` (~300).** `def-diameter` (diam A = sup{d(x, y) : x, y ∈ A} for nonempty A; A bounded iff diam A < ∞), or the recall of AN2.1's. **`lem-diameter-closure`** `[Closure Does Not Increase Diameter]`: diam cl A = diam A. Proof (≤ 6 lines): for x, y ∈ cl A take aₙ → x, bₙ → y in A (`@thm-closure-sequences`); d(x, y) ≤ d(x, aₙ) + d(aₙ, bₙ) + d(bₙ, y) ≤ d(x, aₙ) + diam A + d(bₙ, y); let n → ∞. **Quick check:** "diam of the open unit ball in (ℝ², d₂), and of {1/n : n ≥ 1}?" *Solution:* 2 (points (±(1 − t), 0)); 1 (sup of 1 − 1/n, not attained).

**`## Three ways to lose the point` (~800; failure before the theorem).**
- **`exm-nested-failures`** `[Nested Sets with Empty Intersection]`. In each, F₁ ⊇ F₂ ⊇ … are nonempty and ⋂Fₙ = ∅; find the hypothesis that fails. (a) Fₙ = (0, 1/n] in ℝ: not closed. (b) Fₙ = [n, ∞) in ℝ: closed, but diam Fₙ = ∞. (c) Fₙ = {q ∈ ℚ : |q − √2| ≤ 1/n} in ℚ: closed in ℚ, diam ≤ 2/n → 0, but ℚ is not complete. *Solution* 2 lines each.
- **`exm-nested-balls-empty`** `[A Complete Space Where Nested Closed Balls Miss]` (weird row 4). X = ℕ_{≥1}, d(m, n) = 1 + 1/min(m, n) for m ≠ n, d(n, n) = 0. Show: (a) d is a metric; (b) (X, d) is complete; (c) the closed balls B[n, 1 + 1/n] = {m : m ≥ n} are nested, nonempty and closed, with empty intersection. *Solution:* (a) distinct points are at distance in (1, 2], so for distinct a, b, c, d(a, c) ≤ 2 < d(a, b) + d(b, c); the cases with two equal points are trivial. (b) With ε = 1, a Cauchy sequence is eventually constant. (c) For m ≠ n, d(n, m) ≤ 1 + 1/n iff 1/min(m, n) ≤ 1/n iff min(m, n) ≥ n iff m ≥ n. Every subset is closed (every convergent sequence is eventually constant). ⋂{m ≥ n} = ∅. Aftermath: the radii decrease to 1, not to 0. In ℝ the length condition was not needed because closed bounded sets in ℝ are compact (Chapter 8 explains); in a general complete space it is.

**`## The Cantor intersection theorem` (~1,200).**
1. **`thm-cantor-intersection`** `[Cantor Intersection Theorem]`: "Let (X, d) be complete, and F₁ ⊇ F₂ ⊇ ⋯ nonempty closed subsets with diam Fₙ → 0. Then ⋂ₙ Fₙ consists of exactly one point."
2. **`[Scratch work]`** with a table:

   | Givens | Goal |
   |---|---|
   | Fₙ nonempty, nested, closed; diam Fₙ → 0; X complete | a point in every Fₙ; no second one |
   | choose xₙ ∈ Fₙ | (xₙ) Cauchy? for m, p ≥ n, xₘ, x_p ∈ Fₙ (nested), so d(xₘ, x_p) ≤ diam Fₙ |
   | xₙ → x (complete) | x ∈ Fₙ for each n: the tail (x_k)_{k≥n} lies in Fₙ, which is closed |
   | x, y in all Fₙ | d(x, y) ≤ diam Fₙ → 0 |

   One sentence: choosing one point from each of infinitely many sets uses countable choice (`@fd2:ax-choice`), as the proof says in a clause.
3. **Proof** (8–10 lines) following the table.
4. Aftermath, and **Remark** (converse question): "Does this property characterize completeness?" leads to:
5. **`thm-cantor-intersection-converse`** `[Nested Closed Sets Characterize Completeness]`: "If every nested sequence of nonempty closed subsets of X with diameters tending to 0 has nonempty intersection, then X is complete." Idea (3 lines): the tails of a Cauchy sequence, closed up. Proof: (xₙ) Cauchy; Fₙ = cl{x_k : k ≥ n}; nested, nonempty, closed; diam Fₙ = diam{x_k : k ≥ n} (`@lem-diameter-closure`) → 0 because the sequence is Cauchy; x ∈ ⋂Fₙ; d(xₙ, x) ≤ diam Fₙ → 0.
6. **Quick check:** "Which hypothesis of @thm-cantor-intersection does @exm-nested-balls-empty violate, and which does @exm-nested-failures (c) violate?" *Solution:* diam → 0 (the diameters are ≥ 1 for every n: d(n, n+1) = 1 + 1/n); completeness.
7. **Looking ahead** (prose, ≤ 80 words): in compact spaces nested nonempty closed sets always meet, with no diameter condition (Chapter 8); the theorem is the engine of the Baire category theorem (*Integration, Series, and Function Spaces*, Chapter 12); Chapter 10 compares it with Chapter 1's nested intervals.

### 4.4 Section 5.4: The Contraction Mapping Theorem (`04-contraction-mapping.md`)

**Budget:** ≤ 5,000 (expected ~4,800). Key proof: `thm-banach-fixed-point`.

**Opening frame (~200).** Back to the hook: 1, 2, 3/2, 5/3, 8/5, … are the iterates x_{n+1} = T(xₙ) of T(x) = 1 + 1/x. Chapter 4 met another iteration, x_{n+1} = (xₙ + 2/xₙ)/2, and proved by monotonicity that it converges to √2 (cite by label if AN1.2 keeps it). The questions here: when do iterates of a map converge, is the limit a solution of x = T(x), is that solution unique, and how far is xₙ from it? One sentence: a solution of x = T(x) is a **fixed point** of T (**`def-fixed-point`**, inline definition block).

**`## Maps that pull points together` (~800; failure-motivated definition).**
1. First attempt: "T shrinks every distance", d(Tx, Ty) < d(x, y) for x ≠ y.
2. **`exm-strict-shrink-no-fixed-point`** `[A Map That Shrinks Every Distance and Fixes Nothing]` (weird row 5). X = [1, ∞) (complete: closed in ℝ), T(x) = x + 1/x. Show: T maps X into X; |Tx − Ty| < |x − y| for x ≠ y; T has no fixed point; the iterates from x₀ = 1 tend to ∞. *Solution:* T(x) ≥ 2 by x + 1/x − 2 = (x − 1)²/x ≥ 0; Tx − Ty = (x − y)(1 − 1/(xy)) with 0 ≤ 1 − 1/(xy) < 1 (xy ≥ 1); x + 1/x = x is impossible; x_{n+1}² = xₙ² + 2 + 1/xₙ² ≥ xₙ² + 2, so xₙ² ≥ 1 + 2n. The factor 1 − 1/(xy) is below 1 at each pair, but it tends to 1 as x, y → ∞: there is no single factor below 1 that works for all pairs.
3. **`def-contraction`** `[Contraction]`: "A map T : X → X of a metric space to itself is a **contraction** if there is a q with 0 ≤ q < 1 such that d(Tx, Ty) ≤ q·d(x, y) for all x, y ∈ X; q is a **contraction constant**." Symbols: ∃q ∈ [0, 1) ∀x, y ∈ X d(Tx, Ty) ≤ q d(x, y).
4. **Quick check** (the quantifier swap, STYLE §10): "Swap the quantifiers: ∀x, y ∈ X ∃q ∈ [0, 1) with d(Tx, Ty) ≤ q d(x, y). Which maps satisfy it?" *Solution:* exactly the maps with d(Tx, Ty) < d(x, y) for x ≠ y (take q = d(Tx, Ty)/d(x, y), and any q when x = y): the weaker property of the example above, which allows q to depend on the pair.

**`## The theorem` (~1,500).**
1. **`thm-banach-fixed-point`** `[Contraction Mapping Theorem]`: "Let (X, d) be a nonempty complete metric space and T : X → X a contraction with constant q. Then: (a) T has exactly one fixed point x*; (b) for every x₀ ∈ X the iterates xₙ = Tⁿx₀ converge to x*; (c) d(xₙ, x*) ≤ qⁿ/(1 − q) · d(x₀, x₁) (a priori bound); (d) d(xₙ, x*) ≤ q/(1 − q) · d(xₙ₋₁, xₙ) for n ≥ 1 (a posteriori bound)."
2. **`[Scratch work]`** (≈ 20 lines), table:

   | Givens | Goal |
   |---|---|
   | d(Tx, Ty) ≤ q d(x, y), q < 1; X complete | a fixed point; its uniqueness; the bounds |
   | xₙ₊₁ = Txₙ | d(xₙ₊₁, xₙ) ≤ qⁿ d(x₁, x₀) (induction) |
   | consecutive steps shrink geometrically | (xₙ) Cauchy: for p > n, d(xₙ, x_p) ≤ Σ_{k=n}^{p−1} q^k d(x₁, x₀) ≤ qⁿ/(1 − q) · d(x₁, x₀) (telescope, finite geometric sum, F4.6) |
   | xₙ → x* (complete) | Tx* = x*: d(Tx*, x*) ≤ d(Tx*, Txₙ) + d(xₙ₊₁, x*) ≤ q d(x*, xₙ) + d(xₙ₊₁, x*) → 0 |
   | two fixed points x*, y* | d(x*, y*) = d(Tx*, Ty*) ≤ q d(x*, y*), so (1 − q) d(x*, y*) ≤ 0 |

   No continuity is used: the fixed-point step is one triangle inequality. The Idea ends: "*Before reading on: where is completeness used, and where is q < 1 used? (Two places for q < 1.)*"
3. **Proof** (15–20 lines), forwards. (c): let p → ∞ in d(xₙ, x_p) ≤ qⁿ/(1 − q) · d(x₀, x₁), using d(xₙ, x_p) → d(xₙ, x*) by **`lem-distance-limits`** `[Distances Pass to the Limit]` (|d(x, y) − d(x′, y′)| ≤ d(x, x′) + d(y, y′), hence d(xₙ, yₙ) → d(x, y) when xₙ → x and yₙ → y), stated and proved just before the theorem (≤ 120 words); §5.5 and §5.6 cite it. (d): apply (c) to the sequence started at xₙ₋₁, with n = 1.
4. Aftermath (2 sentences): one theorem gives existence, uniqueness, an algorithm, and an error bound. (c) tells in advance how many steps suffice; (d) tells after the fact how good the current step is.
5. **Each hypothesis is needed** (a table, STYLE §10):

   | Drop | Witness | What fails |
   |---|---|---|
   | completeness | T(x) = x/2 on (0, 1] | q = 1/2, T maps into (0, 1/2], no fixed point (0 ∉ X) |
   | the uniform constant | `exm-strict-shrink-no-fixed-point` | no fixed point |
   | q < 1 (allow q = 1) | x ↦ x + 1 on ℝ; identity on ℝ | no fixed point; every point fixed |
   | T(X) ⊆ X | T(x) = x/2 + 1 restricted to [0, 1] | contraction of ℝ with q = 1/2, fixed point 2 ∉ [0, 1]; T([0, 1]) = [1, 3/2] ⊄ [0, 1] |
6. **Quick check:** "In the proof, which steps would survive if X were (0, 1] and T(x) = x/2?" *Solution:* everything up to "(xₙ) is Cauchy"; the limit 0 is not in X.

**`## Three iterations` (~1,600).**
1. **`exm-babylonian-contraction`** `[√2 by Iteration, with an Error Bound]`. X = [1, 2], T(x) = (x + 2/x)/2. Show T is a contraction of X with q = 1/2, find the fixed point, and bound |x₃ − √2| from x₀ = 1 by (c) and by (d). *Solution:* T(x) − √2 = (x − √2)²/(2x) ≥ 0 and T(x) ≤ 3/2 since (x − 1)(x − 2) ≤ 0 on [1, 2] gives x + 2/x ≤ 3; so T(X) ⊆ [√2, 3/2] ⊆ X. Tx − Ty = (x − y)(1/2 − 1/(xy)) and 1 ≤ xy ≤ 4 gives 1/2 − 1/(xy) ∈ [−1/2, 1/4]; q = 1/2. Fixed point: x = (x + 2/x)/2 ⇔ x² = 2; x* = √2 (so the theorem reproves `@thm-sqrt2-exists`). x₁ = 3/2, x₂ = 17/12, x₃ = 577/408. (c): |x₃ − √2| ≤ (1/2)³/(1/2) · 1/2 = 1/8. (d): ≤ 1 · |577/408 − 17/12| = 1/408 (17/12 = 578/408). The true error is about 2.1 × 10⁻⁶. **Remark:** the identity T(x) − √2 = (x − √2)²/(2x) shows the error is squared at each step; the theorem gives a guarantee for every contraction, not the speed of this one.
2. **`exm-golden-ratio-contraction`** `[The Fractions of the Opening]` (pays the hook). X = [3/2, 2], T(x) = 1 + 1/x. *Solution:* for x ∈ X, 1/x ∈ [1/2, 2/3], so T(x) ∈ [3/2, 5/3] ⊆ X; |Tx − Ty| = |x − y|/(xy) ≤ (4/9)|x − y| since xy ≥ 9/4; X is complete (closed in ℝ). The fixed point solves x² − x − 1 = 0 in X: x* = (1 + √5)/2. The opening sequence starts at 1 ∉ X, but its next term 2 is in X, so from 2 on: 2, 3/2, 5/3, 8/5, … → (1 + √5)/2, with (c): |xₙ − x*| ≤ (4/9)ⁿ/(5/9) · (1/2) = (9/10)(4/9)ⁿ, counting from x₀ = 2. Back to the opening: the fractions settle down because T is a contraction; they settle on nothing in ℚ because x* is irrational (Quick check). The theorem cannot be applied in ℚ ∩ [3/2, 2], which is not complete; the sequence is Cauchy there all the same.
3. **Quick check:** "Show that (1 + √5)/2 is irrational." *Solution:* if p/q in lowest terms solved x² − x − 1 = 0, then p² = pq + q², so q | p² and p | q², forcing p, q = ±1, and ±1 is not a root (1 − 1 − 1 ≠ 0, 1 + 1 − 1 ≠ 0). (Uses only divisibility from F2.1.)
4. **`exm-linear-system-contraction`** `[A Linear System by Iteration]`. Solve x = x/2 + y/4 + 1, y = x/3 + y/3 + 1 by iterating T(x, y) = (x/2 + y/4 + 1, x/3 + y/3 + 1) on (ℝ², d_∞). *Solution:* |(Tp)₁ − (Tp′)₁| ≤ (1/2 + 1/4) d_∞(p, p′) and |(Tp)₂ − (Tp′)₂| ≤ (1/3 + 1/3) d_∞(p, p′), so q = 3/4; ℝ² is complete (`@thm-rn-complete`, `@prp-comparable-metrics-cauchy`). Remark (one sentence): this is the Jacobi iteration of numerical linear algebra; "row sums below 1" is the contraction condition for d_∞.
5. **`exm-contraction-depends-on-metric`** `[A Contraction for One Metric Only]` (weird row 6). T(x, y) = (0.9x, 0.9x) on ℝ². For d_∞: d_∞(Tp, Tp′) = 0.9|x − x′| ≤ 0.9 d_∞(p, p′). For d₁: d₁(Tp, Tp′) = 1.8|x − x′|, which is 1.8 d₁(p, p′) when p − p′ = (1, 0): not a contraction. **`## Iterates that contract` (~400).**
- **`cor-iterate-contraction`** `[When an Iterate Is a Contraction]`: "Let X be nonempty and complete and T : X → X. Proof (≤ 8 lines): x* the fixed point of Tᵐ; Tᵐ(Tx*) = T(Tᵐx*) = Tx*, so Tx* is a fixed point of Tᵐ, hence Tx* = x*; any fixed point of T is one of Tᵐ, so unique; convergence: for each r ∈ {0, …, m − 1} the subsequence T^{km+r}x₀ = (Tᵐ)ᵏ(Tʳx₀) → x* by (b); finitely many subsequences covering ℕ, each converging to x*, give convergence (max of m thresholds).

### 4.5 Section 5.5: Extending Maps from a Dense Subset (`05-extension-from-dense-subsets.md`)

**Budget:** ≤ 4,000 (expected ~3,100).

**Opening frame (~200).** The question: a map is known only on a dense subset, for example on the rationals; can it be extended to the whole space, and in only one way? The natural definition f(x) = lim f(aₙ) with aₙ → x raises two worries: the limit must exist, and it must not depend on the chosen sequence. Section 5.6 needs the answer to compare two completions of a space. A sentence on scope: the right hypothesis in general is uniform continuity, a notion of Chapter 7; here Lipschitz maps suffice.

**`## Lipschitz maps and isometries` (~600).** **`def-lipschitz`** (f : A → Y is L-Lipschitz if d(f(a), f(b)) ≤ L·d(a, b)); **`def-isometry`** (d(f(a), f(b)) = d(a, b); an isometry is injective; a 1-Lipschitz map). **`lem-lipschitz-sequences`** `[Lipschitz Maps Preserve Cauchy and Convergent Sequences]` (proof 3 lines). One sentence: Chapter 6 shows Lipschitz maps are continuous. (`lem-distance-limits` is §5.4's; cite it.) **Quick check:** "Is f(q) = 0 for q < √2, f(q) = 1 for q > √2 Lipschitz on ℚ?" *Solution:* no: rationals on either side of √2 at distance < δ have values 1 apart, so no L works (density, `@thm-rationals-dense`).

**`## The extension theorem` (~1,400).**
1. **`thm-lipschitz-extension`** `[Extension from a Dense Subset]`: "Let A be dense in X, Y complete, and f : A → Y L-Lipschitz. Then there is exactly one L-Lipschitz F : X → Y with F|_A = f. If f is an isometry, so is F."
2. **`[Scratch work]`**, table:

   | Givens | Goal |
   |---|---|
   | x ∈ X, A dense | aₙ ∈ A with aₙ → x (`@thm-closure-sequences`; choice, flagged) |
   | (aₙ) Cauchy, f Lipschitz | (f(aₙ)) Cauchy (`@lem-lipschitz-sequences`), so converges in Y (complete): set F(x) = lim f(aₙ) |
   | another bₙ → x | same limit? Interleave: a₁, b₁, a₂, b₂, … → x, so its image converges, and its two subsequences (f(aₙ)), (f(bₙ)) share that limit |
   | x ∈ A | constant sequence: F(x) = f(x) |
   | F(x) − F(x′) | d(F(x), F(x′)) = lim d(f(aₙ), f(a′ₙ)) ≤ L lim d(aₙ, a′ₙ) = L d(x, x′) (`@lem-distance-limits`) |
3. **Proof** (15 lines): existence (definition, well-definedness, extension, Lipschitz; isometry: equality passes to the limit), uniqueness (G another: G(x) = lim G(aₙ) = lim f(aₙ) = F(x), by `@lem-lipschitz-sequences` for G and uniqueness of limits).
4. **Warning** `**The target must be complete.**` "The inclusion ℚ → ℚ is an isometry from a dense subset of ℝ into ℚ, and it has no extension ℝ → ℚ that is Lipschitz: F(√2) would be the limit in ℚ of rationals tending to √2." The Quick check above shows that Lipschitz cannot be weakened to "locally constant".
5. **Remark:** the same theorem holds for uniformly continuous f (Chapter 7, with §5.5 C2 as the preview); with continuity alone it fails (the Quick check's f is continuous on ℚ in Chapter 6's sense).

**`## The sum of an absolutely summable sequence` (~700).**
- **`exm-sum-as-extension`** `[The Sum as an Extension]`. (a) Show that c₀₀ is dense in ℓ¹. (b) Show that σ(x) = Σ_k x_k (a finite sum) is 1-Lipschitz from (c₀₀, d₁) to ℝ. (c) Conclude that σ has exactly one 1-Lipschitz extension Σ : ℓ¹ → ℝ, and that Σ(x) = lim_N Σ_{k≤N} x_k. *Solution:* (a) the truncation x^{[N]} (keep x_k for k ≤ N) has d₁(x, x^{[N]}) = sup_{K>N} Σ_{N<k≤K} |x_k| = ‖x‖₁ − Σ_{k≤N} |x_k| → 0, since the increasing partial sums approach their supremum (`@cor-sup-approximation`). (b) |σ(x) − σ(y)| ≤ Σ |x_k − y_k| = d₁(x, y). (c) `thm-lipschitz-extension` with Y = ℝ; x^{[N]} → x, so Σ(x) = lim σ(x^{[N]}) = lim_N Σ_{k≤N} x_k. Aftermath: this defines the sum of an absolutely summable sequence with no theory of series; *Integration, Series, and Function Spaces*, Chapter 4, gets the same number from partial sums.

### 4.6 Section 5.6: The Completion of a Metric Space (`06-completion.md`)

**Budget:** ≤ 5,000 (expected ~4,800). Key proof: `thm-completion-exists`. Last section: R group.

**Opening frame (~200).** Three incomplete spaces have appeared, and in each the missing limits are visible from a larger space: ℚ inside ℝ, c₀₀ inside ℓ¹, (0, 1] inside [0, 1]. The polynomials of §5.2 lack limits too, and no larger space has been named for them yet. The question: can every metric space be placed, isometrically and densely, inside a complete one, and is that space unique?

**`## Completions` (~600).**
- **`def-completion`** `[Completion]`: "A **completion** of a metric space X is a pair (X̂, ι), where X̂ is a complete metric space and ι : X → X̂ is an isometry with ι(X) dense in X̂." Symbols for density not needed (AN2.1's definition is cited).
- **`exm-completions-by-inspection`** `[Completions You Can See]`: (a) [0, 1] with the inclusion is a completion of (0, 1] (closed in ℝ so complete; (0, 1] dense in it); (b) ℓ¹ is a completion of (c₀₀, d₁) (`thm-ell-one-complete`, `exm-sum-as-extension` (a)); (c) ℝ is a completion of ℚ (complete, `@thm-rationals-dense`). (2 lines each.)
- **Quick check:** "Is ℝ a completion of ℤ?" *Solution:* no: ℤ is not dense in ℝ (the ball of radius 1/2 about 1/2 misses ℤ). ℤ is complete, and its completion is ℤ itself (B3).

**`## Building the completion` (~2,300).**
1. Failure first (~150): "Add the missing limits" has nothing to add, because the limits do not exist; the only data are the Cauchy sequences themselves. So the new points are Cauchy sequences, and two of them are identified when they "should have the same limit": when d(xₙ, yₙ) → 0.
2. **`thm-completion-exists`** `[Every Metric Space Has a Completion]`: "Let (X, d) be a metric space, 𝒞 the set of Cauchy sequences in X, and (xₙ) ~ (yₙ) iff d(xₙ, yₙ) → 0. Then ~ is an equivalence relation on 𝒞; D([(xₙ)], [(yₙ)]) = lim d(xₙ, yₙ) is a well-defined metric on X̂ = 𝒞/~; ι(x) = [(x, x, x, …)] is an isometry with dense image; and (X̂, D) is complete. So (X̂, ι) is a completion of X."
3. **Idea** (Step roadmap, STYLE §4 long theorems): ① ~ is an equivalence relation (`@fd2:def-equivalence-relation`); ② the limit defining D exists and does not depend on representatives, and D is a metric; ③ ι is an isometry onto a dense subset; ④ X̂ is complete. **`[Scratch work]` for ④** (≤ 15 lines): a Cauchy sequence (ξ_k) in X̂ has points of X̂, not of X. By density ③, replace each ξ_k by a point of X within 1/k: choose x_k with D(ι(x_k), ξ_k) < 1/k. Then work in X: (x_k) is Cauchy there, d(x_k, x_l) = D(ι(x_k), ι(x_l)) ≤ 1/k + D(ξ_k, ξ_l) + 1/l; its class ξ is the candidate, and D(ξ_k, ξ) ≤ 1/k + D(ι(x_k), ξ) = 1/k + lim_l d(x_k, x_l), small for large k. Ends: "*Before reading on: in step ②, where is the completeness of ℝ used?*"
4. **Proof**, `**Step 1.**` … `**Step 4.**` with Claims:
   - Step 1: reflexive, symmetric (trivial); transitive by d(xₙ, zₙ) ≤ d(xₙ, yₙ) + d(yₙ, zₙ).
   - Step 2: |d(xₙ, yₙ) − d(xₘ, yₘ)| ≤ d(xₙ, xₘ) + d(yₙ, yₘ) (`@lem-distance-limits`' inequality), so (d(xₙ, yₙ)) is Cauchy in ℝ and converges (`@thm-cauchy-criterion`). Independence: |d(xₙ, yₙ) − d(x′ₙ, y′ₙ)| ≤ d(xₙ, x′ₙ) + d(yₙ, y′ₙ) → 0. Metric: D ≥ 0 and symmetric; D = 0 iff the sequences are equivalent, i.e. the classes are equal; triangle inequality by passing to the limit in d(xₙ, zₙ) ≤ d(xₙ, yₙ) + d(yₙ, zₙ) (`@thm-order-limit`).
   - Step 3: D(ι(x), ι(y)) = lim d(x, y) = d(x, y). Density: given ξ = [(xₙ)] and ε, take N with d(xₙ, xₘ) < ε for m, n ≥ N; D(ι(x_N), ξ) = lim_m d(x_N, xₘ) ≤ ε.
   - Step 4: as in the scratch work, with the choice flagged (`@fd2:ax-choice`) and the ε bookkeeping: given ε, K with 1/K < ε/3 and D(ξ_k, ξ_l) < ε/3 for k, l ≥ K; then d(x_k, x_l) < ε for k, l ≥ K, and D(ξ_k, ξ) ≤ 1/k + sup_{l≥K} d(x_k, x_l) ≤ 2ε for k ≥ K (≤ after the limit).
5. Aftermath (2 sentences): the points of X̂ are classes of Cauchy sequences; most of them are not of the form ι(x), and those are the "missing limits". The class of a Cauchy sequence (xₙ) is the limit of ι(xₙ) in X̂ (one line: D(ι(xₙ), [(x_k)]) = lim_k d(xₙ, x_k) → 0 as n → ∞), so every Cauchy sequence of X converges in X̂, to its own class.

**`## Uniqueness` (~800).**
1. **`thm-completion-unique`** `[The Completion Is Unique]`: "If (Y, j) and (Z, k) are completions of X, there is exactly one isometry Φ of Y onto Z with Φ ∘ j = k." Idea (3 lines): extend k ∘ j⁻¹ from the dense set j(X) (`@thm-lipschitz-extension`), and the same backwards; the two composites fix a dense set, so they are identities. Proof (10 lines): k ∘ j⁻¹ : j(X) → Z is an isometry (j is injective); Z complete; so it extends to an isometry Φ : Y → Z with Φ ∘ j = k; likewise Ψ : Z → Y with Ψ ∘ k = j. Ψ ∘ Φ : Y → Y is an isometry equal to the identity on j(X); by uniqueness in `@thm-lipschitz-extension` (the identity is another 1-Lipschitz extension), Ψ ∘ Φ = id_Y; likewise Φ ∘ Ψ = id_Z, so Φ is onto. Uniqueness of Φ: any such Φ′ agrees with Φ on j(X), so Φ′ = Φ.
2. **`cor-completion-universal`** `[Lipschitz Maps Extend to the Completion]`: "If (X̂, ι) is a completion of X, Y is complete and f : X → Y is L-Lipschitz, there is exactly one L-Lipschitz F : X̂ → Y with F ∘ ι = f." Proof: `@thm-lipschitz-extension` applied to f ∘ ι⁻¹ on ι(X).
3. Aftermath: "the completion" is now justified; any concrete complete space containing X densely *is* the completion (§5.6 B2).

**`## ℝ is the completion of ℚ, and why that builds nothing` (~600).**
1. **`exm-reals-completion-of-rationals`** `[ℝ as the Completion of ℚ]`. By `exm-completions-by-inspection` (c) and `thm-completion-unique`, the Cauchy-sequence completion of (ℚ, |·|) is isometric to ℝ by an isometry fixing ℚ. *Solution* (3 lines).
2. **Warning** `**This is not a construction of ℝ.**` "The definition of a metric space uses real-valued distances, and Step 2 of @thm-completion-exists used the completeness of ℝ. Applied to ℚ, the construction presupposes the real numbers it would produce." Then one paragraph, prose only: *Number Systems: From ℕ to ℝ*, Chapter 5 runs the same construction with rational ε and rational distances only, and also defines addition, multiplication and order on the classes, which an isometry does not give; its Chapter 6 proves that any two complete ordered fields are isomorphic. Chapter 10 of this volume relates the two meanings of complete.
3. **Back to the fractions** (~200, pays the hook a second time): the fractions 2, 3/2, 5/3, 8/5, … form a Cauchy sequence in ℚ (`exm-golden-ratio-contraction`); in ℚ it converges to nothing; in the completion of ℚ it converges to its own class (aftermath of the theorem), and under the isometry of `exm-reals-completion-of-rationals` that class is (1 + √5)/2. "The fractions were not approaching nothing; they were approaching a point that ℚ does not contain."

**`## Completions met later` (~250, remark).** Polynomials on [0, 1] with the sup distance: completion C[0, 1] (*Integration, Series, and Function Spaces*, Chapters 6 and 12). Continuous functions with the integral distance: completion L¹, in the measure-theory volume. Prose only; no labels cited.
