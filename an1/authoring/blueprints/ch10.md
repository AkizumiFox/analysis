### 4.0 `index.md`

- **Title** `# The Forms of Completeness`.
- **Story (~350 words).** Define \( g \colon \nQ \to \nQ \) by \( g(x) = 0 \) if \( x < 0 \) or \( x^2 < 2 \), and \( g(x) = 1 \) otherwise. Three facts, in prose with one display:
  - Every rational \( p \) has a rational \( \delta > 0 \) with \( g \) constant on \( (p - \delta, p + \delta) \cap \nQ \), because \( p^2 \ne 2 \). So \( g \) is continuous at every point of \( \nQ \), and the difference quotients of \( g \) at \( p \) are eventually \( 0 \), so \( g'(p) = 0 \). The definitions are AN1.5's and AN1.7's, read in \( \nQ \).
  - On \( [0, 2] \cap \nQ \) the function takes only the values \( 0 \) and \( 1 \). The IVT fails (no \( c \) with \( g(c) = 1/2 \)). The MVT fails: \( g(2) - g(0) = 1 \), but \( g'(c) \cdot 2 = 0 \) for every \( c \). "Zero derivative implies constant" fails too.
  - Each of these theorems was proved in Chapters 7 and 9 from the axiom of Chapter 1. So the axiom was doing the work. Then the three questions (Q1)–(Q3) of §1, in plain words, each with the section that answers it.
  - Close: "The chapter works in an arbitrary ordered field, so that each property can be switched on and off. It ends the volume by matching the axiom of Chapter 1 with the metric completeness of Chapter 5."
- **What you need.** Chapter 1 (the axiom, Archimedes, nested intervals, `@exm-nonarchimedean-field`); Chapter 4 (monotone convergence, monotone subsequences, Cauchy sequences); Chapter 2 (Bolzano–Weierstrass, Heine–Borel); Chapters 6, 7, 9 (continuity, IVT and EVT, derivative and MVT); Chapter 3 and Chapter 5 (metric spaces, complete metric spaces, Cantor intersection) for 10.5. From *Number Systems*, Chapter 4 (sequences in an ordered field) is used throughout, and Chapters 5–6 are cited (F3.10, F3.4). From *Analytic Preliminaries*: the weird-examples table (F4.7) and the discrete and sup metrics (F4.11).
- **Time** as in §2.
- **Roadmap.** Five bullets, one per section, each naming its main result in words.
- **Named moves** (one line each, linked to their first worked use):
  1. **Audit the proof.** Run an ℝ-proof in an arbitrary ordered field, and mark the line that used the axiom (`@exm-completeness-audit`).
  2. **Count the jumps.** A bounded monotone sequence that is not Cauchy has infinitely many jumps of size ε. Archimedes then makes it unbounded (`@lem-bounded-monotone-cauchy`).
  3. **Bisect and keep the invariant** (recalled from *Number Systems*, Chapter 5, and `@exr-nested-intervals-c1`). Used in `@thm-completeness-equiv` (v) ⇒ (i).

### 4.1 Section 10.1: Five Candidate Axioms (`01-candidate-axioms.md`)

**Budget:** ≤ 5,000 (expected ~3,600). Four Quick checks, one warning.

A warning (`.warning`): in ℝ(x), "ε = 1/n for some n" does not cover every positive ε, since \( 1/x < 1/n \) for every n (`@exm-nonarchimedean-field`). 2. **`## Auditing Chapters 1–9` (~1,100).** Failure motivation for the definitions.

     - Cauchy criterion: "a bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence" (BW).
     - Heine–Borel: "take the supremum of the good points" (LUB), or, in the bisection proof, "the nested squares share a point" (NI).
     - IVT: "let c be the supremum of {x : f(x) < y}" (LUB).

Name the move "Audit the proof".

3. **`## Five properties` (~700).**
   - Lead (one sentence), then **`def-completeness-properties`** `[Forms of Completeness]`. Let \( F \) be an ordered field. Then \( F \) has:
     - **(LUB)**, the *least upper bound property*, if it is complete in the sense of `@fd3:def-complete-ordered-field`: every non-empty subset bounded above has a supremum in F.
     - **(MC)**, the *monotone convergence property*, if every bounded monotone sequence in F converges in F.
     - **(BW)**, the *Bolzano–Weierstrass property*, if every bounded sequence in F has a subsequence that converges in F.
     - **(CC)**, *Cauchy completeness*, if every Cauchy sequence in F converges in F (`@fd3:def-complete-ordered-field`, last sentence).
     - **(NI)**, the *nested interval property*, if for every sequence of closed bounded intervals \( [a_n, b_n] \) of F (\( a_n \le b_n \)) with \( [a_{n+1}, b_{n+1}] \subseteq [a_n, b_n] \) for all n, some element of F lies in every \( [a_n, b_n] \).
     - Recall (A), the Archimedean property, from `@fd3:def-archimedean-ordered-field`.
   - (CC) and (NI) are also given in symbols (STYLE §10). For (CC): \( \forall (x_n) \bigl[ (\forall \varepsilon \in F_{>0}\ \exists N\ \forall m, n \ge N\ \abs{x_m - x_n} < \varepsilon) \Rightarrow \exists L \in F\ \forall \varepsilon \in F_{>0}\ \exists N\ \forall n \ge N\ \abs{x_n - L} < \varepsilon \bigr] \).
   - One remark: in ℝ all five hold, together with (A) (AN1.1, AN1.2, AN1.4). The definition is about other fields.
   - **Quick check** (weird examples, the motivation mode, right after the definition): "(a) Does an ordered field with only finitely many elements exist? (b) In an ordered field, must a sequence that is eventually constant be Cauchy? (c) Can a field have (NI) for intervals with \( a_n = b_n \) for all n and still fail (NI)?" *Solution:* (a) No. \( 0 < 1 < 1 + 1 < \cdots \) are distinct (AN1.1 `@thm-ordered-field-rules`), so F is infinite. (b) Yes: \( \abs{x_m - x_n} = 0 < \varepsilon \) from the constant index on. (c) Yes. Such families are constant from the start (\( [a_1, a_1] \supseteq [a_2, a_2] \) forces \( a_2 = a_1 \)), so they always have a common point. That says nothing about general families; ℚ is an example (`exm-rationals-fail-all-five`).
4. **`## Two foils` (~1,100).**
   - **`exm-rationals-fail-all-five`** `[ℚ Fails All Five]`. Question: "Show that ℚ has (A) and none of the five properties." Solution, with one witness doing all the work: the decimal truncations \( r_n \) of √2 (AN1.1 `@thm-sqrt2-exists`, and the bisection of `07-nested-intervals.md`). They form a bounded increasing sequence of rationals. In ℝ it converges to √2 (AN1.2, applied in ℝ ⊇ ℚ, which is legitimate here because the question is about ℚ as a subset of ℝ). So:
     - (MC) fails: a rational limit q would also be a limit in ℝ, so q = √2 by uniqueness of limits, which is impossible.
     - (BW) fails the same way for every subsequence.
     - (CC) fails: \( (r_n) \) is Cauchy in ℚ (its differences are below \( 10^{-n} \)).
     - (NI) fails for \( [r_n, r_n + 10^{-n}] \cap \nQ \).
     - (LUB) fails by `@thm-no-rational-sup-sqrt2`.
     - (A) holds by `@exr-archimedean-property-c3`.

     One sentence: "so (A) is far from enough."

3. **`## Laurent series` (~1,000).**
   - **`def-laurent-field`** `[The Field of Laurent Series]`. \( \nR((t)) \) is the set of functions \( a \colon \nZ \to \nR \), \( k \mapsto a_k \), for which some m ∈ ℤ has \( a_k = 0 \) for all k < m. It is written \( \sum_k a_k t^k \). Sum: coefficientwise. Product: \( (ab)_k = \sum_{i + j = k} a_i b_j \), a finite sum, because \( i \ge m_a \) and \( j \ge m_b \) force \( m_a \le i \le k - m_b \). For a ≠ 0, the *order* \( v(a) \) is the least k with \( a_k \ne 0 \), and \( a_{v(a)} \) is the *leading coefficient*. Positive: a ≠ 0 with leading coefficient > 0. The reals sit inside as constants, and t is the series with \( t_1 = 1 \) and all other coefficients 0.
   - **`prp-laurent-ordered-field`** `[ℝ((t)) Is an Ordered Field]`. Statement: with these operations and positives, ℝ((t)) is an ordered field containing ℝ, in which 0 < t < r for every real r > 0. Idea (~6 lines): \( v(ab) = v(a) + v(b) \), and leading coefficients multiply. So products of positives are positive, and there are no zero divisors. The leading coefficient of a sum of positives is either one of the two leading coefficients or their sum. Inverses by recursion: if \( v(a) = m \), set \( b_{-m} = 1/a_m \) and \( b_{-m+n} = -(1/a_m) \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{m+i} b_{-m+n-i} \) for n ≥ 1. Then ab = 1, coefficient by coefficient. Proof: the order axioms written out (trichotomy, closure of positives under + and ·) and the inverse. The ring axioms (associativity and distributivity of the product, as identities between finite double sums) go in a fold titled "The ring axioms, routine", which is a ledger entry.
   - **`lem-laurent-small`** `[Small Means High Order]`. For a ∈ ℝ((t)) and N ∈ ℤ: (a) if a = 0 or v(a) > N, then \( \abs{a} < t^N \); (b) if \( \abs{a} < t^N \), then a = 0 or v(a) ≥ N; (c) every positive ε ∈ ℝ((t)) satisfies \( \varepsilon > t^{N} \) for some N ∈ ℤ. Proof: compare leading terms. In (a), \( t^N - \abs a \) has leading term \( t^N \). In (b), if v(a) < N, then \( \abs a - t^N \) has leading coefficient \( \abs{a_{v(a)}} > 0 \). In (c), N = v(ε) + 1.
   - **`prp-laurent-non-archimedean`** is *not* a separate label. It is a sentence with a one-line proof after the lemma: \( t^{-1} - n \) has leading term \( t^{-1} \), so \( t^{-1} > n \) for every n ∈ ℕ, and ℝ((t)) fails (A).
   - **Quick check:** "Order these by size: \( t^{-1} \), \( 1000 \), \( t^{-1} - 10^6 \), \( 2t^{-1} \), \( t^{-2} \)." *Solution:* \( 1000 < t^{-1} - 10^6 < t^{-1} < 2 t^{-1} < t^{-2} \), by leading terms of the differences.
4. **`## Cauchy completeness` (~1,300). Key proof.**

### 4.4 Section 10.4: Calculus in Reverse (`04-calculus-in-reverse.md`)

2. **`## A gap splits the field` (~1,000).**
   - **`lem-gap-split`** `[A Gap Splits the Field into Two Open Pieces]`. Let A ⊆ F be non-empty, bounded above, and without a supremum. Let U be the set of upper bounds of A, and L = F ∖ U. Then (a) L and U are non-empty; (b) ℓ < u for all ℓ ∈ L, u ∈ U; (c) L has no greatest element and U has no least element; (d) every p ∈ F has a δ > 0 with \( (p - \delta, p + \delta) \subseteq L \) or \( \subseteq U \).
   - **`[Scratch work]`** (≤ 20 lines). The picture in ℚ: A = {x : x < 0 or x² < 2}, L = A, U = the rest. Where would a boundary point be? It would be the least upper bound, and there is none. So every point has room around it on its own side. Finding δ in (d): for p ∈ U, U has no least element, so some u ∈ U lies below p, and δ = p − u works. For p ∈ L, take ℓ ∈ L above p (no greatest element), and δ = ℓ − p. Name the move "Cut along the gap".
   - Proof as sketched: (a) a − 1 ∈ L for a ∈ A, and a bound lies in U. (b) ℓ ∉ U gives a ∈ A with a > ℓ, and u ≥ a. (c) min U would be sup A. If ℓ = max L, some a ∈ A exceeds ℓ, so a ∈ U ∩ A, and then a = max A = sup A. (d) as in the scratch work. For p ∈ L, use (b) to show every x < ℓ lies in L.

3. **`## Four theorems that imply the axiom` (~2,200). Key proof.**
   - **`thm-calculus-implies-lub`** `[Calculus in Reverse]`. Let F be an ordered field. Each of the following properties implies (LUB):
     - **(IVT)** for all a < b in F and every continuous f: [a, b] → F, every element between f(a) and f(b) is a value of f;
     - **(EVT)** for all a < b, every continuous f: [a, b] → F attains a greatest value;
     - **(HB)** for all a < b, every cover of [a, b] by open intervals has a finite subcover;
     - **(ZD)** every f: F → F that is differentiable at every point with f′ = 0 everywhere is constant.

     Conversely, all four hold when F has (LUB).
   - Idea: one function per property, all built from the split of `@lem-gap-split`. *Converse:* F with (LUB) is a complete ordered field, so there is an order-preserving field isomorphism onto ℝ (`@fd3:thm-cof-uniqueness`). Such a map carries continuity, derivatives and covers, by the definitions, and the four properties hold in ℝ (AN1.6's IVT and EVT, AN1.4's Heine–Borel, AN1.7's zero-derivative corollary).
   - **Try it first** before the proof: "Find a continuous function on ℚ ∩ [0, 2] that skips the value 1/2."
   - Proof: suppose (LUB) fails, take A, L, U from the lemma, a ∈ L, b ∈ U (so a < b). Let χ = 0 on L and 1 on U. By (d), χ is constant near every point, so it is continuous and differentiable with χ′ = 0 everywhere.
     - (IVT): χ on [a, b] skips 1/2. This is the index function again.
     - (ZD): χ is not constant.
     - (EVT): Idea only (fading). h(x) = x on L and h(x) = a on U is continuous (locally the identity or constant). A greatest value on [a, b] would be attained at some x₀. If x₀ ∈ U, then h(x₀) = a < ℓ = h(ℓ) for any ℓ ∈ L above a (L has no greatest element). If x₀ ∈ L, a larger ℓ ∈ L ∩ [a, b] gives a larger value. (ℓ < b holds automatically, by (b).)
     - (HB): cover each p ∈ [a, b] by its interval from (d). Take a finite subcover; a ∈ L ∩ [a, b] lies in one of its members contained in L, so there is at least one such member. Let \( (c_1, d_1), \dots, (c_k, d_k) \) be its members contained in L that meet [a, b]. Claim: each \( d_i \in L \). (If some u ∈ U had u < \( d_i \), the points of \( (\max(c_i, u), d_i) \) would lie in L above u, contradicting (b). So \( d_i \le u \) for all u ∈ U, and \( d_i \in U \) would make it the least element of U.) Let d = max \( d_i \) (finiteness). Then d ∈ L ∩ [a, b], so d lies in some member of the subcover, which must be one of the \( (c_j, d_j) \) contained in L. So d < \( d_j \le d \). Contradiction. *Self-explanation prompt:* "Where is finiteness of the subcover used?"
   - **Aftermath.** One remark each:
     - Archimedes is not on the list: ℚ has (A) and fails (LUB).
     - (MVT) ⇒ (ZD) in one line, so the MVT also gives the axiom back.
     - One sentence: "which theorems imply which axioms is studied systematically in *reverse mathematics*, which this book does not pursue."
   - **Quick check:** "The index function shows that the IVT fails in ℚ. Why does this not contradict AN1.6?" *Solution:* AN1.6 proves the IVT for ℝ, from (LUB). ℚ lacks (LUB).
   - **Quick check:** "Which part of the lemma does the (EVT) function use that the (IVT) function does not?" *Solution:* (c), that L has no greatest element.

### 4.5 Section 10.5: Two Meanings of "Complete" (`05-two-meanings-of-complete.md`)

**Budget:** ≤ 5,000 (expected ~4,400). Four Quick checks; summary table; List of Results; R group (last section).

1. **Opening (~200).** Pay (Q3) and AN1.1's promise. In Chapter 1, "complete" meant (LUB). In Chapter 5 it meant "every Cauchy sequence converges", for metric spaces (AN2.2). The first needs an order and speaks about sets. The second needs a metric and speaks about sequences. This section says exactly how they meet.
2. **`## Archimedean fields live inside ℝ` (~1,100).**
   - **`thm-archimedean-inside-r`** `[Every Archimedean Ordered Field Sits Inside ℝ]`. If F is Archimedean, there is exactly one order-preserving field map φ: F → ℝ. It is injective and fixes ℚ. Proof: `@fd3:thm-cof-embedding-into-complete` with L = ℝ, which is complete by `@ax-least-upper-bound`. A field map is injective, because its kernel is an ideal not containing 1. (fd3's statement includes the fixing of ℚ, via `@fd3:prp-cof-map-fixes-rationals`.)
   - **`prp-gaps-are-missing-reals`** `[The Gaps of F Are the Reals It Misses]`. Let F be Archimedean and A ⊆ F non-empty and bounded above. Then A has a supremum in F if and only if \( \sup_{\nR} \varphi(A) \in \varphi(F) \). In that case \( \varphi(\sup_F A) = \sup_{\nR} \varphi(A) \). Proof (~10 lines): (⇐) the preimage is the supremum, since φ preserves and reflects order. (⇒) φ(σ) is an upper bound. If a real r < φ(σ) were one, pick q ∈ ℚ with r < q < φ(σ) (`@thm-rationals-dense`). Then q < σ in F, so some a ∈ A has a > q, and φ(a) > q > r. Contradiction.
   - **Quick check:** "In ℚ(√2) (10.1 B1), which real number does the gap of {y : y < √3} correspond to?" *Solution:* √3, which is not in φ(F) = ℚ(√2).
   - **`cor-cauchy-complete-archimedean-is-r`** `[A Cauchy-Complete Archimedean Field Is a Copy of ℝ]`. If F has (CC) and (A), then φ is onto, so φ is an order-preserving field isomorphism F → ℝ. Proof: given r ∈ ℝ, choose rationals \( q_n \in (r - 1/(n+1), r] \). They form a Cauchy sequence in ℝ, and hence in F: by (A) every positive ε ∈ F exceeds some 1/N, and the inequality \( \abs{q_m - q_n} < 1/N \) between rationals holds in F as in ℝ. So \( q_n \to y \) in F, and \( \abs{\varphi(y) - q_n} = \varphi(\abs{y - q_n}) \) shows φ(y) = r. Aftermath: with `@thm-completeness-equiv` this is a third proof of (iv) ⇒ (i), by transport from ℝ, and with `@fd3:thm-cof-uniqueness` it says again that "the" real numbers are well defined.
3. **`## The metric meaning` (~900).** In a metric space (AN2.1) there is no order, so (LUB) and (MC) have no meaning. (CC) does: it is AN2.2's completeness. For F ⊆ ℝ Archimedean with d(x, y) = |x − y|, (CC) for the order and metric completeness coincide. A metric takes values in ℝ, so its ε's are real, and ε = 1/n suffices by Archimedes in ℝ. So in the metric world (A) is built in, and (CC) alone is the whole of (LUB) for subfields of ℝ. **Quick check:** "Is d(f, g) = |f − g| a metric on ℝ((t))?" *Solution:* no. Its values lie in ℝ((t)), not in ℝ. But ℝ((t)) does carry an honest metric (B2). One warning (`.warning`): metric completeness depends on the metric, not only on the set. ℝ has (LUB), yet with another metric it is not complete (B1).
4. **`## Nested closed sets` (~1,200).**
   - Recall AN2.2's Cantor intersection theorem (complete ⇒ nested non-empty closed sets with diameters → 0 meet in exactly one point) with its label. What changes from AN1.1's `@thm-nested-intervals`: intervals become closed sets, and length becomes diameter. What survives: the shrinking hypothesis, which is (A) in disguise, since diameters are real.
   - **`thm-complete-iff-nested-closed`** `[Completeness as a Nested-Set Property]`. A metric space X is complete if and only if every sequence \( C_1 \supseteq C_2 \supseteq \cdots \) of non-empty closed subsets with diam \( C_n \to 0 \) has non-empty intersection.
   - (⇒) is AN2.2's theorem (cited). (⇐) gets a **`[Scratch work]`** (≤ 15 lines). Given a Cauchy \( (x_n) \), we need a point. Nested closed sets will produce one, so build them from the sequence: the tails \( T_n = \{ x_k : k \ge n \} \) are nested, and their diameters tend to 0 exactly because the sequence is Cauchy. Close them up: \( C_n = \overline{T_n} \), noting diam \( \overline{T} \) = diam T (AN2.1/AN2.2; one line if not there). A point x in every \( C_n \) is within diam \( C_n \) of \( x_n \). Name the move "Shrink closed sets around a Cauchy sequence".
   - Proof forwards (~8 lines). *Self-explanation prompt:* "Where is 'diam \( C_n \to 0 \)' used, and where is 'closed'?"
   - **`prp-heine-borel-property-complete`** `[Bounded Sequences with Convergent Subsequences Force Completeness]`. If every bounded sequence in X has a convergent subsequence, X is complete. Proof: a Cauchy sequence is bounded and has a convergent subsequence, so it converges (AN1.2's metric lemma, or `@lem-cauchy-subsequence-ordered-field`'s argument verbatim with real ε).
   - **`exm-discrete-naturals`** `[Complete, Bounded, and Not Compact]`. ℕ with the discrete metric (F4.11): Cauchy sequences are eventually constant (ε = 1/2), so it is complete; diam = 1; the sequence n has no convergent subsequence. So the converse of the proposition fails: in metric spaces the (BW)-type property is strictly stronger than (CC), unlike in ordered fields (`@thm-completeness-equiv`). One sentence: B(S) with the sup metric (F4.11) is another such space, and *Integration, Series, and Function Spaces*, Chapter 6 proves it complete.
