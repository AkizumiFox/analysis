**Part I, Analysis on the Real Line.** Starting from the least upper bound axiom alone, Part I builds ε-N and ε-δ fluency on ℝ through sequences, series, the topology of the line, continuity, the derivative, the Riemann–Darboux integral, uniform convergence and power series, because each classical theorem of calculus (IVT, EVT, MVT, FTC, term-by-term differentiation) fails or is unprovable without completeness; it closes by proving that the forms of completeness are equivalent.

## Planned sections

1. Continuous images of compact sets are compact; the extreme value theorem
2. Uniform continuity: the quantifier swap; x² on ℝ, 1/x on (0,1), sin(1/x); Lipschitz ⇒ uniformly continuous
3. Heine–Cantor: continuous on a compact set ⇒ uniformly continuous, proved twice (BW and contradiction; Lebesgue number)
4. Uniformly continuous functions preserve Cauchy sequences and extend continuously from a dense set
5. The intermediate value theorem: via sup (the √2 template), via connectedness, via bisection; its failure over ℚ
6. Monotone and inverse functions: continuous injective on an interval ⇒ strictly monotone; the inverse is continuous

## Must prove

- continuous image of a compact set is compact
- extreme value theorem
- Heine–Cantor (compact ⇒ uniformly continuous), by BW and by the Lebesgue number
- uniformly continuous functions map Cauchy sequences to Cauchy sequences
- intermediate value theorem
- continuous image of an interval is an interval
- a continuous injection on an interval is strictly monotone with continuous inverse

## Key examples and counterexamples

- x² continuous, not uniformly continuous on ℝ
- 1/x and sin(1/x) on (0,1)
- √x uniformly continuous but not Lipschitz on [0,1]
- f(x) = x² − 2 on ℚ ∩ [0,2]: no zero
- a bounded continuous function on (0,1) with no maximum
