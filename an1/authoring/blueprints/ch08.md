## 2. Devices as applied

- **Chapter hook** (index story, restated at the start of 8.1 and 8.6):
  - Counting cannot tell a segment from a square, since [0, 1] and [0, 1]² have the same cardinality.
  - So is the square "bigger" in any sense that analysis sees? Can a continuous map from [0, 1] cover the square? Can a continuous bijection do so? Can a homeomorphism?
  - The questions are posed in the index and not answered there.
  - **Paid in 8.6.**
    - `thm-interval-square-not-homeomorphic`: no homeomorphism, by connectedness.
    - `thm-no-continuous-bijection-interval-square`: no continuous bijection, since compactness upgrades a bijection to a homeomorphism.

- **Story (2 paragraphs).**
  - (1) The hook. [0, 1] and [0, 1]² have the same number of points (F2.3, or the 8.6 fold; §2). Counting cannot see dimension. The chapter asks whether continuity can: is there a continuous map from the segment onto the square, a continuous bijection, a homeomorphism? The answers come at the end of the chapter, and they are different.
  - (2) The tools are the two properties of [a, b] that made the theorems of Chapter 7 work, taken into metric spaces. *Compactness*: every open cover has a finite subcover. *Connectedness*: the space does not fall into two open pieces. In ℝⁿ, compactness is the same as closed and bounded, and that is why Chapter 2 could prove it with accumulation points. In other spaces the two come apart. The chapter finds the right replacement, completeness plus total boundedness, and shows where finite dimensions are special.

### 4.1 Section 8.1: Compact Metric Spaces (`01-compact-metric-spaces.md`)

**Budget:** ≤ 5,000 (~4,100). No folds.

**Opening frame (spiral, ~200).**
- Recall Chapter 2: for K ⊆ ℝⁿ, closed and bounded ⇔ every open cover has a finite subcover ⇔ every infinite subset of K has an accumulation point in K (cite AN1.4's Heine–Borel and Bolzano–Weierstrass labels).
- Recall the hook in one sentence.
- "Three descriptions of one property in ℝⁿ. In a metric space they can disagree, and the theorems of Chapter 7 need the one that survives."

**`## Closed and bounded is not enough` (failure, ~450).** **`exm-discrete-closed-bounded`** `[A closed, bounded set that behaves badly]`. Take X = ℕ with the discrete metric (F4.11).
- X is bounded, with diam X = 1, and closed in itself.
- The sequence (n) has no convergent subsequence: distinct terms are at distance 1, so no subsequence is Cauchy (cite AN1.2's convergent ⇒ Cauchy).
- f(n) = n is continuous, since every function on a discrete space is continuous (AN1.5's preimage criterion: every set is open). It is unbounded, so the EVT fails.
- The cover by the balls B(n, 1/2) = {n} has no finite subcover.
- Conclusion: "closed and bounded" is a fact about ℝⁿ, not a definition. Of the three descriptions, the cover description is taken as the definition (Apostol's choice). 8.2 shows that the accumulation-point and sequence descriptions are equivalent to it in every metric space.

**`## Open covers and compactness` (~1,000).** Explain template, all eleven steps (STYLE §3, major notion).
- `def-open-cover`: a family 𝒰 of open subsets of X with K ⊆ ⋃𝒰; a subcover; a finite subcover.
- **Slogan:** *every open cover can be cut down to finitely many of its sets.*
- **`def-compact`** `[Compact set]`: K ⊆ X is **compact** if **every** open cover of K has a **finite** subcover; X is a compact metric space if X is compact as a subset of itself. Then the symbols: ∀𝒰 (open, K ⊆ ⋃𝒰) ∃U₁, …, U_m ∈ 𝒰 with K ⊆ U₁ ∪ ⋯ ∪ U_m.
- Paraphrase clause by clause.
- **`exm-compact-first-examples`**, with solution:
  - (a) A finite set: pick one member per point.
  - (b) K = {0} ∪ {2^{-k} : k ≥ 0} ⊆ ℝ. Given 𝒰, take U₀ ∋ 0. It contains (−r, r), hence every 2^{-k} with 2^{-k} < r, which is all but finitely many. Add one member for each of the rest.
  - (c) The degenerate case ∅: the empty subfamily covers it.
- **Non-example by minimal change.** Remove 0 from (b): {2^{-k}} is covered by Uₖ = (2^{-k} − 2^{-k-2}, 2^{-k} + 2^{-k-2}), each containing exactly one point. No finite subcover exists. The clause that fails is "every cover"; finite covers of this set certainly exist.
- **Why this definition:** it is the description that does not mention order or coordinates, and the one that turns a local statement (each point has a good ball) into a global one (finitely many balls suffice), which is what EVT and uniform continuity need.
- **`::: {.warning}`**: "Has a finite open cover" is not compactness. Every set has one, namely {X}. The definition quantifies over **every** cover.
- **Quick check:** "Is ℚ ∩ [0, 1], as a metric space with the usual distance, compact? Find a cover that shows it." *Solution:* no. Uₙ = {q : |q − 1/√2| > 1/n} (n ≥ 1) are open in ℚ ∩ [0, 1] and cover it since 1/√2 ∉ ℚ. A finite subfamily lies in one U_N, which misses rationals within 1/N of 1/√2. Density of ℚ (AN1.1) supplies such rationals.
- **`prp-compact-intrinsic`** `[Compactness does not depend on the surrounding space]`. For K ⊆ Y ⊆ X, K is compact as a subset of the metric space Y if and only if it is compact as a subset of X. *Idea:* open sets of Y are V ∩ Y with V open in X (cite AN2.1's subspace result). Proof of about 8 lines.
- **Contrast** (one sentence + Quick check): closedness does depend on the surrounding space.
  - **Quick check:** "(0, 1] is closed in (0, 2). Is it closed in ℝ? Is it compact?" *Solution:* not closed in ℝ (0 is a limit). Not compact, by `thm-compact-closed-bounded` below and `prp-compact-intrinsic`. Placed after the theorem.

**`## What compactness gives at once` (~1,500).**
- **`thm-compact-closed-bounded`** `[Compact Sets Are Closed and Bounded]` (Apostol 3.38 (i)).
- **`[Scratch work]`, manufacture a cover (first appearance).**
  - Givens/Goal: K compact, and the goal is bounded. *Bounded* means "inside one ball". The data offer the cover {B(p, n) : n ≥ 1} for any p, and keeping finitely many gives one ball.
  - For *closed*: take x ∉ K. The goal is a ball around x missing K. Manufacture the cover Uₙ = {y : d(y, x) > 1/n}. These sets are open (cite AN2.1: the complement of a closed ball), and they cover K because x ∉ K. Keep finitely many: they are nested, so one U_N covers K, and B(x, 1/N) misses K.
  - Table, three rows, ending with "the finiteness is what turns the radii 1/n into one radius 1/N".
- **Proof** (two parts, about 12 lines).
- **Aftermath:** the converse fails (`exm-discrete-closed-bounded`). This is said in a `::: {.remark}`.
- **`thm-closed-subset-compact`** `[Closed Subsets of Compact Sets Are Compact]` (Apostol 3.39). *Idea:* add the open set X ∖ F to any cover of F. About 6 lines.
- **Quick check:** "Is the intersection of a compact set with a closed set compact?" *Solution:* yes. K ∩ F is closed in X (the intersection of two closed sets; K is closed by `thm-compact-closed-bounded`), so it is a closed subset of K. Apply `thm-closed-subset-compact`.
- **`thm-compact-fip`** `[Finite Intersection Property]`. K is compact if and only if every family of closed subsets of K, each finite subfamily of which has non-empty intersection, has non-empty intersection.
  - *Idea:* complements turn covers into intersections.
  - Proof with (⇒) in full and (⇐) as "the same argument read backwards", in 4 lines. STYLE allows skipping a symmetric half; here the second half is short and is written.
- **`cor-nested-compact`** `[Nested Compact Sets]`. K₁ ⊇ K₂ ⊇ ⋯, non-empty and compact ⇒ ⋂Kₙ ≠ ∅.
  - **Spiral remark:** in ℝ this was nested intervals (AN1.1) and AN1.4's nested compact sets. Chapter 5's Cantor intersection theorem asked for completeness and diameters tending to 0. Compactness needs neither.
  - Witness: in ℓ^∞, Fₙ = {e_k : k ≥ n} are closed, bounded, nested and non-empty, with empty intersection. The proof that Fₙ is closed takes one line: there are no accumulation points, since distinct points are at distance 1. Cite F4.11 for ‖e_k − e_j‖_∞ = 1.
- **Quick check:** "In `cor-nested-compact`, which hypothesis does the witness (Fₙ) violate?" *Solution:* compactness. Fₙ is not compact: the balls B(e_k, 1/2) cover it with no finite subcover.

### 4.2 Section 8.2: Sequential Compactness and Total Boundedness (`02-sequential-compactness.md`)

**Budget:** ≤ 5,000 (~4,800). The longest proof in the chapter. Step roadmap in its Idea (STYLE §4).

**Opening frame (spiral, ~200).** Chapter 4 proved that every bounded sequence in ℝⁿ has a convergent subsequence, and Chapter 2 proved the accumulation-point version. In a metric space boundedness is not enough (`exm-discrete-closed-bounded`). What replaces it?

**`## Sequential compactness` (~300).** **`def-sequentially-compact`**: every sequence in K has a subsequence converging **to a point of K**. Two examples (a finite set; [0, 1]ⁿ by AN1.2) and a non-example ((0, 1], with 1/n). One sentence: the main theorem below shows this is the same as compactness, in every metric space.

**`## Total boundedness` (failure-motivated, ~900).**
- **Failure:** in ℓ^∞ the set E = {eₙ} is bounded, yet no subsequence of (eₙ) converges. Ask what a bounded subset of ℝⁿ has that E lacks. Answer: for every ε > 0 it is covered by finitely many ε-balls (a grid of cubes). E is covered by one ball of radius 2, but no finite number of balls of radius 1/2 covers it, since each such ball contains at most one eₙ.
- **Slogan:** *bounded at every scale: finitely many small balls suffice, however small.*
- **`def-totally-bounded`**: for **every** ε > 0 there are **finitely many** points x₁, …, x_m ∈ X with X ⊆ B(x₁, ε) ∪ ⋯ ∪ B(x_m, ε). Symbols. The set {x₁, …, x_m} is an **ε-net**.
- **`prp-bounded-rn-totally-bounded`**: a bounded subset of (ℝⁿ, d₂) is totally bounded.
  - *Idea:* a grid of side h has every point of the cube within h√n/2 of a grid point. Choose h < 2ε/√n.
  - The proof also handles the subset issue: centres must lie in the set, so use an ε/2-net of the cube and replace each centre whose ball meets the set by a point of the set in that ball (radius doubles).
- **`exm-totally-bounded-gallery`** (four items, with solution):
  - (a) Totally bounded ⇒ bounded (one ball around x₁ of radius ε + max d(x₁, xᵢ)).
  - (b) E = {eₙ} ⊆ ℓ^∞ is bounded, not totally bounded.
  - (c) ℕ with the discrete metric is not totally bounded (ε = 1/2).
  - (d) **Weird:** ℕ_{≥1} with d(m, n) = |1/m − 1/n|. This is a metric, because n ↦ 1/n is injective into ℝ. Its open sets are exactly those of the discrete metric (each point n is isolated: its nearest neighbour is at distance 1/n − 1/(n+1)). Yet it is totally bounded, as an isometric copy of a bounded subset of ℝ. So total boundedness depends on the metric, not only on the open sets (picked up in 8.6's invariants table).
- **Quick check** (quantifier swap, STYLE §10): "Swap the quantifiers: 'there are finitely many points such that for every ε > 0 the ε-balls around them cover X'. Which spaces satisfy this?" *Solution:* exactly the finite spaces. Letting ε → 0, each point must equal some xᵢ.
- **`::: {.warning}`**: bounded does not imply totally bounded outside ℝⁿ. The witness is E.
- **`lem-separated-sequence`** `[A Separated Sequence]`. If X is not totally bounded, there are ε > 0 and a sequence (xₙ) with d(xₘ, xₙ) ≥ ε for all m ≠ n.
  - Proof: induction, picking x_{n+1} outside B(x₁, ε) ∪ ⋯ ∪ B(xₙ, ε), which is possible because these balls do not cover X.
  - One clause flags the successive choices (binding rule 6).
  - Aftermath: such a sequence has no Cauchy subsequence, so no convergent subsequence. This is Named move 3.

**`## The Lebesgue number` (~700).** Lead-in: a cover of a compact space is not just finite, it is uniformly fine.
- **`lem-lebesgue-number`** `[Lebesgue Number Lemma]`. Let X be sequentially compact and 𝒰 an open cover of X. There is λ > 0 such that every subset of X of diameter less than λ lies in some member of 𝒰.
- **`[Scratch work]`, suppose not, radius 1/n (first appearance in this chapter).**
  - Write the negation as its own line: for every λ > 0 some set of diameter < λ lies in no member.
  - Specialize to λ = 1/n to get sets Aₙ, and pick xₙ ∈ Aₙ.
  - Sequential compactness hands a subsequence → p. The point p lies in some U, with B(p, r) ⊆ U.
  - The goal is a contradiction: for large k the whole of A_{n_k} lies in B(p, r). This needs d(x_{n_k}, p) < r/2 and diam A_{n_k} < r/2.
  - Table, three rows.
- **Proof** (about 10 lines; Aₙ ⊆ B(x_{n_k}, 1/n_k)).
- **Aftermath:** "One radius for the whole cover" (Named move 2), used for uniform continuity in Section 8.3.
- **Quick check:** "Find a Lebesgue number for the cover {(−1, 1/2), (1/3, 2)} of [0, 1]." *Solution:* any λ ≤ 1/6. A set of diameter < 1/6 that is not inside (−1, 1/2) contains a point ≥ 1/2, so all its points are > 1/2 − 1/6 = 1/3. λ = 1/6 is the largest, since [1/3, 1/2] has diameter 1/6 and lies in neither set. Placed right after the aftermath.

**`## Four descriptions of compactness` (~1,800).**
- **`thm-metric-compact-equiv`** `[Characterizations of Compact Metric Spaces]` (registry). For a metric space X, the following are equivalent:
  - (a) X is compact.
  - (b) Every infinite subset of X has an accumulation point in X.
  - (c) X is sequentially compact.
  - (d) X is complete and totally bounded.

  One sentence after the statement: the same holds for K ⊆ X with the subspace metric, by `prp-compact-intrinsic`.
- **Idea, with Step roadmap:**
  - ① (a) ⇒ (b): manufacture a cover by small balls, each holding at most one point of the set.
  - ② (b) ⇒ (c): an infinite range has an accumulation point, and a finite range has a constant subsequence.
  - ③ (c) ⇒ (d): a Cauchy sequence with a convergent subsequence converges (Chapter 5), and a separated sequence (`lem-separated-sequence`) would contradict (c).
  - ④ (d) ⇒ (c): the diagonal.
  - ⑤ (c) ⇒ (a): the Lebesgue number plus total boundedness from ③.
  - Self-explanation prompt (§2).
- **`[Scratch work]` for ④, the diagonal (first appearance).**
  - Cover X by finitely many balls of radius 1. One of them contains xₙ for infinitely many n, which gives an infinite index set N₁.
  - Inside it, use balls of radius 1/2 to get N₂ ⊆ N₁ infinite, and so on.
  - Take n_k ∈ N_k increasing. Then for j, k ≥ m both x_{n_j} and x_{n_k} lie in one ball of radius 1/m, so they are within 2/m of each other: the subsequence is Cauchy, and completeness finishes.
  - "The diagonal choice n_k ∈ N_k is what makes one subsequence work for every scale at once."
- **Proof**, in Steps 1–5, each a `**Step k.**` paragraph. Step 2 uses "every ball around an accumulation point of S contains infinitely many points of S" (cite AN2.1's lemma; §9 item 4). Step 5: if (c), then X is totally bounded by Step 3. Take λ from `lem-lebesgue-number` and a λ/3-net. Each ball B(xᵢ, λ/3) has diameter ≤ 2λ/3 < λ, so it lies in some Uᵢ, and the Uᵢ cover X.
- **Aftermath:**
  - In ℝⁿ, closed and bounded ⇒ complete (Chapter 5: closed subsets of complete spaces) and totally bounded (`prp-bounded-rn-totally-bounded`), so AN1.4's Heine–Borel is recovered as a special case.
  - In general it is (d), not "closed and bounded", that carries over.
- **`::: {.remark}`**: Apostol proves (a) ⇒ (b) and refers elsewhere for the converse. Do not cite Apostol. Instead: "the four descriptions are equivalent for metric spaces; for the topological spaces of *Integration, Series, and Function Spaces*, Chapter 13 (AN2.7), (a) and (c) part company." Prose pointer; §9 item 9.
- **Quick check:** "Which of (a)–(d) does (0, 1] fail, and which half of (d)?" *Solution:* all four. It is totally bounded but not complete ((1/n) is Cauchy with no limit in (0, 1]).

**`## Products` (~400).**
- **`thm-product-compact`** `[Products of Compact Spaces]`. If X and Y are compact, then X × Y with the product metric of F4.11 is compact. State it for the max metric, and add one sentence that d₁- and d₂-type product metrics give the same convergent sequences (F4.11's comparisons).

- Aftermath: [0, 1]² is compact without Heine–Borel in ℝ². The point here is the method, which AN2.7 will need for products where coordinates do not exist.

**Exercises.**

### 4.3 Section 8.3: Continuous Maps on Compact Spaces (`03-continuity-on-compact-spaces.md`)

**Budget:** ≤ 5,000 (~4,500).

**Opening frame (spiral, ~200).** Recall Chapter 7: on [a, b] a continuous function is bounded, attains its extrema (`thm-evt`), and is uniformly continuous (AN1.6's Heine–Cantor, proved there twice). What survives is everything. The proofs become shorter, because covers do the work that bisection did.

**`## Compactness travels forward` (~900).**
- **`thm-continuous-image-compact`** `[Continuous Images of Compact Sets Are Compact]`.
- **Try it first** (one of two): "Before reading on, try to prove this from `def-compact`. Which way does a cover move?" The Idea starts from the attempt, which tries to push a cover of K forward and finds nothing to push.
- **`[Scratch work]`, pull the cover back:** the goal is a cover of f(K), so take one, {V}. Its preimages are open (AN1.5) and cover K. Keep finitely many and push them forward. Givens/Goal table of two rows.
- **Proof**, about 6 lines.
- **`::: {.warning}`**: preimages of compact sets need not be compact (the constant map ℝ → {0}), and images of closed sets need not be closed (x ↦ x²/(1+x²) maps ℝ onto [0, 1)).
- **`thm-evt-metric`** `[Extreme Value Theorem]` (spiral of `thm-evt`). Let K be non-empty and compact and f : K → ℝ continuous. Then f is bounded and attains its sup and inf.
  - Proof of 4 lines: f(K) is compact in ℝ, hence closed and bounded, so sup f(K) ∈ f(K) (AN1.4/AN1.1: the sup of a non-empty bounded closed set belongs to it).
- **`exm-separated-sequence-unbounded`** `[Continuity without compactness]`. On E = {eₙ} ⊆ ℓ^∞:
  - g(eₙ) = n is continuous (E is discrete) and unbounded;
  - h(eₙ) = 1 − 1/n is continuous and bounded, but has no maximum.
  - Both are witnesses for the dropped hypothesis "compact", although E is closed and bounded.
- **Quick check:** "Does x ↦ x/(1+x) attain a maximum on [0, ∞)?" *Solution:* no. Its values are < 1 with sup 1 (it is 1 − 1/(1+x)). [0, ∞) is closed but not bounded.

**`## Homeomorphisms and inverses` (~800).**
- **`def-homeomorphism`** (see Seams): a continuous bijection with continuous inverse. Examples:
  - x ↦ x/(1 + |x|) from ℝ onto (−1, 1), with inverse y ↦ y/(1 − |y|) (the same formula as fd2's `exm-fraction-map`; cite it);
  - any bijection between discrete spaces.
- **`thm-compact-inverse-continuous`** `[A Continuous Bijection on a Compact Space Is a Homeomorphism]` (Apostol 4.29). *Idea:* continuity of f^{-1} means f maps closed sets to closed sets. Closed in K gives compact (`thm-closed-subset-compact`), then the image is compact (`thm-continuous-image-compact`), then closed (`thm-compact-closed-bounded`). Proof of about 6 lines, citing AN1.5's closed-set criterion.
- **`exm-bijection-discontinuous-inverse`** `[Compactness cannot be dropped]`. Let S = [0, 1) ∪ [2, 3] and f(x) = x on [0, 1), f(x) = x − 1 on [2, 3].
  - f is a continuous bijection onto [0, 2];
  - f^{-1}(1) = 2 but f^{-1}(1 − h) = 1 − h → 1, so f^{-1} is discontinuous at 1;

**`## Distance to a set` (~1,300).**

- **`thm-compact-closed-positive-distance`** `[A Compact Set and a Disjoint Closed Set Are a Positive Distance Apart]`. K compact and non-empty, F closed, K ∩ F = ∅ ⇒ inf{d(k, f)} > 0. Proof: x ↦ d(x, F) is continuous and positive on K (`prp-distance-to-set` (b)), so its minimum is positive (`thm-evt-metric`).

### 4.4 Section 8.4: Finite and Infinite Dimensions (`04-finite-and-infinite-dimensions.md`)

**`## All norms on ℝⁿ are equivalent` (~1,500).**
- **`def-equivalent-norms`**: there are a, b > 0 with a‖x‖ ≤ ‖x‖′ ≤ b‖x‖ for all x. A one-line consequence: equivalent norms have the same convergent sequences, the same Cauchy sequences and the same open sets.

### 4.5 Section 8.5: Connectedness (`05-connectedness.md`)

**Budget:** ≤ 5,000 (~4,900). This is the fullest section after 8.2. If the measured count exceeds 5,000, move `thm-components-partition`'s proof into a fold (it is not a cited statement).

**Opening frame (~250).** Chapter 7 proved the IVT on [a, b] by a sup. Which property of [a, b] did that proof use, and what is it in a metric space, where there is no order? Recall `thm-evt` and AN1.6's IVT label.

**`## What should "in one piece" mean?` (failure-motivated, ~650).**
- **Attempt 1:** "X is not a union of two disjoint non-empty sets." Every space with two points fails it, [0, 1] included. The pieces must be "apart".
- **Attempt 2:** "X is not a union of two non-empty sets at positive distance." Then ℚ = (ℚ ∩ (−∞, √2)) ∪ (ℚ ∩ (√2, ∞)) passes the test, since the two pieces are at distance 0. Yet the IVT fails over ℚ (AN1.1's x² − 2), so ℚ should not count as one piece.
- **The fix:** each piece is **open in X**. Then neither piece contains a limit of the other, which is what the √2 split has: each piece of ℚ is open in ℚ.
- **`def-connected`** `[Connected space; connected subset]` (all eleven steps): X is **disconnected** if X = U ∪ V with U, V **open in X**, **disjoint** and **non-empty** (a **separation**); otherwise **connected**. A ⊆ X is connected if it is connected as a metric space with the subspace metric.
  - Slogan: *it does not fall into two open pieces.*
  - Examples: a point; ∅ (connected by the definition; say why the convention is harmless); ℚ (not connected); {0, 1} (not connected).
  - Non-example by minimal change: [0, 1] ∪ [2, 3].
- **`::: {.warning}`**: "open" means open **in A**. For A = [0, 1) ∪ (1, 2], the pieces [0, 1) and (1, 2] are not open in ℝ but are open in A, so A is disconnected.
- **`thm-connected-two-valued`** `[Connectedness by Two-Valued Functions]`. X is connected iff every continuous f : X → {0, 1} is constant, iff the only subsets of X that are both open and closed are ∅ and X. Proof of about 8 lines, with preimages (AN1.5). Named move 7.

**`## The connected subsets of ℝ` (~800).**
- **`thm-connected-iff-interval`** `[Connected Subsets of ℝ Are the Intervals]`. Here an interval is any I ⊆ ℝ with a, b ∈ I, a < c < b ⇒ c ∈ I.
- **`[Scratch work]`, construct a point as a sup** (AN1.1's move, recalled by `@label`).
  - Given a separation U ∪ V of an interval I, with a ∈ U, b ∈ V and a < b without loss of generality (one clause on why: the roles of U and V are symmetric).
  - Let s = sup(U ∩ [a, b]). Rule out s ∈ U (U is open in I, and s < b, so U contains points beyond s) and s ∈ V (V is open in I and s > a, so V contains (s − η, s], which makes s − η/2 an upper bound).
  - Table of three rows.
- **Proof** with (⇐) as in the scratch work, about 12 lines, and (⇒) in 3 lines: if c ∉ A lies between two points of A, then A ∩ (−∞, c) and A ∩ (c, ∞) separate A.
- **Aftermath:** this is the one place where completeness of ℝ enters connectedness. ℚ, which lacks it, is not connected. This links to AN1.11 (prose: "Chapter 10 lists connectedness of ℝ among the forms of completeness"; §9 item 9).

**`## Continuous images and the intermediate value theorem` (~550).**
- **`thm-continuous-image-connected`** (Apostol 4.37). Proof by `thm-connected-two-valued`: compose.
- **`thm-ivt-connected`** `[Intermediate Value Theorem for Connected Spaces]`. X connected, f : X → ℝ continuous, f(p) < c < f(q) ⇒ some x has f(x) = c. Spiral remark recalling AN1.6's IVT.
- **Quick check:** "Show that a continuous f : [0, 1] → ℚ is constant." *Solution:* f([0, 1]) is a connected subset of ℝ contained in ℚ. An interval with two points contains irrationals (AN1.1), so f([0, 1]) is one point.

**`## Building connected sets` (~700).**
- **`thm-union-connected-common-point`** (Apostol 4.39). A union of connected sets with a common point is connected. Proof by a two-valued f: f is constant on each set, with the same value at the common point.
- **`thm-closure-connected`**. A ⊆ B ⊆ cl A and A connected ⇒ B connected. Proof of about 6 lines: a two-valued f on B is constant on A, and by continuity on cl A ∩ B = B.
- **`def-component`** and **`thm-components-partition`**. Each point lies in exactly one component, the union of all connected sets containing it. Components are connected and closed (by `thm-closure-connected`). Proof of about 6 lines, possibly folded (see Budget).
- **Quick check:** "Is the union of the two coordinate axes in ℝ² connected? Is the union of the hyperbola xy = 1 and the axes?" *Solution:* yes, by the common point 0. For the second set: no. Each branch of the hyperbola is closed in ℝ² and open in the union (a small ball around a branch point misses the axes and the other branch), so it is clopen, although the hyperbola is at distance 0 from the axes. The union has three components: the two branches and the axes. Distance 0 does not join pieces. This checks the attempt-2 lesson in reverse.

**`## Paths` (~950).**
- **`def-path-connected`**: any two points are joined by a continuous γ : [0, 1] → X. Note Apostol's name, "arcwise connected", in one clause.
- **`thm-path-connected-connected`**. Proof: fix p; X is the union of the path images through p, each connected (`thm-continuous-image-connected`, `thm-connected-iff-interval`), with a common point (`thm-union-connected-common-point`).
- **`exm-zigzag-closure`** `[Connected but not path-connected]`.
  - Let z : (0, 1] → [−1, 1] have z(1/n) = (−1)ⁿ, linear on each [1/(n+1), 1/n]. Let G be its graph and Z = G ∪ ({0} × [−1, 1]).
  - (i) Z = cl G. On each [1/(n+1), 1/n], z takes every value in [−1, 1]. Points with x > 0 are on the graph by continuity of z.
  - (ii) Z is connected, by `thm-closure-connected`, since G is the continuous image of an interval.
  - (iii) Z is not path-connected. Suppose γ = (γ₁, γ₂) runs from (0, 0) to (1, z(1)) = (1, −1).
    - Let t* = sup{t : γ₁(t) = 0}. The set is closed, so γ₁(t*) = 0, and t* < 1.
    - Continuity of γ₂ at t* gives η with |γ₂(t) − γ₂(t*)| < 1/2 on [t*, t* + η].
    - γ₁ is continuous on [t*, t* + η] from 0 to c = γ₁(t* + η) > 0, so it takes the values 1/n and 1/(n+1) for some n with 1/n < c (AN1.6's IVT).
    - There γ₂ = z(γ₁) takes the values ±1, two values 2 apart, both within 1/2 of γ₂(t*). Contradiction.
  - The failure motivates `def-path-connected` as a separate notion: connectedness does not supply paths.
  - One sentence: this is a piecewise-linear relative of the curve usually drawn with sin(1/x).

### 4.6 Section 8.6: Telling Spaces Apart (`06-telling-spaces-apart.md`)

**Budget:** ≤ 5,000 (~4,300), including the optional result (~800). The section ends with A, B, C and R.

**Opening (hook restated, ~300).**
- The index's questions return. [0, 1] and [0, 1]² have the same cardinality; give the fold sketch if F2.3 lacks it (§2).
- A homeomorphism preserves everything defined from open sets. To show two spaces are **not** homeomorphic, find such a property that one has and the other lacks.

**`## Invariants` (~650).**
- **`prp-homeomorphism-invariants`**. If h : X → Y is a homeomorphism and X is compact (connected, path-connected), then so is Y. Proof of 3 lines: images (`thm-continuous-image-compact`, `thm-continuous-image-connected`, compose paths).
- **Table** `Invariants` (weird-examples table, spaces):

| Property | Preserved by homeomorphisms? | Witness |
|---|---|---|
| compact | yes | — |
| connected, path-connected | yes | — |
| bounded | no | ℝ ≅ (−1, 1) via x/(1+\|x\|) |
| complete | no | the same map: (−1, 1) is incomplete |
| totally bounded | no | ℕ discrete ≅ ℕ_{≥1} with \|1/m − 1/n\| (the identity) |

- **Quick check:** "Is (0, 1) homeomorphic to [0, 1]?" *Solution:* no. [0, 1] is compact and (0, 1) is not.
- **Failure-motivated step:** compactness and connectedness alone do not separate [0, 1] from [0, 1]², since both are compact and connected. A finer invariant is needed.

**`## Cut points` (~400).**
- **`def-cut-point`**: for X connected, x is a **cut point** if X ∖ {x} is disconnected.
- **`lem-cut-points-invariant`**. A homeomorphism h : X → Y maps cut points to cut points, because h restricts to a homeomorphism X ∖ {x} → Y ∖ {h(x)}.
- **Quick check:** "Is (0, 1) homeomorphic to [0, 1)?" *Solution:* no. 0 is not a cut point of [0, 1), since [0, 1) ∖ {0} = (0, 1) is connected. Every point of (0, 1) is a cut point.

**`## The segment and the square` (~900).**
- **`thm-interval-square-not-homeomorphic`** `[The Segment and the Square Are Not Homeomorphic]`.
- **Try it first** (two of two): "Both spaces are compact and connected. Before reading on, find a point whose removal changes one of them."
- **`[Scratch work]`, remove a point:**

  - Back-reference to 8.5 B3 (same move).
- **Proof**, about 8 lines, citing `lem-cut-points-invariant` and `thm-path-connected-connected`.
- **`cor-line-plane-not-homeomorphic`**: ℝ ≇ ℝ² (8.5 B3).
- **`exm-square-boundary-not-interval`** `[A loop is not a segment]`. ∂[0, 1]² (the boundary of the square, used in place of a circle so that no trigonometry is needed) has no cut points: walk around the other way. So it is not homeomorphic to [0, 1], and not to [0, 1]² either (removing two points disconnects the loop but not the square). This is one more row for the invariant idea.
- **`thm-no-continuous-bijection-interval-square`** `[No Continuous Bijection from the Segment onto the Square]`. Proof of 3 lines: by `thm-compact-inverse-continuous` such a map is a homeomorphism, which contradicts the theorem above.
- **Aftermath:** the hook's second and third questions are answered. Which half of the chapter answered which, compactness or connectedness? One sentence.

**`## A curve that fills the square` (optional, ~1,200).**
- **`lem-cantor-digit-distance`** (not optional, since 8.6 C2 uses it). Every point of C has a unique expansion Σ a_k 3^{-k} with a_k ∈ {0, 2} (cite AN1.4's description of C; §9 item 3). If x, y ∈ C first differ at digit j, then |x − y| ≥ 3^{-j}. Proof of 3 lines: the difference is 2·3^{-j} minus at most Σ_{k>j} 2·3^{-k} = 3^{-j}.
- **`thm-square-filling-curve`** `.optional` `[A Continuous Map of the Segment onto the Square]`.
  - The digit map φ : C → [0, 1]² sends Σ a_k 3^{-k} to (Σ (a_{2k−1}/2) 2^{-k}, Σ (a_{2k}/2) 2^{-k}).
  - On each gap (a, b) of C (a maximal open interval of [0, 1] ∖ C, with a, b ∈ C), extend φ linearly. The result Φ : [0, 1] → [0, 1]² is continuous and surjective.
  - *Idea, Step roadmap:*
    - ① φ is onto, since every point of [0, 1] has a binary expansion (cite the binary-expansion source, §9 item 3);
    - ② φ is uniformly continuous: |x − y| < 3^{-2m} ⇒ agreement in the first 2m digits ⇒ |φ(x) − φ(y)|_∞ ≤ 2^{-m} (`lem-cantor-digit-distance`);
    - ③ Φ is continuous on each gap (linear) and at each point of C. At a point of C, the gaps near it are short, so their endpoints are near it, and convexity keeps Φ near too. On a side where the point bounds a gap, Φ is the linear formula.
    - ④ Φ([0, 1]) ⊇ φ(C) = [0, 1]², and Φ maps into the square by convexity.
  - **Proof** in four steps.
  - Optional-marker invariant: nothing cites it (STYLE §4). 8.6 C2's solution uses `lem-cantor-digit-distance` only.
- **Aftermath:** all three hook answers are now in. A continuous surjection exists; a continuous bijection and a homeomorphism do not. A prose pointer: "the optional gallery chapter of *Integration, Series, and Function Spaces* (AN2.15) builds a square-filling curve as a uniform limit instead".
